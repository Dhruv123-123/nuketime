# Gap hunt, round 2 (raw agent output, 2026-10-07)

A three-agent hafnium deep-dive plus six size-first hunters with two skeptics on each hunter's best candidate. Agent-written; re-check claims before use. Synthesis in `PICK4.md`.

## Hafnium: hf-supply-price

**Conclusion.** Hafnium is a market of roughly 70-150 t/yr worth well under $1B (the MMTA says so outright). Its price is set by export controls and scarcity, not by what it costs to make. USGS annual averages were $781/kg in 2021, $6,130 in 2023, $4,560 in 2024 and $3,800 in 2025. Western spot then rose to about $13,100/kg in April 2026 and above $14,000/kg in June 2026, while China's domestic price was only about CNY16,500/kg (~$2,300) in March 2025. Long-run prices are capped by two cost thresholds the MMTA gives: dehafniating non-nuclear zirconium pays at $2,500-3,000/kg, and producing hafnium as the main product (stockpiling or dumping the zirconium) needs a sustained $5,000-6,000/kg. ATI and Westinghouse alone could each roughly double to about 80 t/yr, and Critical Metals proposes a 120-150 t/yr refinery in Romania. My estimate, in an inelastic market of about 130 t: a new Western producer adding 20 t/yr would see prices settle around $7-9k/kg (~$140-180M/yr), 50 t/yr around $4.5-6k (~$225-300M/yr), and 100 t/yr around $2.5-4k (~$250-400M/yr, but only by taking about half the world market while incumbents expand too). A hafnium-only company therefore tops out at roughly $150-300M/yr in steady state, and it would be most exposed to a price crash around 2029-31. That confirms round 1's cap and nudges it up only slightly. Hafnium can be a high-margin by-product inside a bigger zirconium or chemicals business. It is not a $300M+/yr standalone startup.

SUPPLY (who makes Hf, how much)
- Global output of pure hafnium metal is "only 70-75 t/yr", from four countries: France, US, China and Russia (Argus, Apr 2025: https://argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2675178-fresh-concerns-over-the-us-80pc-tariff-on-hafnium). Critical Metals repeats 70-75 t, says traditional suppliers are unlikely to grow more than 4%/yr, and claims a ~20 t/yr shortfall (May 2026: https://www.criticalmetalscorp.com/?p=1422).
- The MMTA gives two higher figures for 2025: effective capacity of "at most 140-150t" for aerospace and electronic grades, and non-military demand of about 130 t (Aug 2025: https://mmta.co.uk/hafnium-demand-dynamics-and-capacity-constraints/). A later MMTA piece estimates 120-140 t/yr, derived from 6,000-7,000 t/yr of nuclear-grade zirconium (Aug 2026: https://mmta.co.uk/hafnium-where-will-it-end/). It also says Hf:Zr is about 1:50 and nuclear zirconium is about 6,000 t/yr (Dec 2024: https://mmta.co.uk/?p=26704). The gap between 70-75 t (metal) and 120-150 t (capacity) is probably metal vs oxide/capacity and how China is counted. Plan on roughly 100-130 t of real supply.
- Plants: Chepetsky (Russia), Framatome Jarrie (France), Westinghouse Ogden UT, ATI Albany OR, and several sites in China (MMTA 2025, link above). USGS also says US production comes from zirconium chemical intermediates in Oregon and Utah (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-zirconium-hafnium.pdf). The MMTA says ATI and Westinghouse could each reach about 80 t/yr (~100% expansion), and ATI, Framatome and Westinghouse together could exceed 200 t/yr. The constraint is that each extra 80 t of hafnium leaves about 2,000 t/yr of dehafniated zirconium to place (MMTA 2025).
- USGS MCS 2026 says world primary hafnium production data and reserves are "not available". US imports of unwrought hafnium went from 23 t (2021) to 72 t (2025e). Germany supplied 54% of 2025 imports and China 21%. Superalloys are the leading use, and recycling is minimal (USGS link above).

DOES CHINA'S ZIRCONIUM OXYCHLORIDE (ZOC) INDUSTRY SIDE-EXTRACT HAFNIUM? Mostly no, at least not yet.
- A Chinese industry report puts China's hafnium output at about 18 t in 2024 and 19 t projected for 2025. Producers named: CNNC zirconium-hafnium entities, CITIC Jinzhou, Beijing Jinboyu (1 t/month), Jiangsu Jinzixuan, Beijing Xingrongyuan and Chaoyang Dongzao (chyxx, Aug 2025: https://www.chyxx.com/industry/1233271.html). That is small next to China's ZOC volume, so most hafnium in Chinese ZOC is not recovered. Note that Critical Metals' and DataM's claim that China holds ~75% of supply (https://www.criticalmetalscorp.com/?p=1422, https://www.datamintelligence.com/research-report/hafnium-market) does not fit 18-19 t out of 70-150 t. Treat the 75% figure as unreliable or as a refining-share claim.
- This is changing. Orient Zirconic (002167) started building a 60,000 t/yr battery-grade ZOC plant on 23 June 2026. The wider plan includes "12,000 t zirconium-hafnium separation high-purity oxides", but the company says that part is not in Phase 1 (10jqka, Jul 2026: https://stock.10jqka.com.cn/20260701/c677864604.shtml; SMM: https://news.metal.com/en/newscontent/103970037-orient-zirconic-begins-construction-of-battery-grade-zirconium-oxychloride-project). My own arithmetic, not a sourced figure: 12 kt of separated oxide at about 2% hafnium implies on the order of 150-250 t/yr of HfO2 potential, which by itself exceeds today's world market. Asian Metal headlines also report Liaoning Huaxiang launching a zirconium-hafnium separation project (https://www.asianmetal.com/news/2217938/Liaoning-Huaxiang-to-launch-zirconium-hafnium-separation-project/1; the page did not load, headline only). Sanxiang Advanced Materials is partnering with Nanjing Youtian on an integrated Zr-Hf chain (https://www.asianmetal.com/news/2020776/; headline only). China is the most likely source of a supply wave, even if export controls keep it out of Western spot markets.

PRICES 2020-2026 (Western vs Chinese)
- USGS unwrought annual averages: 2021 $781/kg; 2022 $1,590; 2023 $6,130; 2024 $4,560; 2025e $3,800 (USGS link above).
- SMM says Western prices sat near $5,000/kg from May 2024 to October 2025, rose sharply from November 2025 after China's H2-2025 export controls, and reached $13,114.75/kg in April 2026 (+162%). Chipmakers and traders stockpiled, and some hedged (https://news.metal.com/en/newscontent/103938701-from-stability-to-surge-the-overseas-hafnium-market-caught-between-ai-demand-and-supply-controls). The MMTA cites Argus quotes above $14,000/kg in June 2026 (https://mmta.co.uk/hafnium-where-will-it-end/).
- Two-tier market: in spring 2025 Rotterdam was $3,700-3,900/kg duty unpaid and long-term contracts were near $6,000+, while China domestic ex-works was CNY16,300-17,000/kg (~$2,250-2,350). The total US tariff on Chinese hafnium rose to about 79% (Argus Apr 2025, link above). China also detained a hafnium shipment in May 2025 and later released it (MMTA 2025).
- Critical Metals quotes early-2026 prices of $13-15M/t for 99.99% hafnium and about $6M/t for 98-99% unwrought (https://www.criticalmetalscorp.com/?p=1422). That premium is for purity, not volume.

WHAT HAPPENED AFTER PAST SPIKES AND SUPPLY RESPONSES
- The 2023 peak of $6,130 fell 26% in 2024 and 38% by 2025 (USGS) as imports into the US rose from 23 t to 72 t and demand normalised. The MMTA notes that after the 2022 spike the price "has fallen somewhat closer to earth" but stayed historically high (https://mmta.co.uk/?p=26704). The pattern: in a market this thin, an extra ~20-40 t of imports or released stock was enough to take about 40% off the price within two years. The 2026 spike is a policy premium of the same kind and is equally fragile.

DEMAND CURVE TO 2032 (no primary source gives tonnage by segment; this is my synthesis)
- 2025 baseline: about 130 t non-military demand (MMTA). Superalloys were historically the leading use (USGS). Electronics is "quickly becoming the largest market", and other uses (nuclear control rods, space/hypersonics, catalysts, plasma cutting, optics) make up 15-20% (MMTA 2026).
- Gas turbines: GE Vernova has 116 GW of backlog plus slot reservations (53 GW firm, 63 GW slots) as of Q2 2026, targets at least 125 GW by year-end, and plans output of 20 GW/yr from Q3 2026, 24 GW in 2028 and 30 GW in 2030 (https://www.turbomachinerymag.com/view/ge-vernova-gas-turbine-backlog-hits-116-gw-as-power-orders-more-than-double). That is about +50% turbine output by 2030, so superalloy hafnium might grow about 40-50% if Siemens Energy and MHI scale similarly (not verified here).
- Semiconductors: HBM, advanced DRAM and high-k logic are the cited drivers (SMM). Ferroelectric HfO2 memory (FeRAM/FeFET) adds upside, but in tonnage terms the thin films use grams per wafer. Precursor yield losses make the real draw larger, but I found no reliable tonnage figure. Critical Metals' "+70% by 2030" claim, and its $420M→$726M AI/datacentre market at ~10% CAGR, come from Market.us and IMARC promotional reports (https://www.criticalmetalscorp.com/?p=1422).
- Nuclear: hafnium is mostly a supply source here (dehafniating nuclear zirconium); control-rod demand is small. Japan and Korea raised purchases (Argus Apr 2025).
- My projection: 2032 demand of about 190-250 t (+45-90% on 130 t), against potential supply of more than 200 t from Western incumbent expansions, plus 120-150 t from Critical Metals' Romania plan, plus Chinese ZOC dehafniation (Orient Zirconic, Huaxiang). Market-report sizes range from $172M (2026) to $387M (2025) (gelonghui: https://m.gelonghui.com/p/6698935; DataM link above). That range confirms a sub-$0.5B market at normal prices.
- Energy Fuels completed its acquisition of ASM on 28 Aug 2026. No published hafnium tonnage was found for the Dubbo project (1 Mtpa ore; produces rare earth, zirconium, niobium and hafnium oxides) (https://www.cruxinvestor.com/posts/energy-fuels-completes-australian-strategic-materials-acquisition-adding-rare-earth-metal-alloy-capacity; https://www.nsenergybusiness.com/projects/dubbo-project/).

ELASTICITY AND PRICE IF A NEW WESTERN PRODUCER ADDS 20 / 50 / 100 t/yr (my model, built on the sourced anchors above)
- Demand is very price-inelastic in the short run because hafnium is a tiny share of the cost of a chip or a turbine blade. In the medium run, superalloy designers can trim hafnium content. Supply is inelastic in the short run because it is a by-product, but it responds within 2-4 years once prices clear the $2,500-3,000 (dehafniation) or $5,000-6,000 (hafnium-as-main-product) thresholds (MMTA 2025).
- +20 t (~15% of supply): the scarcity premium mostly goes and the price settles around $7-9k/kg Western. Revenue ~$140-180M/yr. Fragile if China loosens controls.
- +50 t (~35-40%): the price falls to the incumbents' expansion cost, about $4.5-6k/kg. Revenue ~$225-300M/yr. ATI and Westinghouse would likely respond by expanding as well.
- +100 t (~70-80%): the price falls toward the dehafniation floor, about $2.5-4k/kg, close to the pre-control 2025 average of $3,800 and above China's domestic ~$2,300. Revenue ~$250-400M/yr, but only by taking roughly half the world market. With Critical Metals and Chinese capacity also arriving, the low end is more likely, around $250M.
- Net: steady-state hafnium revenue for one new entrant tops out around $150-300M/yr. Gross margin is attractive only if the process (for example, OSU's aqueous thiocyanate route, if its higher separation factor holds at scale; not verified in this pass) gets cost below $1,500-2,000/kg, and only if the dehafniated zirconium has a buyer.

Conclusion: a hafnium-only company is a $100-300M/yr niche at best and does not meet the >$300M/yr bar on its own.

## Hafnium: hf-tech-ip

**Conclusion.** The idea of bolting hafnium recovery onto non-nuclear zirconium chemical plants is not open ground. Two companies announced it in the last 60 days. On 25 Aug 2026 Daiichi Kigenso, the largest non-Chinese zirconium chemical maker, said it will recover Hf from its Vietnamese zirconium intermediate by solvent extraction, working with the Japanese startup Emulsion Flow Technologies. Samples ship in 2026 and mass production starts in 2028. Separately, China's Sanxiang Advanced Materials is finishing a 20,000 t zirconium-hafnium separation project on its zirconium oxychloride (ZOC) lines by end-2026. It is meant to add 250+ t/yr of HfCl4, about 139 t of Hf. CPM Group puts the whole world's effective capacity for aerospace- and electronic-grade Hf at only 140-150 t/yr. Oregon State's new chemistry is real but early: bench scale only, a best-case single-stage separation factor of 33 versus 6-7 for the standard solvent process, no named licensee, and the lead author says a startup or industry money is needed to scale it. A Western licensee of that process could be a niche business. With China alone set to roughly double world supply, a credible path to more than $300M/yr looks unlikely. The ~$13k/kg Western price depends on Chinese export controls, while Chinese domestic HfO2 sells for roughly 10M yuan/t, about $1.4k/kg.

1) THE OSU PAPER (checked)
- Paper: "Emergent Hf-Selective Precipitation of Aqueous Zr", JACS vol. 148, issue 37, article 39885. Authors are May Nyman (OSU chemistry), Alex Roseborough and Stephen Nyman. ANS article 1 Oct 2026: https://www.ans.org/news/2026-10-01/article-8451/study-finds-a-better-way-to-separate-zirconium-and-hafnium/
- How it works: water-based. Thiocyanate ligands plus choline (a nontoxic food additive) make a hafnium-rich solid precipitate, in one step. Best separation factor is 33, against 6-7 for the industrial standard. Funded by US DOE and the Murdock Charitable Trust. Nyman: "We still have questions to answer and milestones to achieve." https://www.sflorg.com/2026/09/chm09232601.html
- Scale: bench only. Nyman: "work would need to be done to translate the process from a bench experiment to a pilot demo... would likely require a start-up company and/or industry investment." The feed was zirconium from zircon with its natural Hf. Testing on real plant ZOC mother liquor or on recycled reagent is NOT reported (ANS link above).
- Patent: press coverage says OSU "patented" it (https://www.ico-optics.org/?p=95203). Google Patents would not load (blocked by robots.txt), so I could not see whether it is an application or a granted patent, its claims, or any licensee. No startup or licensee has been announced. Reagent cost is not reported.
- Background: the same group published the mechanism of the old thiocyanate/MIBK process in JACS 2022, vol. 144, p. 2816: https://pubs.acs.org/jacsat/article-abstract/144/6/2816/451594/
- Caveat: the "over 90% used in nuclear" line in the ANS piece is wrong for zircon as a whole. USGS says ceramics is the largest use of zircon.

6) VERDICT ON THE GAP
- Not open. The world's largest non-Chinese zirconium chemical maker, together with a separation startup, announced exactly this model on 25 Aug 2026. A Chinese ZOC producer is commissioning it at a scale that roughly doubles world supply.

- Daiichi Kigenso: Hf samples 2026, mass production 2028, solvent extraction with Emulsion Flow Technologies (TSE filing 2026-08-25)
- Hf market volume 100.96 t in 2026 rising to 132.08 t in 2031, 5.52% CAGR (Mordor Intelligence)
- Framatome Jarrie about 30 t/yr Hf, about 43% of world output (truesourcemetals, secondary)
- Value of 139 t Hf: about $1.8B at $13k/kg vs about $0.23B at the Chinese domestic price, showing the market depends on export policy (derived)

Risks: Hardest technical risk:
- Turning a single, best-case precipitation (SF 33, bench scale, likely on clean synthetic or purified zirconium solutions) into a multi-stage continuous process on real ZOC mother liquor. That liquor is strongly acidic and carries silica, Fe, Ti, U/Th and other impurities.
- The process must also recover and recycle choline thiocyanate cheaply. Thiocyanate in acid can give off toxic HCN/H2S-type gases, and its effluent is regulated, so safety and permitting are hard problems in their own right.
- Moving from a 2% Hf feed to roughly 99% Hf product with low Zr needs many stages, plus handling of solid precipitates and their washing losses.
- The product must then pass semiconductor ALD precursor qualification (trace metals at ppb level, 12-24 month customer qualification). That bar is harder than the separation itself.
- The zirconium host plant must accept the tie-in without its own ZOC/ZrO2 specs drifting.

Market risks:
- Sanxiang's roughly 139 t Hf alone matches world capacity. If China relaxes export controls, the Western premium (about $13k/kg vs about $1.4-1.65k/kg domestic China) collapses.
- Western demand is about 100-130 t/yr. Even at $13k/kg that caps the whole non-China market near $1.3-1.7B, and a newcomer's share at today's price would be tens of millions.
- Incumbents (ATI, Westinghouse) can roughly double output under existing permits. Daiichi plus Emulsion Flow already hold the Japanese and Southeast Asian zirconium chemical feed.

## Hafnium: hf-big-company

**Conclusion.** Of the four options, only a Western Zr/Hf toll-dehafniation refinery that sells both nuclear-grade Zr and hafnium, option (b) with (a) folded in, plausibly reaches $300M/yr. I put the odds at about 10-15% within 8-10 years, and it needs $300-600M+ of capex plus 5-10 years of nuclear qualification. The hafnium market is small. Argus (Oct 2025) put supply at 70-75 t/yr, and DataM valued the market at $387M in 2025. A pure hafnium producer would need about 40-60 t/yr, roughly a third to half of world supply, to make $300M at $5-8k/kg. Adding that much would push prices back toward the $4-5k/kg pre-shock level, and China's Liaoning Huaxiang plant (about 250 t/yr hafnium oxychloride, about 100 t Hf contained by my estimate) plus Framatome going from 30 to 45 t/yr already point that way. So hafnium-only is about 5%, and $80-200M/yr is the realistic ceiling. The precursor play is capped by an all-metals high-k/ALD precursor market of only about $548M (2024), held by Merck, Air Liquide, SK/DNF and Adeka, so under 5%. A multi-pair separations platform could get big only by becoming a rare-earth separator, which is crowded and not novel (about 10%, little novelty). On novelty: a hafnium shortage is not an unnamed gap. ATI, Framatome, Westinghouse, Critical Metals Corp (a planned 120-150 t/yr refinery), Energy Fuels/ASM, China and a Navy SBIR are all chasing it. What is still open is the narrow wedge of a low-capex aqueous dehafniation process (OSU's separation factor of 33) licensed and run as a merchant toll service outside the three vertically integrated Western incumbents.

4. SIZING EACH OPTION
- (a) Hafnium-only producer:
  - $300M needs 60 t at $5k/kg, 37 t at $8k, or 23 t at $13k.
  - 40 t of Hf needs about 2,000 t of Zr feed (Hf:Zr about 1:50). The dehafniated Zr earns no premium unless it is qualified as nuclear grade.
  - Adding 40-60 t to a 120-180 t market, alongside the Chinese and Framatome additions, would collapse the scarcity price.
  - Realistic steady state is 20-40 t × $4-7k = $80-280M. P(>$300M) is about 5%.
- (b) Western Zr/Hf refinery:
  - About 1,500 t/yr nuclear sponge × $50-100/kg = $75-150M, plus about 30 t Hf × $5-8k = $150-240M, totalling $225-390M.
  - That is the only option that plausibly clears $300M. Adding downstream alloy or tube products pushes it higher.
  - Blockers: the three buyers (Westinghouse, Framatome, GE via Global Nuclear Fuel) are vertically integrated competitors. Fuel qualification takes years. Kroll and chlorination capex likely runs $300M+ (my estimate).
  - P is about 10-15% in 8-10 years, and lower on YC timelines.
- (c) Separations platform:
  - The OSU result covers Zr/Hf only. Nothing shows it transfers to Nb/Ta or rare earths, and rare-earth separation is already crowded.
  - P(>$300M) is about 10%, but only by becoming a rare-earth company. Novelty is low.
- (d) Precursor vertical:
  - Hf-based precursors are perhaps $150-250M of the $548M total (my estimate). Fab qualification takes 2-3 years and the incumbents are entrenched.
  - P is under 5%. It works better as a downstream margin layer on top of (b).

## Hunter: byproduct-inversions

### Bolt-on co-product recovery for uranium in-situ recovery (ISR) wellfields: scandium, rhenium and rare earths now pumped back into the ground (novelty 5, size 5, doability 6)

Modular ion-exchange skids, run as a tolling or royalty service on ISR uranium circuits in Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and the US (Texas, Wyoming). They pull Sc, Re and REE out of the leach solution that already flows through the plant and is currently re-injected.

- Gap evidence: Uranium One's six ISR mines pump more than 120 million m3 of productive solution a year. It carries up to 1 mg/L Sc and Re and 5-20 mg/L REE, which comes to about 30 t Sc, 40 t Re and more than 2,000 t REE a year. That solution is 'pumped out annually with leaching solutions and returned back to aquifer' (IAEA URAM contribution: https://conferences.iaea.org/event/146/contributions/5238). For scale, world rhenium output was 62 t in 2024 at $1,370/kg (USGS MCS 2025: https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-rhenium.pdf), and world scandium consumption is about 60 t/yr. China controls more than 90% of refined Sc, Bloom Energy uses about 74% of it, demand is projected to reach about 300 t by 2030, and the DLA has paid $6,250/kg against a historical $640/kg (MWI West Point: https://mwi.westpoint.edu/the-sixty-ton-problem-scandium-supply-chain-risk-the-us-defense-industrial-base/). Chinese controls and Bloom's exposure are covered at https://www.barchart.com/story/news/3304085/a-short-seller-report-says-china-could-kill-this-key-data-center-suppliers-growth. The same pattern appears elsewhere. The US Bureau of Mines recovered Re at Palangana, Texas in 1982, a site that now belongs to UEC (https://onemine.org/documents/recovery-of-rhenium-from-uranium-in-situ-leach-liquor). ARMZ/Dalur ran a pilot recovering Sc oxide from ISR in 2013 (https://www.mining.com/?p=700747). Kazatomprom said at PDAC 2025 that it is only 'testing potential equipment' for Sc byproducts (https://www.mining-technology.com/news/pdac-2025-kazakh-uranium-miners-look-to-take-advantage-of-nuclear-resurgence).
- Gain: The extra capex is small because pumping, wells and solution handling are already paid for by uranium. The Uranium One streams alone could supply about 50% of world Sc and about 65% of world Re. For the operator, the byproducts are worth roughly 10-25% of uranium revenue on top, plus a non-Chinese source of material for Bloom fuel cells (Sc), AlScN RF and ferroelectric devices, and turbine superalloys (Re: 80% of use goes to superalloys, per USGS).
- Market: Gross value at the Uranium One mines: Re 40 t x $1.37k/kg = about $55M. Sc 30 t (about 46 t Sc2O3) at $640-6,250/kg = $30-290M. The NdPr share of 2,000 t REE (mostly La/Ce) = about $20-40M. That totals roughly $100-380M/yr. Including all Kazakh ISR (about 40% of world U) plus US ISR could give 2-3x that in gross value. A company capturing it through tolling or a 30-50% share would reach more than $300M/yr only with Kazakhstan on board and Sc prices staying well above the historical $640/kg. That is borderline, and I would not claim the size is certain.
- Prior art: Known in CIS hydrometallurgy literature and to Kazatomprom, which is testing in-house. ARMZ/Dalur ran a Sc pilot (2013) and USBM ran Palangana Re (1982). I found no Western venture-backed company selling this to ISR operators. The new uranium buildout in Texas and Wyoming (UEC, enCore, Ur-Energy) has no co-product programme I could find.
- Killer risk: There are four big risks. (1) The market caps itself: adding 30 t to a 60 t Sc market would crash the price, so Sc revenue falls as volume rises. (2) Kazatomprom or Rosatom (Uranium One is Rosatom-owned, which raises sanctions issues) may simply build it themselves. (3) US ISR flows are 10-50x smaller than Kazakh ones, so US-only revenue is probably tens of $M. (4) Sc concentrations are given as 'up to' and could be far lower on average, and keeping U and Ra out of the eluate brings licensing costs.

### Yttrium and heavy rare earths from feed that is discarded because it is radioactive (uranium-leach raffinates, and monazite or xenotime returned to mineral-sands pits), using a nuclear-licensed thorium/uranium residue service (novelty 3, size 7, doability 4)

A nuclear-licensed residue processor that accepts Th/U-bearing monazite and xenotime that mineral-sands miners (the zircon and Zr/Hf supply chain) currently return to the pit, and uranium-mill raffinates. It recovers Y, Dy, Tb and NdPr, sells the U back into the fuel cycle, and stores or sells the Th and Ra-228. Yttrium, now in shortage for turbine and chip-tool coatings, leads the pitch.

- Gap evidence: WNA states that monazite (5-12% Th) 'is thus normally returned to the mine and dispersed with the tailings'. A French plant that processed about 160,000 t of WA monazite closed because it could not dispose of the radioactive waste (https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/appendices/mineral-sands-appendix-to-norm-information-paper). Yttrium oxide in Europe rose 4,400% in 2025 to $270/kg against about $7/kg in China. The US imports all its yttrium, 93% of it directly from China, about 470 t in 2024, and it is used in turbine and jet-engine thermal barrier coatings and semiconductor tool coatings (Reuters via AJOT, Nov 2025: https://www.ajot.com/news/a-new-rare-earth-crisis-is-brewing-as-yttrium-shortages-spread). Reports in Feb 2026 say shortages are worsening despite the trade truce (https://www.miningreporters.com/noticia/news/2026/02/rare-earth-shortages-deepen-us-aerospace-chipmakers-despite-us-china-trade-truce). There is a nuclear precedent: Elliot Lake uranium leach operations once supplied 'perhaps one third of the world's yttrium' (1965-1990) (https://onemine.org/documents/canadian-rare-earth-and-thorium-recovery-operations-the-first-30-years).
- Gain: Monazite and xenotime that are already mined and concentrated as a byproduct of zircon and ilmenite cost close to nothing as feed, compared with a greenfield HREE mine. Y sells at more than $50-270/kg outside China against $7/kg inside it, and Dy and Tb are also under Chinese controls. Recovering U and Th/Ra-228 (Pb-212 feedstock) as sales lowers the cost of handling the residue.
- Market: Non-Chinese Y demand is a few kt/yr; at $50-270/kg that is about $150M-1B+ (US imports alone are about 470 t). Monazite from mineral sands (Toliara alone: 17-26 kt/yr monazite at 55-60% REO, per Argus: https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2571275-re-monazite-demand-shifts-mineral-sands-supply-chain) carries NdPr, Dy and Tb worth hundreds of $M/yr. A credible path to more than $300M/yr exists through processing, but it is a capital-heavy materials business, not a software-light YC business.
- Prior art: This is heavy and named. Energy Fuels has processed monazite at White Mesa since 2021 and bought Base Resources (Toliara). Iluka is building the Eneabba refinery from stockpiled monazite. Lynas, ReElement (200-400 t/yr Y planned) and USA Rare Earth are also active. The yttrium-specific shortage and the uranium-raffinate yttrium precedent are less crowded, but the licensed thorium-residue sink idea is what Energy Fuels already sells.
- Killer risk: Energy Fuels already owns the only US licensed mill that combines uranium and REE processing. Licensing a new 11e.(2)/source-material facility takes years and hundreds of $M. The Y premium could disappear quickly if China loosens controls (the Chinese price is $7/kg). Overall, this falls short of the 'nobody has named it' bar.

**Skeptics on 'Bolt-on co-product recovery for uranium in-situ recovery (ISR) wellfields: scandium, rhenium and rare earths now pumped back into the ground':**

- killed: Novelty: low. The modular-skid concept has already been piloted by Kazatomprom (Re, 2024) and by a Kazakh academic consortium (multi-metal 40-ft container, 2022, $350M claim). It is also being worked on in Uzbekistan and Russia. Realistic revenue: world Sc plus Re markets total under $150M/yr and would deflate under new supply. A US-only ISR co-product or sorbent business might be worth $5-30M/yr, possibly with DoD premiums. A credible path to more than $300M/yr: about 0%. The only narrow residual angle is selling a selective, radionuclide-rejecting Sc sorbent to US ISR operators for DoD offtake, which is a small business, not a YC-scale gap. Counter-evidence: The strongest case for it is this. Selective sorbents that reject Th, U and Ra are what Uranium One itself names as the remaining bottleneck (https://conferences.iaea.org/event/146/contributions/5238), and none of the in-house programs has visibly reached commercial Sc output in more than 10 years (the Dalur pilot was 2017, and Kazatomprom was still "testing equipment" in 2025). In the US (UEC Palangana and Christensen, enCore, Ur-Energy Shirley Basin, which started in 2026: https://www.ans.org/news/2026-04-30/article-7994/urenergy-begins-isr-mining-operations-at-wyomings-shirley-basin/), no co-product program is visible. Domestic Sc/Re could also attract a DoD/DLA strategic premium, since the Pentagon is funding mine-to-alloy Sc chains (USGS MCS 2026). A US-only sorbent or skid business is plausible, but it would be a niche worth about $5-30M/yr.

- killed: Realistic startup revenue ceiling is about $20-120M/yr, from tolling or royalty on Kazakh plus US ISR co-products. The total addressable gross value is about $55-240M/yr, capped by world Re (about $85M) and Sc (about $70-370M) market sizes, not by solution tonnage. Odds of clearing $300M/yr: under 5%. Novelty is low: Uzbekistan (NMMC) recovers Re at commercial scale, Rosatom/Dalur piloted Sc, and Kazatomprom is developing it in-house. The best salvage is a niche US play recovering Re from new Texas/Wyoming ISR plants, probably worth single-digit $M/yr. Counter-evidence: China's April 2025 export controls on Sc are still in effect (USGS MCS 2026). The ex-China Sc price is quoted at about $3,333/kg against $640/kg historically. Projected demand is about 110 t in 2026 against about 41 t of output, and is projected to reach about 300 t by 2030, driven by Bloom and data centres. The Pentagon is lending hundreds of millions to close the gap. If Sc sustained $2,000-3,000/kg at 200-300 t of demand and a non-Chinese ISR supplier won a large share, Kazakh ISR Sc alone could be worth $100-200M gross. Re prices are also rising (+28% in 2024). A capital-light ion-exchange retrofit would sit low on the cost curve, because pumping and leaching are already paid for by uranium. Even on these terms the startup's revenue share stays under about $150M, and the economics depend on the most lock-up-prone counterparties in the industry.

Killed:
- Pb-212/Th-228 from REE or thorium waste: crowded. Orano Med is building a EUR250M Th-228 extraction plant meant to 'supply all' Pb-212 labs from 2027 (https://radiologybusiness.com/node/271621). entX/IsoMedica in Australia already extracts Th-228 and Yb-176 from mineral waste (https://www.innovationplaces.sa.gov.au/news/turning-waste-into-life-saving-cancer-therapies/). Chinese groups extract Pb-212 from REE slag (https://news.metal.com/newscontent/104078372-chinese-researchers-extract-high-purity-lead-212-for-cancer-treatment-from-rare-earth-ore-slag). DOE sells Th-228 (https://www.isotopes.gov/node/1530).
- Scandium as a standalone play (Bloom/AlScN chokepoint): real, but now heavily discussed (Hunterbrook short report, MWI West Point https://mwi.westpoint.edu/the-sixty-ton-problem-scandium-supply-chain-risk-the-us-defense-industrial-base/), with Rio Tinto, Scandium Canada, NioCorp and others already chasing it. The only angle left is the ISR one, folded into survivor 1.
- Rhenium standalone: world output 62 t x $1,370/kg = about $85M/yr total market (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-rhenium.pdf), far below $300M. Kept only as a co-product in survivor 1.
- Mo-99/Mo-100: market about $0.5-0.6B and crowded (NorthStar, SHINE, Niowave, BWXT, Eckert & Ziegler).
- Co-60 from CANDU and LWRs: the whole Co-60 market is a few hundred $M, Nordion/Sotera dominates, and BWR/PWR Co-60 programmes already exist (GE Hitachi, Framatome). Capped and known.
- Ir-192: small radiography market, capped well under $300M.
- Ra-226 from uranium tailings as Ac-225 feedstock: real scarcity, but Ra targets are recycled, so feedstock demand is grams and revenue is capped. The Ac-225 producers downstream are crowded (NorthStar, PanTera, TerraPower Isotopes, BWXT, Eckert & Ziegler).
- Yb-176 / Si-28 / O-18 / Mo-100 / Ni-64 enriched stable isotopes: ASP Isotopes and Urenco Stable Isotopes already cover these, and each market is capped at about $50-200M.
- Vanadium from uranium ores: Energy Fuels already recovers it at White Mesa, V prices are depressed, and there is no gap.
- Lithium from uranium processing: no evidence of meaningful Li in uranium circuits. Dead.
- Germanium/gallium/tellurium: genuine AI-chip and solar chokepoints, but they come from zinc, bauxite and copper refining, not the nuclear industry. Off-lens, and Ga/Ge recovery startups exist.
- Niobium/tantalum/beryllium: Kazatomprom's Ulba plant already integrates Ta/Nb/Be (https://mugglehead.com/leading-uranium-producer-kazatomprom-develops-rare-metals-deposit-in-kazakhstan/). Materion and CBMM dominate. No discarded nuclear byproduct.
- B-10 enrichment and its B-11 byproduct (11BF3 implant gas): Entegris already sells 11BF3, and the market is about $100M or less. Capped.
- NTD silicon irradiation: research-reactor service market about $50-100M, and SiC is eroding Si IGBTs. Capped.
- Tritium/Li-6 for fusion: demand arrives only after 2035, and it overlaps the excluded fusion-inventory area. Capped today.
- Deuterium (OLED and chip anneal): overlaps the excluded heavy-water area.
- Sr-90 to Y-90 and Am-241 from legacy stocks: generator chemistry means kCi needs against MCi held. Zeno Power and NNL already work this. Capped.
- Low-alpha lead from thorium-ore (Pb-208-rich) sources for chip packaging: an interesting physics angle, but lead tonnage in packaging is tiny, Honeywell and Mitsubishi supply it, and the market is capped well under $300M.
- Irradiated graphite decontamination / C-14 recovery: government cleanup contracts, Studsvik and IAEA programmes already exist, and C-14 diamond batteries (Arkenlight) are known. Not novel.
- Xenon/krypton from reprocessing off-gas: commercial reprocessing happens only at La Hague, and the Xe market is about $300M at most with Kr-85 contamination problems. Capped and hard to do.

## Hunter: orbital-radiation

### Neutron-aware AI datacentres: mitigating and attributing thermal-neutron soft errors in liquid-cooled GPU fleets (novelty 7, size 6, doability 6)

Cosmic-ray neutrons slowed down by water in liquid-cooled racks raise soft-error rates in boron-containing COTS GPUs and HBM. The product sells hyperscalers neutron-absorbing coolant or cold-plate and rack liners, fleet neutron sensing, and analytics that link the neutron field to error logs, priced per GPU per year.

- Gap evidence: NTT and Hokkaido University found low-energy neutrons cause about 1/5 to 1/3 of soft errors. They state that 'if semiconductors are cooled by water, the thermal neutron count is expected to increase significantly' (https://theregister.com/2023/03/17/neutrons_cause_soft_errors_electronics). A CERN workshop paper reports thermal-neutron failure rates in NVIDIA GPUs, AMD APUs, DDR and FPGAs 'comparable or even higher than the high energy neutron FIT rate', and names HPC liquid cooling and concrete as modifiers (https://indico.cern.ch/event/1198078/contributions/5037300). The industry is moving to liquid cooling at the same time as synchronous training multiplies the cost of each error. In Meta's Llama 3 run, 16,384 H100s saw 419 interruptions in 54 days; 30.1% were faulty GPUs and 17.2% were HBM3, and one GPU failure can restart the whole job (https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/meta-report-details-hundreds-of-gpu-and-hbm3-related-interruptions-to-llama-3-training-run). A targeted search found no commercial neutron-mitigation product aimed at datacentres. The closest result was a chip-process patent (https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8946874).
- Gain: Fewer interruptions and silent corruptions in large training jobs. On a 100k-GPU cluster, every 1% of goodput recovered is worth roughly $30-50M a year in GPU-hours at $2-3 per GPU-hour (my estimate). It also gives operators evidence for warranty and RMA disputes when a fault was caused by radiation rather than a defect.
- Market: These figures are my estimates, not sourced. There are 10M+ datacentre AI accelerators installed and most new builds are liquid-cooled. At $30-60 per GPU per year for sensing, analytics and passive mitigation, the ceiling is about $300-600M a year. Selling borated or Gd-loaded liners and coolant additives into the multi-billion-dollar liquid-cooling bill of materials could add more. Reaching $300M+ needs about 30%+ hyperscaler penetration or bundling with a cooling OEM.
- Prior art: The academic work is solid: Paolo Rech (UniTN, LANL) on thermal neutrons in HPC and NTT/Hokkaido on the neutron energy spectrum. The 2018 IC patent covers in-process boron reduction. Fleet-level SDC detection is done in-house at Meta and Google. I found no company selling neutron mitigation or attribution for datacentres.
- Killer risk: Radiation may be a small share of GPU and HBM faults compared with manufacturing defects, heat and power. If the measured uplift from liquid cooling is under about 2x, or the absolute FIT is small, the value disappears. NVIDIA and the HBM makers could also design the problem out with B-10-free processes and stronger ECC. Hyperscalers may only buy after a public quantification. The first step is a cheap pilot with a neutron detector beside a liquid-cooled rack, correlated with XID and ECC logs.

### Radiation qualification and rad-aware runtime for orbital AI compute (per-kW licence) (novelty 3, size 4, doability 7)

A full-stack space-qualification layer for COTS GPUs, TPUs and HBM: beam-test campaigns and a shared characterisation database, plus firmware and runtime that checkpoint, scrub, detect SDCs and reroute on SEFIs, licensed per kW in orbit to Starcloud, Google Suncatcher, SpaceX/xAI and defence constellations.

- Gap evidence: Starcloud-1 flew an H100 (https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/starcloud-1-satellite-reaches-space-with-nvidia-h100-gpu-now-operating-in-orbit/). Google's Trillium TPUs survived proton testing, but HBM showed TID sensitivity and uncorrectable errors 'likely acceptable for inference'; training remains unresolved, and two prototype satellites are planned by early 2027 (https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/project-suncatcher-google-to-launch-tpus-into-orbit-with-planet-labs-envisions-1km-arrays-of-81-satellite-compute-clusters/). Academics argue for application-aware radiation tolerance in orbital compute (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.11853), and ESA funds software resilience work (https://connectivity.esa.int/projects/astraeus).
- Gain: Each orbital DC operator avoids repeating beam campaigns, which cost months and $1-6k per beam-hour (https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24993/chapter/10). It also gets more usable FLOPs per kg by replacing shielding mass and TMR with software mitigation. At $200/kg launch, every kg of shielding saved has direct value.
- Market: Speculative. Revenue above $300M a year needs about 3-5+ GW of orbital compute at roughly $60-100 per kW per year, or a share of hardware. That is plausible only in the 2030s and depends on Starship cadence; Google itself assumes 180 launches a year (same DCD source). Near-term revenue is about $5-30M a year from early operators and defence.
- Prior art: Zero-Error Systems ($10M raised, rad-hardening for COTS, https://techcrunch.com/?p=2557058), Aethero, Ubotica, Unibap, Xiphos, Aitech, NASA SpaceCube, and in-house teams at Google and SpaceX. Prior art is crowded.
- Killer risk: The largest customers (Google, SpaceX/xAI) build this in-house, and the market timing depends on launch costs. Survives only as a long-dated option.

### Radiation-resilient HBM controller and ECC IP licensed to accelerator makers (orbital and terrestrial) (novelty 3, size 5, doability 3)

HBM is the weak link in both orbital (TID, uncorrectable errors) and terrestrial (17.2% of Llama 3 interruptions) AI compute. The product is memory-controller, scrubbing and ECC IP that tolerates multi-bit and SEFI-style HBM upsets, licensed to accelerator or custom-ASIC teams for a per-unit royalty.

- Gap evidence: Google singled out HBM as the radiation-sensitive subsystem in Suncatcher (https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/project-suncatcher-google-to-launch-tpus-into-orbit-with-planet-labs-envisions-1km-arrays-of-81-satellite-compute-clusters/). HBM3 caused 72 of the 419 Llama 3 interruptions (https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/meta-report-details-hundreds-of-gpu-and-hbm3-related-interruptions-to-llama-3-training-run).
- Gain: Higher goodput and fewer RMAs on the ground, and training made viable in orbit.
- Market: My estimate: a $5-20 royalty on 10-20M accelerators a year gives $50-400M. Reaching $300M+ requires NVIDIA, AMD or the hyperscaler ASIC teams to adopt it.
- Prior art: In-house RAS teams at NVIDIA, AMD, Google and the HBM vendors (SK hynix, Samsung, Micron); Synopsys and Cadence HBM controller IP; established academic ECC work. Very crowded at the IP level.
- Killer risk: Incumbents own the controller and the HBM interface, so selling into tape-outs is very hard for a startup. The most likely outcome is an acqui-hire, not a $300M business.

**Skeptics on 'Neutron-aware AI datacentres: mitigating and attributing thermal-neutron soft errors in liquid-cooled GPU fleets':**

- weakened: Novelty: moderate. No product exists, but the science (Rech/LANL/ISIS 2021), expired absorber patents and NTT testing services are all public, so it is a known-and-deprioritised problem rather than an unnamed one. Realistic revenue: about $5-30M/yr as a niche reliability-analytics or testing service for HPC labs and a few hyperscaler studies, possibly an acquihire by a cooling OEM (Vertiv, CoolIT) or by NTT. A path to >$300M/yr needs proof that liquid cooling raises the uncorrectable error rate by a material factor (for example >20% of job interruptions), and current evidence points the other way. Probability of clearing the YC $300M bar: under 5%. Counter-evidence: Meta Engineering (July 2025) says cosmic-ray soft errors are about 1 fault per million devices, while silicon-defect and wear-out SDCs are about 1 per thousand, 'much higher than cosmic-ray-induced soft errors' (https://engineering.fb.com/2025/07/22/data-infrastructure/how-meta-keeps-its-ai-hardware-reliable/). The largest fleet operator treats radiation as the solved, minor term. On top of that, ECC on HBM and SRAM absorbs most single-bit upsets, and the water-cooling flux increase has never been publicly quantified. Thermal-neutron attribution would address a small slice of a reliability budget that defects dominate.

- killed: Realistic ceiling is about $20–50M/yr, and only after the field effect is proven. Analytics and sensing sold as a feature: about $1–7M/yr ($1–5 per GPU per year on 3–5M liquid-cooled accelerators at 30% penetration). Borated or Gd liners and coolant additives through cooling OEMs: about $15–45M/yr gross at thin margins, with no moat. Revenue today is zero; nobody buys datacentre neutron mitigation. The idea is novel but low-value, and the chip vendors can make it obsolete. The case relies on unsourced figures (10M+ accelerators, $30–60 per GPU per year) and on an effect that no field study has measured in liquid-cooled fleets. Counter-evidence: The strongest support for the idea:
- Several lab results converge. NTT/Hokkaido measure low-energy neutrons at about 1/5 to 1/4 of soft errors (https://theregister.com/2023/03/17/neutrons_cause_soft_errors_electronics). The CERN/UniTN work measures GPU thermal FIT rates that can match or exceed high-energy rates, and the exascale paper cites thermal neutrons as up to 59% of MTBF for some devices and codes (https://indico.cern.ch/event/1198078/contributions/5037300, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07770).
- H100 HBM3 is about 3.2x worse per GPU than A100 on uncorrectable ECC errors, and memory errors dominate H100 job failures (https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11901v3). As HBM capacity and cluster size grow, a modest environmental multiplier could become material in synchronous training. My generous estimate puts the thermal-attributable cost at about $50–100 per GPU per year.
- Neither the DRAM null result (https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16487) nor the Delta study was done on liquid-cooled GPU fleets. The specific claim that water cooling raises fleet error rates has not been tested in the field either way.
- The surviving wedge is a measurement study run with one hyperscaler. If it proved a 20%+ effect, it could fund a consulting or standards business, or justify a feature inside an existing chip-telemetry vendor. That is a research paper or an acqui-hire, not a $300M/yr company.

Killed:
- Heavy-ion beam time as a service or a new private SEE facility: the shortage is real (DoD analysis of alternatives found a ~5,000 hr/yr gap by 2025, https://nepp.nasa.gov/workshops/dhesee2021/talks/6a_SEE%20AoA%20summary%20Nov%202020%20approved%20for%20release.pdf; Aerospace Corp says 'demand ... significantly exceeding the supply', https://aerospace.org/fact-sheet/alternative-sources-single-event-effects-testing-fact-sheet). But prices are $800-6,000/hr on about 10-15k US hours (https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24993/chapter/10), so the whole US market is about $15-50M a year. Federal supply is also growing: the FRIB K500 opened Feb 2026 and tripled capacity to 6,000 hrs (https://frib.msu.edu/news-center/news/frib-inaugurates-k500-chip-testing-facility-expanding-us-microelectronics-testing), plus a DoW ORBITS high-energy beamline (https://ncms.org/news/project-announcement/project-announcement-orbits/). Capped well under $300M.
- Beam-time marketplace or scheduling broker: a small slice of a market under $50M; the DoD already recommends central demand management (same AoA source).
- Compact superconducting cyclotrons sold to chip and space firms: perhaps tens of units worldwide at $10-30M each; lumpy revenue capped around $100M a year, and national labs and FRIB are expanding.
- Pulsed-laser or two-photon SEE screening as a heavy-ion substitute: a niche test-equipment market; Aerospace Corp is already developing alternative SEE methods (https://aerospace.org/fact-sheet/alternative-sources-single-event-effects-testing-fact-sheet).
- Using proton-therapy centres for SEE testing: already done by MGH ($750/hr) and Northwestern ($1,000/hr) (https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24993/chapter/10); tiny market.
- Radiation shielding materials for satellites and orbital DCs: Cosmic Shielding Corp already serves this ($5.3M raised, 3D-printed composites, https://www.caplight.com/company/cosmicshielding), and orbital DC designs favour software mitigation over mass.
- Rad-hard or rad-tolerant ICs for COTS protection: Zero-Error Systems (https://techcrunch.com/?p=2557058) plus incumbents Frontgrade, Microchip, BAE and TI SEP parts; the total rad-hard market is about $4.5B by 2030 and crowded.
- Rad-tolerant COTS compute modules (space-grade GPU boards): crowded (Aitech, Unibap, Xiphos, Ubotica, Aethero) and hyperscalers build their own.
- Radiation-risk underwriting for orbital DCs: the total space insurance premium pool is about $0.5B, so this cannot reach $300M a year; it is also adjacent to the excluded MGA ground.
- Space-weather-aware workload scheduling for constellations: a software feature, not a company; under $50M a year.
- Co-60 TID testing capacity: the gamma irradiator market is owned by Sterigenics and Nordion, and TID testing is a small niche.
- Korea or other sovereign SEE capacity as a business: national labs are filling this (IBS RAON demonstration, https://view.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2026012910125910431).

## Hunter: waste-disposal-capacity

### Radium-stripped phosphogypsum: turning gypsum stacks into a gypsum and REE feedstock business (novelty 3, size 7, doability 4)

Run a toll-processing plant at the phosphate producer's site that leaches Ra-226 out of phosphogypsum (PG) until it falls below the EPA 10 pCi/g line. The producer pays a per-ton fee to shrink its stack-closure liability, and the clean calcium sulfate is sold to wallboard, cement and agricultural buyers, with REE recovery as a side stream.

- Gap evidence: Florida holds about 1 billion tons of PG in 24 stacks and adds about 30 Mt a year (https://www.wusf.org/environment/2023-04-17/radioactive-roads-environmentalists-florida-plan-reuse-toxic-mining-waste). Federal rules (40 CFR 61 Subpart R) allow PG off the stack for farm use only below 10 pCi/g Ra-226, and every other use needs case-by-case EPA approval under 61.206 (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-61/subpart-R). Closure liability is large: Mosaic's 2015 RCRA settlement required a $630M trust plus a $50M letter of credit and about $1.8B in guarantees for closure and long-term care across 8 facilities (https://www.epa.gov/enforcement/mosaic-fertilizer-llc-settlement). There is explicit customer pull. ICL publicly sought a process to cut Ra-226 from 700-1,200 Bq/kg to under 400 Bq/kg at $10-20/t and 0.3-3 Mt/yr (https://www.innocentive.com/challenges/innovative-approaches-to-reducing-radium-226-contamination-in-phosphogypsum-waste-stream/). Regulators are cautiously opening up. The 11th Circuit upheld EPA's Dec 2024 approval of Mosaic's PG test road on 12 Aug 2026, but stressed that each future use needs its own approval (https://floridaphoenix.com/2026/08/12/u-s-appeals-court-approves-test-of-radioactive-waste-as-road-paving-material/).
- Gain: Producers move liability off their balance sheet: each ton processed is a ton that no longer needs a lined stack, water treatment and 50 years of care. A plausible split is a $10-20/t fee (ICL's own target) plus $5-15/t in product revenue. One 3 Mt/yr plant would gross about $45-100M a year.
- Market: Florida generation alone at 30 Mt/yr x $15/t is about $450M a year before product sales, and the 1 Bt legacy stockpile adds decades of feed. Global PG output is several hundred Mt a year (Morocco, China, India); this is an estimate I did not verify with a primary source this session. Getting past $300M a year requires roughly 15-20 Mt/yr processed, which is 5-7 large plants. That is credible but capital-heavy.
- Prior art: Crowded on the technology side. Carbon Cycle (UK) has a patented gypsum purification process aimed at PG stacks (https://www.chemistryworld.com/news/carbon-cycles-gypsum-purification-process-cleans-up-radioactive-fertiliser-waste/4010670.article). thyssenkrupp Uhde offers PG-to-clinker plus sulfuric acid (https://www.bcinsight.crugroup.com/2026/07/08/phosphogypsum-for-clinker-production-delivering-a-circular-economy-solution/). US patents 11479472 and 11912582 cover REE/U/Th recovery from PG (https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11912582). Older art includes US7118718 and US4388292. Mosaic/UF research exists, and ICL ran its own challenge. Bottom line: the problem has been named many times. The opening is in execution and business model (toll processing plus liability transfer plus EPA 61.206 approvals), not a new idea.
- Killer risk: Regulatory: 61.206 may treat radium-stripped PG as still being PG, so every end use needs EPA approval, and environmental NGOs litigate each one. Economics: leaching chemistry at $10-20/t with acid and water recycling has defeated decades of attempts, and the stripped radium ends up as a concentrated TENORM residue that needs its own disposal route. Novelty fails the 'nobody has named it' bar.

### Permian NORM residuals capacity: the radium bottleneck behind produced-water discharge (novelty 5, size 3, doability 6)

Build licensed radium-capture and NORM-residue disposal capacity in the Delaware Basin, sized for the radium-bearing sludge and spent sorbent that produced-water desalination plants will start generating once Texas issues its first discharge permits.

- Gap evidence: The Permian produces about 1 billion gallons of produced water a day. TCEQ has taken discharge permit applications since 2024 but issued none as of Aug 2026, and a land-application rule was proposed in 2026 (https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southcentral/2026/08/25/882802.htm). NGL is targeting 800,000 bbl/d of treatment capacity, starting with about a 50,000 bbl/d plant, and expected a TPDES permit in early 2026 (https://www.powermag.com/natura-ngl-move-to-pair-nuclear-molten-salt-reactors-with-large-scale-produced-water-treatment-in-permian/). Permian brines carry Ra-226 at 19-36 Bq/L (about 500-970 pCi/L) and Ra-228 at 10-21 Bq/L (https://texaspluswater.wp.txstate.edu/2022/04/26/thinkwater-naturally-occurring-radioactive-materials-in-permian-produced-water-covid-and-water-use-patterns-and-funding-for-colonias). A Texas Tech pilot measured Ra-226 at 68 pCi/L and Ra-228 at 328 pCi/L and removed 99.999% of it, which means all of the radium ends up in the residuals (https://www.depts.ttu.edu/tawc/events/water-college/wc2025/leeds.pdf). Texas exempts NORM only below 30 pCi/g Ra-226+228. The RRC states there are currently no RRC-permitted commercial landfills or pits for NORM, and only about 10 commercial NORM injection wells (https://rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/applications-and-permits/environmental-permit-types/norm-waste/). My own arithmetic, not a sourced figure: at about 500 pCi/L across about 3.8B L/day, the basin moves on the order of hundreds of curies (hundreds of grams) of Ra-226 a year. Today that is reinjected in dilute form. Once water is treated for discharge, it has to be captured as concentrated solids.
- Gain: Desalination operators need a guaranteed, licensed home for high-activity residues before they can finance plants. Historical NORM disposal prices were $100-150/bbl by injection and $500+/drum by landfill (https://www.ogj.com/home/article/17230971/norm-disposal-options-costs-vary). Capacity controlled close to the plants could earn scarcity pricing, and a radium-selective capture step would shrink the residue volume and the cost per barrel treated.
- Market: This is likely capped under $300M a year as a standalone business. If 2-3M bbl/d are eventually treated for discharge, residuals handling at $0.05-0.15/bbl is about $40-160M a year. It clears $300M only by bundling in all Permian oilfield NORM (scale, tank bottoms, pipe decon) or by becoming the whole treatment operator, a space already crowded with NGL, Aris, Texas Pacific Water Resources and TETRA. Possible upside: the captured Ra-226 as feedstock for Ac-225, though that market is small and served by recycling (see killed list).
- Prior art: Incumbent NORM handlers already exist: Lotus LLC (West Texas injection) and Newpark/R360-type oilfield waste firms (https://www.ogj.com/home/article/17230971/norm-disposal-options-costs-vary). Big water-treatment vendors (Veolia, Aquatech) design pretreatment trains. I found no one publicly positioning radium residuals as the gate on produced-water discharge, so the framing is somewhat fresh, but the underlying service is not.
- Killer risk: Timing and size. Discharge permits keep slipping, and the freeze-desal pilots send half the brine back underground (https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southcentral/2026/08/25/882802.htm), so concentrated residues may stay small for years. Incumbents can permit an extra NORM injection well faster than a startup can. Most likely outcome is a good $50-150M services business, not a company with a path past $300M a year.

**Skeptics on 'Permian NORM residuals capacity: the radium bottleneck behind produced-water discharge':**

- killed: The addressable market is well below the candidate's $40-160M/yr. Most radium leaves in liquid concentrate through existing Class II disposal wells, so the new solids stream is only pretreatment sludge and spent media. That is plausibly $10-50M/yr basin-wide even at 2-3M bbl/d treated, and much of it would go to WCS and the existing NORM-injection operators. Reaching more than $300M/yr would mean becoming a full produced-water treatment operator or a basin-wide NORM consolidator, competing with NGL, Aris, TPL, TETRA and WCS. Novelty: the framing is moderately fresh; the service, technology and licensed capacity are not. Not a YC-grade gap. Counter-evidence: The best case for the idea is that the people building treatment plants do not talk about residuals at all. TxPWC, TPL, TETRA and Western Midstream JIP 2 all report >99% radioactivity removal and say nothing about where the radium goes. RRC solid-NORM capacity is thin (no commercial landfills or pits, 7 injection facilities), so if TCEQ discharge permits or the proposed land-application rule push operators toward ZLD or solid residuals, or if radium-rich concentrate is barred from ordinary disposal wells, demand for licensed solids handling in the Delaware Basin could jump. Even in that scenario, WCS in Andrews County is the obvious winner.

- killed: Realistic radium-residuals revenue for the whole Permian by 2030 is about $5-40M a year. Under a bull case (about 10M bbl/d desalinated plus a ZLD mandate) it is perhaps $100-180M, which is the full addressable pool, not one company's share. A single entrant's plausible revenue is under $20M a year. The idea reaches $300M only by becoming a general Permian oilfield waste or water-treatment operator, which is a crowded, existing market. It fails the >$300M bar and the 'nobody named it' bar. Counter-evidence: The strongest case for the idea has three parts. First, if Texas discharge/land-application rules require concentrate to be minimized (ZLD or crystallizers) because disposal-well capacity and seismicity limits tighten, the radium would end up in solid salt at concentrations above 30 pCi/g. Disposal-well limits and seismicity are already pressuring the Permian (see Capper deck, https://nmpwrc.nmsu.edu/120Laura20Capper20EnergyMakers20NM20OK20to20DISTRIBUTE206.9.20261.pdf). Second, there are zero permitted commercial NORM landfills or pits in Texas (https://rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/applications-and-permits/environmental-permit-types/norm-waste/). Third, Capper projects about $16B in produced-water spend by 2030. If the ZLD scenario plays out, a first-mover surface NORM monofill near Andrews could command strong pricing. Even so, this is a regulatory bet on a ZLD mandate that does not exist, and the realistic tonnage still caps revenue around $100M a year.

Killed:
- Ra-226 from legacy sources or NORM scale as Ac-225 feedstock: already being done. QSA Global and NorthStar signed a multi-year closed-loop Ra-226 deal in May 2026 (https://manufacturingchemist.com/northstar-qsa-global-partnership-actinium-225-supply), and DOE IRP/PNNL is recovering NIST legacy Ra-226 (https://www.pnnl.gov/news-media/legacy-waste-transformed-strategic-medical-radioisotope-supply). Targets are recycled, so feedstock demand is gram-scale and well under $300M a year.

Capital-heavy and too small.

Customers are government bodies (NDA) and revenue would be lumpy and under $300M a year.

- Activated and contaminated metal melting/recycling from decommissioning: incumbents (EnergySolutions Bear Creek, EDF Cyclife) and US restrictions on releasing volumetrically contaminated metal cap the upside.

- Lithium DLE and geothermal brine NORM residues (Smackover, Salton Sea): small residue volumes. Folded into the Permian NORM hypothesis.
- Mixed waste treatment: Perma-Fix is the incumbent and the market is small and DOE-dependent.
- Decommissioning clearance and free-release measurement services: crowded (Mirion, Babcock, Cyclife), and US plant restarts and life extensions are shrinking the near-term decommissioning wave.
- Oilfield TENORM pipe and scale decon yards (onshore US and North Sea): an existing services niche with incumbents. Not novel and probably under $300M a year.

## Hunter: big-software-ai

### Simulation-first sterilization modality transfer (Co-60/EtO to X-ray), used as the wedge into X-ray sterilization capacity (novelty 3, size 6, doability 4)

Use GPU Monte Carlo dose mapping to replace most of the physical dosimeter mapping and revalidation work when device makers move product families off cobalt-60 gamma or EtO onto X-ray. Then sell the X-ray capacity those transfers need, since a pure software play is too small.

- Gap evidence: Two modalities sterilize about 90% of devices: EtO about 50% (roughly 20B devices a year) and gamma about 40%. US EtO capacity ran above 90% utilization with essentially no slack, and lead times are 4-6+ weeks. Co-60 comes from a few reactor countries (Argentina, Canada, China, India, Russia), takes 18-36 months to produce and cannot be stockpiled (5.27-yr half-life). X-ray and e-beam capacity is described as limited. Moving one product family costs about $50k-200k in validation plus $25k-100k for regulatory submission (https://meddeviceguide.com/blog/sterilization-supplier-strategy-capacity-risk-medical-device-guide). Monte Carlo studies already show gamma, e-beam and X-ray are physically equivalent for sterilization (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.01590). Industry talks cover simulation-assisted dose mapping (https://cirms.org/wp-content/uploads/cirms2024/presentations/Monday/Medical/CIRMS2024-Kroc.pdf, https://indico.fnal.gov/event/49747/attachments/148522/191199/Christiane%20Beerlage_Zimmer%20Biomet.pdf). The idea is to bundle simulation-backed transfer packages (dose maps, worst-case loading patterns, regulatory dossier) with guaranteed X-ray capacity.
- Gain: Device makers get cheaper and faster transfers. The figures above cost a transfer at $75k-300k; simulation could plausibly cut physical mapping runs and months of schedule, though that saving is my estimate, not proven. It also removes exposure to Co-60 geopolitics and EtO regulation. Capacity is the actual product sold: fewer weeks waiting and a second source.
- Market: Software or validation services alone are capped below $300M/yr. Tens of thousands of product families times $75k-300k is a large one-time pool, but it does not recur. The >$300M/yr path is X-ray contract sterilization capacity itself. Contract sterilization is a multi-billion-dollar market dominated by Steris and Sotera Health/Sterigenics (same source). Treat that size as my own estimate; I did not verify segment revenue figures in this pass.
- Prior art: RayXpert from TRAD is already a commercial Monte Carlo tool aimed at irradiation and dose mapping (https://iiaglobal.com/iia-news/trad-tests-radiations-release-of-rayxpert-1-9/). Triple Ring Technologies sells dose-simulation case studies (https://www.tripleringtech.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/case-study-dose.pdf). Incumbent validation labs do this work (Nelson Labs is owned by Sotera). Steris and Sotera are already building X-ray capacity. Pairing simulation as a wedge with capacity has not been publicly packaged that I could find, but every component exists.
- Killer risk: The capital-heavy capacity business competes head-on with Steris and Sotera, which control both the capacity and the validation labs. FDA and notified-body acceptance of simulation in place of physical dose mapping is unproven, and dosimeter checks stay mandatory under ISO 11137. The pure software wedge is small and already served by RayXpert.

### Radiation-reliability layer for orbital AI compute (software fault tolerance plus transport-simulation-based qualification of COTS GPUs) (novelty 4, size 4, doability 5)

Combine radiation-transport-informed fault models, application-aware error mitigation and qualification evidence into a stack that lets orbital data-center builders fly commercial GPUs without heavy-ion testing every SKU.

- Gap evidence: Academic work argues for application-aware radiation tolerance in orbital computing (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.11853). Orbital data-center plans and dedicated hardware such as NVIDIA's Space-1 are described in industry write-ups (https://introl.com/blog/orbital-data-centers-space-ai-infrastructure-guide-2025, https://www.softwareseni.com/nvidia-vera-rubin-space-1-the-hardware-behind-orbital-ai-compute/). Aerospace Corp states demand for SEE test hours significantly exceeds supply across the few US facilities (https://aerospace.org/fact-sheet/alternative-sources-single-event-effects-testing-fact-sheet). CERN has a concept design for a new heavy-ion facility (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26369). That makes simulation-first qualification valuable.
- Gain: Cheaper COTS compute in orbit instead of rad-hard parts that cost orders of magnitude more per FLOP, faster qualification that avoids scarce beam time, and fewer silent errors corrupting in-orbit inference or training.
- Market: Speculative. SEE testing services are only about $0.4B in 2025 (low-quality market report: https://marketintelo.com/report/single-event-effects-testing-services-market), so testing alone fails the bar. Reaching >$300M/yr requires orbital compute to scale to many GW and a per-node software licence or royalty. That is plausible but not demonstrated.
- Prior art: Melagen Labs (Techstars) works on space compute radiation tolerance (https://www.techstars.com/blog/startup-profile/from-techstars-to-the-iss-how-melagen-labs-is-solving-spaces-biggest-compute). Rad-hard vendors are established (Cobham/CAES, Aitech, Ramon.Space). Free tools exist (CREME96, OMERE, Vanderbilt MRED), as does iRoC soft-error software. Hyperscalers handle SDC in-house; Meta's tooling is an example (https://engineering.fb.com/2025/07/22/data-infrastructure/how-meta-keeps-its-ai-hardware-reliable/).
- Killer risk: Orbital data centers may never scale. Builders like SpaceX and Google will vertically integrate reliability, and NVIDIA ships space-qualified SKUs. Market timing and customer concentration could kill it.

**Skeptics on 'Radiation-reliability layer for orbital AI compute (software fault tolerance plus transport-simulation-based qualification of COTS GPUs)':**

- weakened: Realistic range: $5-30M/yr as a niche tools and consulting business. That means selling fault models, campaign design and insurer-facing reliability evidence to second-tier orbital-compute startups and space insurers, much like iRoC's market. Reaching >$300M/yr would require (a) orbital compute scaling to multiple GW across many independent operators, (b) those operators buying a per-node licence instead of building in-house, and (c) NVIDIA not shipping its own mitigation stack with Space-1. I put all three together below 5%. The one angle worth testing is an underwriting-grade reliability-evidence product for space insurers and financiers of orbital compute. It is unproven and the market is small. Counter-evidence: Google's own beam campaign found COTS TPUs viable, with SDC at about 1 per 3M inferences (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.19468). The leading buyers can qualify their own silicon and treat the inference problem as minor. Meanwhile every orbital-compute startup (Aethero, Sophia Space, Colossus, Spirit NOVI) already builds its own fault-tolerance layer. On demand, customers say flight data beats simulation (Melagen/Techstars). On supply, iRoC already sells simulation-based SER qualification.

- weakened: Near term (2027-2029), a realistic ceiling is about $5-30M/yr, from roughly 8-15 non-captive operators paying $0.5-2M each. My base case for 2032+ is $50-150M/yr. Beyond that there is a less than 15% chance of exceeding $300M/yr, and it needs two things: at least 1 GW of non-captive orbital compute, and a defensible royalty or certification position that NVIDIA (via firmware) and the captive hyperscalers (SpaceX/xAI, Google, Amazon) do not take for themselves. Novelty is low to moderate, because Melagen Labs already pitches a full-stack radiation platform. On the market lens it is weakened, close to killed. If independents together reach several GW in the early 2030s, and a neutral qualification standard becomes what insurers and launch providers require, a per-kW royalty could pass $300M/yr. ## Hunter: enabling-hardware

### Digital-manufactured high-power RF tubes (klystrons, magnetrons, gyrotrons) for accelerators, linacs and fusion heating: a second-source tube foundry (novelty 4, size 5, doability 4)

Use additive and digitized vacuum-electron-device manufacturing, already proven at mmWave by Elve, for the L/S/C-band klystrons and magnetrons, and later the gyrotrons, that medical and industrial linacs, sterilization X-ray machines, science accelerators and fusion plants depend on, and make cathodes and ceramics in-house to get around the two-vendor bottlenecks.

- Gap evidence: The first Long-Pulse and CW Klystron Workshop (SNS/ORNL, 23-25 Sept 2025) found only four main klystron makers: Canon ETD, Microwave Power Products, Stellant and Thales. Thermionic cathode supply has 'coalesced into only two primary vendors for all cathodes'. Ceramic lead times of '6 months and 1 year are not unusual'. BeO comes only from vendors in Japan and Wales. Demand is 'feast or famine', and refurbished tubes cost 70-75% of a new one. The workshop's recommendations were a procurement database, a tube development center and working groups, which is a cry for help, not a fix (https://conference.sns.gov/event/538/attachments/1829/17905/Summary%20Report%20of%20the%20First%20Long%20Pulse%20and%20CW%20Klystron%20Workshop%20.pdf ; https://conference.sns.gov/event/538). DARPA (March 2025) confirmed that a traditional TWT takes 12-18 months per unit and that Elve's LAM3D additive process cuts this to weeks, but only at Q/V/E-band and above (https://www.darpa.mil/news/2025/accelerating-critical-component).
- Gain: Cut tube lead time from 6-18 months to weeks. That cuts accelerator downtime: a linac or sterilization line with no spare tube sits idle. It would also give buyers a non-incumbent price below the 70-75% refurb benchmark, plus the ability to iterate designs (efficiency, lifetime) quickly. Klystron efficiency gains reduce the power bill on MW-class accelerators directly.
- Market: ABI Research put high-power microwave and mmWave vacuum electron devices (TWTs, klystrons, magnetrons, CFAs, gyrotrons) at about $1B/yr. That figure is old (2008) and I did not verify a current one (https://www.tvtechnology.com/news/microwave-tubes-still-a-billiondollar-business). Klystrons serve radar, satcom, medical and accelerator uses (https://lucintel.com/klystrons-technology-market.aspx). Honest read: the non-defense accelerator, medical and industrial slice is probably a few hundred $M/yr. Getting past $300M/yr in revenue almost certainly needs defense radar and EW tubes too, plus fusion gyrotrons if fusion plants actually get built.
- Prior art: Incumbents: CPI, Stellant (the former L3Harris EDD), Thales, Canon ETD, Mitsubishi, Teledyne e2v (magnetrons), and Kyoto Fusioneering and Gycom (gyrotrons). Elve ($15M Series A in 2024, DARPA SBIR) is the closest startup. It is focused on mmWave TWTs for satcom and backhaul and could move down-band (https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240221377245/en/Elve-Secures-15M-in-Series-A-Funding-to-Increase-Manufacturing-and-Expand-Customer-Base). Solid-state GaN SSPAs are replacing tubes in some synchrotrons.
- Killer risk: Elve or an incumbent takes the accelerator band first. GaN solid-state amplifiers replace tubes below about 1 MW. Qualifying a multi-MW pulsed klystron takes years and a lot of capital, with very few units per year per design. Without defense contracts the market is lumpy and capped under $300M.

### Multi-isotope stable-isotope separation foundry (plasma or centrifuge) for the ~44 isotopes the US still buys almost only from Russia (novelty 3, size 4, doability 4)

Build a compact, multi-element separator line, as a product or tolling service, for the non-uranium stable isotopes that underpin radiopharma, quantum and neutrino physics: Ni-64, Mo-100, Yb-176/171, Ge-76, Si-28, Xe-129, Zn-68. The aim is to sell into the gap before DOE's SIPRC ramps.

Killed on size.
- Co-60 supply crunch and gamma sterilization alternatives: already being addressed. Bruce adjusters, Darlington around 2028, and a Nordion-Westinghouse US PWR Co-60 program around 2030 (https://cirms.org/wp-content/uploads/cirms2026/presentations/monday/plenary/CIRMS2026_Weins.pdf). X-ray and e-beam conversion is crowded (IBA, STERIS, Sotera).
- Compact neutron generators for isotopes, NTD, imaging and testing: crowded (Phoenix, SHINE, Adelphi, Starfire and others) and widely known. Killed on novelty.
- BNCT accelerators: TAE Life Sciences, Neutron Therapeutics and Sumitomo are already there, and clinical volume is small. Killed on size and novelty.
- High-temperature salt and sodium pumps and valves (including EM pumps): developers such as Kairos build their own, the fleet is too small before the 2030s, and per-unit volume is low. Killed on size.
- Gyrotrons alone for fusion heating: Kyoto Fusioneering, Gycom, CPI and Thales are there, and near-term demand is a few dozen units. Folded into the RF tube survivor as an upside, not a standalone business.
- Tritium-compatible components: the fusion market has no meaningful volume before about 2035. Killed on size.
- Radiation-tolerant power electronics and cameras for SMRs, decommissioning and fusion: fragmented, dominated by Mirion and ISEC-type niche players. I found no evidence of a buyer pool above $300M/yr.
- Glovebox lines for HALEU and fuel labs: small capex market served by MBraun, Jacomex and others. Killed on size.
- Solid-state (GaN) replacement for linac RF: known and pursued by incumbents and labs (BNL linac RF upgrade, https://indico.bnl.gov/event/30097/contributions/117495/attachments/66897/114896/04%20C%20AD%20MAC%2022%20%20%20200%20MeV%20Linac%20RF%20Amplifier%20Upgrade,%20Freddy%20Severino.pdf). Not novel.

## Hunter: contrarian-wildcards

### Licensed-operator foundry plus nuclear operating company for owners that are not utilities (novelty 4, size 6, doability 4)

Run an ATP-flight-school-style pipeline that recruits and pre-trains reactor operators and SROs on generic LWR, HTGR and SFR simulators, then sells either placed crews or a full contract operating company to hyperscalers, industrials and developers that own reactors but have no operating organisation.

- Gap evidence: A May 2026 NCEA workforce study calls the 18-24 month NRC-licensing pipeline for each operator 'the single binding bottleneck on fleet expansion speed'. It counts 11,500-13,200 operator and technician FTEs today and expects the total nuclear workforce to peak at 66-85k FTEs in 2029-31, against a 2024 base of 67,900, and says 'it will be very difficult to provide the needed workforce based on current trends' (https://energyanalytics.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026-05-NCEA-NuclearWorkforce_Vinoski-Hiatt.pdf). Owners that are not utilities are signing large deals. Meta's January 2026 deals total up to 6.6 GW across Vistra, TerraPower and Oklo, including 2x345 MW Natrium by 2032 and a 1.2 GW Oklo campus (https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/nuclear-industry-kicks-2026-major-public-and-private-sector-announcements-0). Every new design needs licensed crews before startup. Hadron's CNO said in June 2026: 'You cannot commission a nuclear plant without certified operators' (https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260609236153/en/). Today the operator-supply problem goes either to the incumbent utility partner (TVA, Energy Northwest, Vistra) or to each vendor building its own staff from scratch. Nobody sells a shared, design-agnostic operator pipeline.
- Gain: Every month a plant sits complete without licensed crews delays revenue of about $0.5-1M per day for a ~300-1,000 MW unit at $70-100/MWh (my arithmetic, not sourced). A shared pipeline also stops vendors and utilities poaching each other's SROs, which is already driving wage inflation.
- Market: Training alone (about 500-1,000 candidates/yr at about $100-200k each) is roughly $50-200M/yr, which is under the bar on its own. The $300M+ path is the operating-company model: contract O&M on roughly 5 GW of owner-operated advanced capacity at about $20-30/MWh of O&M adds up to about $0.9-1.3B/yr of revenue billed through (these O&M figures are my estimates, not sourced). The demand side is about 6.6 GW from Meta alone plus the Amazon/X-energy and Google/Kairos programs.
- Prior art: GSE Solutions builds plant-specific simulators and has a staffing arm (Hadron partnership above). Utilities (Constellation, TVA, Energy Northwest) operate plants for hyperscaler-backed projects. Vendors such as Oklo plan to self-operate. State programs include Michigan's $38.4M training program (https://smrintel.com/news/michigan-joins-38-4m-nuclear-workforce-training/). University research reactors (MIT NRL) train small numbers. I found nobody combining a cross-vendor pipeline with an independent operator licensee.
- Killer risk: Part 55 licenses are plant-specific, so generic pre-training only shortens the pipeline by an unproven amount. Becoming an NRC-licensed operator requires a credible nuclear organisation, an NRC-approved QA program, and years of track record. Owners may simply contract Constellation or TVA, which already have fleets and reputations.

### Developer for state-hosted spent-fuel campuses, buying down DOE's standard-contract liability (novelty 3, size 7, doability 2)

Act as private developer and operator for DOE's new Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campuses (NLICs), consolidating stranded spent fuel from shutdown and operating plants, and get paid out of the roughly $40B that DOE otherwise pays utilities in breach damages.

- Gap evidence: DOE has paid about $11.1B in standard-contract breach damages, and its OIG puts remaining liability at $37.6-44.5B, up about 10% in a year (https://morganlewis.com/blogs/upandatom/2024/12/us-spent-fuel-storage-liability-increases-while-other-countries-develop-geologic-repositories). A January 28, 2026 DOE RFI asks states to host NLICs that must include long-term used-fuel storage and recycling, financed mainly with private and state capital. States were selected for MOUs in summer 2026 and host-agreement talks run through fall 2026 (https://www.tn.gov/content/dam/tn/environment/energy/documents/tdec-oep_nlic-informational-one-page-document.pdf). Federal environmental liability is $667.3B in total, of which DOE holds $538.6B (https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-109001.pdf). The NLIC route creates a new procurement opening for an integrator owned by neither a vendor nor a utility.
- Gain: Every year of delay adds billions to the taxpayer liability. Each ISFSI-only shutdown site costs millions a year in security and upkeep, which DOE reimburses (no per-site figure fetched). Consolidation removes those duplicated fixed costs.
- Market: The liability pool is $37.6-44.5B. Even 5-10% of it captured as fees over 20 years is about $100-220M/yr. Above $300M/yr only if the campus also earns storage fees from new advanced-reactor fuel and recycling offtake. I score this as plausible but not proven.
- Prior art: Holtec (HI-STORE CISF) and Interim Storage Partners (Orano/WCS) hold NRC CISF licenses. Deep Isolation works on disposal. Oklo, Curio and SHINE pursue recycling. Several states have bid. This is a well-known policy fight, not an unnamed gap.
- Killer risk: The Nuclear Waste Policy Act makes DOE the party that takes title, so a private company cannot easily take liability off utilities without legislation and appropriations. State consent politics killed earlier CISFs (Texas and New Mexico). Incumbents with existing licenses will be the default NLIC partners. This is not a YC-speed business.

**Skeptics on 'Licensed-operator foundry plus nuclear operating company for owners that are not utilities':**

- weakened: Novelty is low to moderate.
- Utilities are already offered as operators for vendors' customers (Xcel-NuScale 2021, Constellation-ULC 2022).
- Centralised and outsourced operator training exists (NEXA and Accelerant, plus vendor training centres).
- The only less-crowded angle is a Part 53 "generally licensed reactor operator" crew and remote-support operating company serving microreactors that customers own (Nuclea-style, defense, mining).

Realistic size:
- Training and placement: about $30-100M/yr, since most training is facility-specific and limited to licensees.
- The operating-company model: revenue mostly after 2031. The addressable owners without an operating organisation are likely under 1-2 GW equivalent through 2035, because Meta, Google and Amazon buy power while TerraPower, Oklo, Kairos, Vistra and Energy Northwest run their own plants.
- Gross margin on that revenue is thin.

Odds of more than $300M/yr within 10 years are low, perhaps 5-10%. Trust is a real moat but it favours incumbents. The idea is not dead, but it should be re-scoped to the Part 53 microreactor operating-company niche or dropped. Counter-evidence: Points in the idea's favour:
- I found no venture-backed startup combining a cross-vendor pipeline with an independent operator licensee, and GSE's Hadron scope is simulator-only, so a services gap exists.
- Microreactor vendors such as Nuclea explicitly sell reactors for customers to own and operate (remote communities, mining, defense, data centers). Part 53's "generally licensed reactor operator" category for self-reliant mitigation facilities could make a shared multi-site operating company for small reactors workable.
- The NCEA study does call the operator pipeline the binding bottleneck.
- The Xcel-NuScale MOU (2021) shows that vendors selling to owners who don't operate do want a ready-made operator partner. That validates demand even though it weakens novelty.

- killed: Training and placement: about $15-40M/yr at maturity. Contract operating company: about $0-100M/yr of gross billings by 2033-35, mostly from Dow-type industrials and a few small vendors, at thin services margins. This fails the >$300M/yr test. The largest named demand (Meta, Amazon, Google, Microsoft) is explicitly assigned to vendors or incumbent utilities as operators. Vendors (X-energy, TerraPower) are building in-house training and O&M businesses. Part 53's generally licensed operators and customizable staffing shrink the bottleneck. And nobody pays for a cross-vendor operator pipeline today. Counter-evidence: The workforce shortage is real. The NCEA May 2026 study calls operator licensing the binding bottleneck. Hadron's CNO says you cannot commission without certified operators, and Hadron is a microreactor vendor without a utility parent, so it needs outside help. Dow (Long Mott, 320 MWe) is a real industrial owner-licensee with no nuclear operating organisation. Fermi America is a non-utility developer of up to 4 AP1000s that may eventually need an operator. Precedent exists for contract operation by a non-owner: Exelon ran Fort Calhoun for OPPD, and BWXT and Amentum run government nuclear facilities. If many microreactor vendors such as Hadron, Aalo, Radiant, Last Energy and Deep Fission choose not to build their own operations groups, a shared crew-staffing firm could make money as a niche business. But that is a staffing or services business of roughly $20-80M/yr, and it competes with Accelerant, GSE and vendor training centers.

Killed:
- Hanford/DOE-EM off-site tank-waste grouting and disposal service: the money is real (GAO says grouting at $20-45/gal versus about $1,400/gal for vitrification saves about $95B, and 24M gal of LAW grouting is worth $480M-1.1B: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108878, https://www.ans.org/news/2026-06-01/article-8083/gao-grouting-hanford-tank-waste-could-cost-more-than-11b/), but subcontracts went to Perma-Fix, EnergySolutions and WCS in August 2026 (https://www.ans.org/news/2026-08-12/article-8288/hanford-site-grouting-developments-could-accelerate-cleanup/), and grouting alone works out to about $35-80M/yr. Killed for incumbents plus size.
- EM project-controls and data software (GAO flags inconsistent cost and schedule data across a remaining cleanup bill above $500B: https://www.ans.org/news/2026-03-05/article-7820/doe-nuclear-cleanup-costs-schedule-delays-continue-to-rise-gao-says/): software spend is capped well under $300M/yr and it is a federal procurement grind.
- New US UF6 conversion plant: the only US converter, Metropolis, has a backlog above $2B, but FluxPoint Energy and UEC's UR&C/Fluor are already pursuing new plants (https://www.ans.org/news/2026-04-01/article-7899/new-company-throws-hat-into-uranium-conversion-ring/). Not unnamed, and the capex is huge.
- HALEU/LEU enrichment capacity: GAO-26-107385 (Aug 2026) shows $900M plus $1.8B in DOE awards already made (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107385). Crowded and capital-intensive.
- Co-60 supply for medical sterilization (about 40% of single-use devices; Canada about 50% of supply, Russia about 25%): Westinghouse-Nordion PWR targets and OPG Darlington are already coming online in 2026-2030 (https://www.ans.org/news/article-7531/co60-tariffs-would-undermine-us-healthcare-sswg-warns/, https://www.nsenergybusiness.com/analysis/analysing-growing-demand-for-cobalt-60-in-healthcare-applications/). Incumbents are moving, and isotope revenue is likely under $300M/yr.
- Nuclear-grade graphite for HTGRs and MSRs: X-energy has supply with SGL and Radiant with Amsted, so it is already addressed (https://www.neimagazine.com/supply-chain/radiant-and-amsted-graphite-strengthen-nuclear-grade-graphite-supply-chain/, https://interestingengineering.com/energy/x-energy-sgl-carbon-double-nuclear-graphite-capacity).
- Spent-fuel breach-damages claims-prep and litigation-finance firm: the pool is $37.6-44.5B, but a contingency take is about $50-100M/yr and the work belongs to law firms. Too small.
- Nuclear-attribute 24/7 retail for neoclouds: Constellation, Vistra and Talen sell direct to hyperscalers (Meta-Vistra 2.1 GW, Amazon-Talen 1.9 GW, per the Perkins Coie 2026 roundup above). Margins sit with plant owners, so it is low novelty.
- Nuclear fuel-cycle forward exchange or price platform: UxC/CME futures, Sprott and brokers already exist, and fee revenue is far under $300M/yr.
- Craft-labor staffing for the AP1000 and SMR wave: real need (NCEA expects skilled trades to grow from about 9k to 16-21.5k FTEs by 2030), but Bechtel, Day & Zimmermann, union halls and staffing majors already own it. Zero novelty.
- Low-level-waste metal clearance and recycling for decommissioning: EnergySolutions and EDF Cyclife are incumbents, there is no US clearance standard, and it is capped well under $300M/yr.
- Private irradiation-test reactor time for advanced fuel qualification: a real scarcity, but the market is around $100M and it is capital- and licensing-heavy.
- Li-7 / B-10 enriched isotopes for PWR chemistry and MSRs: the PWR market is tiny, and it overlaps ground covered by the isotope lens (Cl-37/N-15 excluded).
- Redeveloping decommissioned plant sites for data centers: Holtec, NextEra and Constellation own the sites and are doing it themselves (Duane Arnold, Palisades).
- Submarine industrial-base nuclear components (defence-prime inversion): falls into the excluded contract-manufacturing and supplier-qualification ground.
- Due diligence on FOAK delivery dates for hyperscalers and sovereign wealth funds: a consulting-sized market, and delivery-risk wraps are excluded as MGAs.
