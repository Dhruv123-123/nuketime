# Gap hunt, round 3 (raw agent output, 2026-10-07)

Seven cross-silo hypotheses, each with one investigator and one adversarial skeptic. Agent-written; re-check claims before use. Synthesis in `PICK4.md`.

## radiation-sdc-ai: investigator killed, skeptic killed

**Thesis.** The physics holds up, but the business does not. Radiation-induced soft errors do cost frontier clusters something real. My bottom-up estimate is about 0.3 to 0.6% of cluster goodput at sea level, which is roughly $30 to 60M per year per GW, given about $10B per year all-in cost per GW. At high-altitude campuses such as the 1.8 GW Crusoe/Tallgrass site in Cheyenne (about 6,000 ft, roughly 3 to 4x the neutron flux), it rises to about 1 to 2%, or $100 to 200M per GW per year. Liquid cooling adds a thermal-neutron (B-10) share of about 20 to 30% on top. But most of this tax cannot be controlled: high-energy neutrons need metres of shielding, sites are chosen for power and not for flux, and flux moves only about 10% with pressure and season. Because the best checkpoint interval scales with the square root of MTBF, flux-aware checkpointing saves almost nothing. The flux can also be predicted from barometric pressure, so an in-rack monitor adds little. The value a third party could actually unlock is a small slice of a tax that is already small: a cheap thermal-neutron liner around liquid-cooled racks, plus sorting transient from permanent faults so fewer good GPUs get drained or RMA'd. My estimate is about $3 to 7B per year of tax worldwide at 50 to 100 GW, with perhaps 5 to 15% of it removable and 10 to 30% of that capturable. That is tens of millions per year, not more than $300M. The bigger SDC problem, mercurial cores with manufacturing defects, is not caused by radiation, and proteanTecs, Synopsys SLM, NVIDIA DCGM and the hyperscalers' in-house fleet scanners already work on it.

**Evidence.** 1) Llama 3 405B: 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days on 16,384 H100s. Breakdown: faulty GPU 148 (30.1%), HBM3 72 (17.2%), GPU SRAM 19 (4.5%), network 8.4%, GPU system processor 4.1%. Meta still kept more than 90% effective training time (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21783 ; https://www.sdxcentral.com/news/meta-report-details-hundreds-of-gpu-and-hbm3-related-interruptions-to-llama-3-training-run/). Memory together is about 22% of interruptions. The Llama 3 table, as cited by TU Berlin, also lists 6 SDC incidents in that run (https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00726v1).
2) SDC frequency: TU Berlin cites Google Gemini having SDC-related disruptions about every 1 to 2 weeks. It gives no split between radiation and defects for terrestrial systems, and says radiation transients matter mainly for space data centers (https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00726v1).
3) NCSA Delta field study (A100 and H100, 11.7M GPU-hours): system-wide MTBE of 1.4 h on A100 and 1.9 h on H100. Per-GPU memory MTBE is 88,768 h on H100 against 283,271 h on A100. Only 24 uncorrectable ECC errors on H100 over 2.1M GPU-hours, and row remapping and containment mitigated 92% of them. The paper never mentions radiation. Most job-killing errors are GSP, NVLink and PMU faults, not memory (https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11901v4).
4) How much is radiation: in DOE/AMD field data, about 42% of single-bit DRAM faults were transient and about 37% permanent. Transient DRAM fault rates at LANL Cielo (7,320 ft) against sea-level Hopper were 2.92x for vendor A, 1.71x for vendor B and 0.88x for vendor C. SRAM error rates at altitude were "not substantially higher" thanks to ECC and interleaving. The authors call particle strikes "noticeable" but not dominant (https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/Classes/739/Web/Papers/memoryerrors-asplos15.pdf). In the earlier comparison, Cielo had about 3.4x Jaguar's L3 transient faults, and the top of a rack had 20% more than the bottom (https://www.infoworld.com/article/2609043/elevation-plays-a-role-in-memory-error-rates.html).
5) Flux and altitude: neutron flux above 50 MeV is 26.4 n/cm²/h at LANL (2,125 m), against about 13 for the NYC sea-level reference and 3.7 at Guangzhou. Sites above 2,000 m see 5 to 7x sea level, and geomagnetic storms add up to about 35% temporarily. The authors say pressure monitoring alone lets operators anticipate radiation errors "without specialized equipment" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07770). The fetched summary said "6-9% per hPa", which looks like a misread. The β0 of -9.2e-3/hPa quoted there means about 0.9% per hPa.
6) Thermal neutrons and liquid cooling: NTT and Hokkaido University found that low-energy neutrons cause about 1/5 to 1/4 of FPGA errors, and expect the thermal-neutron count to "increase significantly" with water cooling (https://theregister.com/2023/03/17/neutrons_cause_soft_errors_electronics). Rech (UFRGS) found thermal-neutron FIT can match or exceed high-energy FIT in COTS GPUs, DDR and an APU. He blames natural boron and flags liquid cooling, concrete and weather (https://indico.cern.ch/event/1198078/contributions/5037300). Google's TPUs have been beam-tested for high-energy and thermal neutron sensitivity, IEEE TNS vol. 69(3), 2022 (https://suchda.hds.hebis.de/EdsRecord/edseee%252Cedseee.9676631).
7) High-altitude AI capacity is real: the Crusoe/Tallgrass campus in Cheyenne, WY starts at 1.8 GW and could scale to 10 GW (https://broadbandbreakfast.com/cheyenne-to-host-data-center-using-more-electricity-than-all-wyoming-homes/).
Cost model, my estimate: Delta's estimate of memory MTBE puts radiation-attributable interruptions at about 22% (memory) times 30 to 50% (transient, particle-driven), which is about 7 to 11% of interruptions. If interruption-driven goodput loss is about 5%, radiation costs about 0.35 to 0.55% of cluster time at sea level. At about $10B per year per GW (roughly $40 to 50B capex over 5 years plus power), that is $35 to 55M per GW per year at sea level and $100 to 200M at 6,000 ft. Fast in-memory checkpointing makes the real loss lower.

**Market ceiling.** Buyers number about 15 to 20: Meta, Google, Microsoft, AWS, Oracle, OpenAI/Stargate, xAI, Anthropic's compute partners, CoreWeave, Crusoe, Lambda, Nebius, Together, plus sovereign clouds. Installed AI capacity by 2028 to 2030 is about 50 to 100 GW, or about 30 to 60M accelerators. The total radiation tax is about $3.5 to 7B per year at a blended $70M per GW, my estimate. Removable share: a thermal-neutron liner (borated polyethylene or Cd around liquid-cooled racks) could cut about 20 to 30% of radiation errors, which is 5 to 10% of the tax. Better transient-versus-permanent attribution could avoid some needless GPU drains and RMAs, maybe another 5%. Site selection and checkpoint tuning are worth almost nothing (see thesis). That leaves about $250 to 700M per year of removable value worldwide. Capturing 20% of it gives a vendor ceiling of about $50 to 140M per year. Hardware alone is tiny: one neutron or thermal-neutron monitor per hall at $5 to 50k across a few thousand halls is under $50M one-time. Software at $10 per GPU per year across 40M GPUs would be $400M, but radiation costs only about $50 to 100 per GPU per year (0.5% of about $14k per year all-in), and most of that cannot be controlled. Paying 10 to 20% of it to a startup when NVIDIA DCGM, proteanTecs and in-house SRE teams already cover fault triage is not credible. Getting past $300M per year would require broadening into general GPU fleet reliability, which is a different and crowded company.

**Prior art.** Physics and characterization: LANL/LANSCE, ChipIr (STFC) and Paolo Rech's group at UFRGS/Trento on GPU, TPU and DDR thermal-neutron FIT, including liquid cooling (https://indico.cern.ch/event/1198078/contributions/5037300 ; https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/work/46880844). NTT and Hokkaido University on thermal neutrons and water cooling (https://theregister.com/2023/03/17/neutrons_cause_soft_errors_electronics). Exascale-site flux modelling with barometric forecasting of SDC (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07770). AMD and DOE field altitude studies (https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/Classes/739/Web/Papers/memoryerrors-asplos15.pdf). Soft-error testing services: iRoC Technologies, plus the Hitachi and Fujitsu SER work (https://www.fujitsu.com/global/documents/about/resources/publications/fstj/archives/vol46-3/paper07.pdf). There are patents on soft-error-rate calculators and on dynamic SER discrimination for enterprise systems (https://patents.google.com/patent/US9645871 ; https://eureka.patsnap.com/patent-US7526683B1). That last one is essentially the transient-versus-permanent attribution idea, and Oracle/Sun has patented it. Fleet SDC detection: Meta's Fleetscanner and Ripple, Google's "Cores that don't count", and Alibaba's CPU SDC study. On the commercial side, proteanTecs sells in-chip agents for SDC and lifecycle monitoring of AI hardware (https://www.proteantecs.com/resources/outsmarting-silent-data-corruption-in-ai-processors-with-two-stage-detection ; https://proteantecs.com/news/proteantecs-gains-momentum-as-the-trusted-choice-for-lifecycle-monitoring-of-ai-hardware), as do Synopsys SLM (https://www.synopsys.com/blogs/chip-design/ai-infrastructure-hardware-failures-data-corruption.html) and NVIDIA DCGM with row remapping. Academic SDC detection for LLM training: Understanding SDC in LLM Training, ACL 2025 (https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.996/), and TU Berlin 2026 (https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00726v1). Researchers have named the gap repeatedly. Nobody has built it as a commercial product, mostly because the value is small.

**Eight-week proof.** Feasible but likely to confirm the kill. (a) Build a public model that combines Llama 3 and Delta failure rates, the AMD/DOE transient share and altitude multipliers, and NTT/Rech thermal fractions into $ per GW by site. A week of work. (b) Put a $2 to 10k He-3 or 6Li thermal-neutron detector next to liquid-cooled and air-cooled racks at a friendly neocloud or university cluster, for example NCSA Delta or a Denver/Utah colo. Measure the thermal-flux ratio and correlate it, and barometric pressure, with NVIDIA Xid 48/63/94/95 and ECC SBE/DBE logs. (c) Ask one neocloud for an LOI on rack-level borated liners. The likely result is that thermal flux runs perhaps 1.2 to 2x higher near water loops, while correctable error counts are too sparse to give a significant correlation in 8 weeks, given about 1 uncorrectable error per 90k GPU-hours on H100. That would itself show the signal is economically small.

**Killer risk.** The controllable share of radiation errors is too small to fund a company. At sea level they are probably under 1% of goodput. ECC and row remapping already contain most HBM upsets (92% on H100, per Delta). Most job-killing faults are non-radiation: defective silicon, GSP/firmware, NVLink and network. Flux is nearly constant and can be predicted from free barometric data. Operators choose sites for power, not for cosmic-ray flux. Whatever remains, NVIDIA, proteanTecs, Synopsys and the hyperscaler SRE teams can absorb as a feature. The one strong exception is space or orbital data centers, where radiation dominates. That is a different hypothesis, and space-radiation engineering incumbents already serve it.

**Skeptic.** I tried to overturn both the novelty and the size of this hypothesis and could do neither. The investigator's own conclusion holds: the physics is real but the business is not.

1. Size, checked against Meta's actual numbers. The Llama 3 paper (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21783, Table 5) reports 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days on 16k H100s. Of these:
   - 148 (30.1%) were faulty GPUs.
   - 72 (17.2%) were HBM3 memory.
   - 19 (4.5%) were GPU SRAM.
   - Silent data corruption was only about 1.4%.
   - Effective training time was still above 90%.
   The categories where radiation could plausibly play a part (HBM uncorrectable errors, SRAM, SDC) add up to about 23% of interruptions. Suppose radiation caused a third to half of those. Interruptions cost at most about 10% of goodput in total, so radiation would account for roughly 0.5 to 1% of goodput. That agrees with the investigator's 0.3 to 0.6% at sea level, perhaps 2x higher at the top end. Meta's measured diurnal throughput swing from heat-driven DVFS (1 to 2%) is about as large as the whole radiation tax. Even if the tax doubled, the removable and capturable slice would stay under about $300M a year.

2. The thermal-neutron share is confirmed. NTT and Hokkaido found that low-energy neutrons cause 1/5 to 1/4 of errors, and that water cooling raises the thermal-neutron count (https://theregister.com/2023/03/17/neutrons_cause_soft_errors_electronics). They proposed modelling, not a product. Thermal-neutron shield patents already exist (https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8450707). So the liner idea is a commodity materials fix: a 20 to 30% cut in an already small tax, with nothing defensible to own.

3. Novelty. I found no startup selling radiation-aware monitoring, shielding or attribution for AI clusters. One search for cosmic-ray, data-centre and SDC startups turned up only academic work and the 2008 Sun SDC panel (https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/dsn2008sdcpanelsunmicrosystems/1689148). The NVIDIA story on the ChipIr site (https://isis.stfc.ac.uk/Pages/NVIDIAusesChipIrtoreduceerrorsinGPUsystems.aspx; the page I fetched gave no details) shows chip vendors already beam-test GPUs themselves. The empty field fits the "too small to matter" explanation better than "missed". The gap has been named repeatedly in the literature (e.g. the exascale flux paper https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07770). Meta has also given conference talks on silent errors in LLM training (https://atscaleconference.com/videos/silent-errors-in-large-scale-llm-training-challenges-and-lessons-learned/).

4. Flux-aware checkpointing. The best checkpoint interval scales with the square root of MTBF (Young/Daly), so a 10% swing in flux changes the interval by about 5%, which saves a negligible amount. Barometric forecasting removes the need for a sensor. I confirm both points.

5. Path to $300M a year. There is none without turning into a general GPU fleet-reliability company. That space is already contested by NVIDIA DCGM, proteanTecs, Synopsys SLM and the hyperscalers' in-house SRE teams, and the dominant failure modes there (faulty GPUs, HBM defects, mercurial cores) are not caused by radiation.

The only part that survives is a possible 8-week measurement deliverable: a high-altitude flux and thermal-neutron model for Cheyenne-class campuses. That is consulting-scale work, not a $300M company.

Revised ceiling: About $30 to 100M a year at most, for a niche product (thermal-neutron liners for liquid-cooled racks plus transient-versus-permanent fault attribution, sold to about 15 to 20 buyers). More realistically it is a feature or consulting line folded into existing fleet-reliability tools. It cannot credibly pass $300M a year without pivoting into crowded general GPU reliability.

## phosphogypsum: investigator killed, skeptic killed

**Thesis.** The pitch: Florida's roughly 1 Bt of phosphogypsum (PG) stacks, growing about 30 Mt/yr, carries 20-30 pCi/g Ra-226. Strip the radium, recover rare earths on the side, and sell the clean gypsum into the roughly 13 Mst/yr of FGD gypsum that US wallboard uses today, a supply that shrinks as coal plants retire. It fails on three counts. (1) Price: the material it would displace is a $13/t commodity. US crude gypsum sells at $13/t f.o.b. mine (USGS MCS 2026), and seaborne imports land at about $30/t. ICL's own target for radium removal was $10-20/t of PG, and no process has shown that cost at scale. (2) Regulation: putting PG in indoor wallboard needs a case-by-case EPA approval under 40 CFR 61.206, which EPA has never given for any indoor use. It withdrew the 2020 road approval in 2021, and even the 2024 Mosaic private-road pilot was sued. Wallboard makers will not accept the 'radioactive waste drywall' label after the Chinese-drywall scandal. (3) Market size: the whole FGD-to-wallboard gap is about 12 Mt, worth about $300-350M/yr at delivered import parity even if PG captured all of it. Quarries ($13/t, with large US reserves), imports (6.8 Mt/yr already) and wallboard plants moving next to quarries will take most of that gap. Rare earths add only about $2-5/t. Many groups have already worked on this (FIPR/CMI, ORNL, Technion, ICL, Carbon Cycle, OCP/UM6P, Prayon-Technophos), so it is not an unnamed gap. The one real chemistry insight below is already published.

**Evidence.** SUPPLY/DEMAND: USGS MCS 2026 gypsum (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-gypsum.pdf), 2025 figures: crude production 20 Mt, worth about $260M (so about $13/t f.o.b.); synthetic gypsum used 17 Mt, about 39% of supply; imports 6.8 Mt; consumption 44 Mt; calcined gypsum $62/t; wallboard capacity 34 Bft2 against 26 Bft2 sold. USGS attributes stalled synthetic supply to less coal generation. Global Gypsum on 2024 (https://globalgypsum.com/news/2348-us-gypsum-production-rises-steadily-in-2024): 22 Mt mined, imports 7.4 Mt (17% of 44 Mt). ACAA 2023 survey (https://acaa-usa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/2023-Production-and-Use-Survey-Results-FINAL.pdf): 17.42 Mst of FGD gypsum produced, 13.06 Mst into gypsum panels, 98.4% used, so no FGD surplus is left. The decline is real, but the market is already filling it from quarries and imports at commodity prices. PRICE BENCHMARK: imported gypsum is about $30/t at port and up to $50/t at the plant in India (Global Cement on Carbon Cycle, https://www.globalcement.com/magazine/articles/1118-novel-gypsum-purification-process-could-cut-costs-for-indian-cement-producers). I could not find a US import unit value, so this is a proxy. REGULATION: eCFR 40 CFR 61 Subpart R (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-61/subpart-R). All PG must go in stacks (61.202). Removal is allowed only for outdoor agriculture at 10 pCi/g or below (61.204), or for indoor R&D up to 7,000 lb (61.205). Any other use needs proof that it is 'at least as protective as' a stack (61.206). EPA's page (https://epa.gov/radiation/phosphogypsum) confirms the 2021 withdrawal of the 2020 road approval and the Dec 2024 approval of Mosaic's New Wales private-road pilot. That pilot drew litigation (https://www.wusf.org/text/environment/2025-09-16/epa-mosaic-challenge-lawsuit-against-use-phosphate-road-project). Note that 10 pCi/g is an agriculture limit, not a building-material limit. FGD and natural gypsum run about 1 pCi/g, so a credible wallboard product needs about 1-3 pCi/g, a 10-25x reduction. FEEDSTOCK: central Florida PG is 20-30 pCi/g, north Florida 9-15; about 600 Mt had accumulated by 1994, plus about 30 Mt/yr (https://www.asrs.us/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/0184-Richardson.pdf). Florida has more than 1 Bt (https://goldschmidtabstracts.info/2023/18737.pdf). CHEMISTRY: much of the Ra-226 is not in the CaSO4 lattice. Autoradiography puts hotspots on CaF2 grains carrying Ra/Ba (Goldschmidt 2023, above), and Ra concentrates in the -400 mesh and +35 mesh fractions. FIPR's acid flotation gives 93-94% gypsum at up to 94% recovery, with intermediate fractions under 10 pCi/g (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42461-025-01419-6). So physical classification plus fluorite and fines rejection is the cheap first step, and hot reagent recrystallization handles what is left. Technion, hot reagent slurry: 902 to 93 Bq/kg (about 24 to 2.5 pCi/g), lab only, no cost given; the authors say earlier methods stayed in the lab 'largely due to cost' (https://ena-norm.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/6_3_6_Konstantin-Klover-Purification-of-phosphogypsum-from-226Ra-for-its-further-utilization-in-construction.pdf). Carbon Cycle: a 90-100C ammonium sulfate double-salt (likely koktaite) recrystallization, 500 kg pilot planned, no Ra efficiency published. ORNL/FIPR: H2SO4 leach recovers about 43% of REE, and elutriation removes 99% of P2O5, 70% of U and 83% of F (https://www.ornl.gov/publication/rare-earths-recovery-and-gypsum-upgrade-florida-phosphogypsum). COST TARGET: ICL's 2023 challenge sought to cut 700-1,200 Bq/kg to under 400 Bq/kg at $10-20/t of PG for 0.3-3 Mt/yr, TRL 4-6, with a $30k prize (https://www.innocentive.com/challenges/innovative-approaches-to-reducing-radium-226-contamination-in-phosphogypsum-waste-stream/). Even ICL's target (about 10 pCi/g) is not wallboard grade, and its cost ceiling already matches the price of quarried gypsum.

**Market ceiling.** Bottom-up for the US: FGD gypsum into wallboard was 13.06 Mst (about 11.8 Mt) in 2023 (ACAA). The named buyers are USG, National Gypsum, CertainTeed/Saint-Gobain, Georgia-Pacific, American Gypsum/Eagle, Continental Building Products/Saint-Gobain and PABCO. Generous case: FGD falls about 50% by 2035, a 6 Mt gap. PG takes all of it at $25-35/t delivered (import parity, since quarry gypsum is $13/t f.o.b.). That is $150-210M/yr. Rare earths add about $2-5/t, since Florida PG holds only a few hundred ppm of mostly light REE and recovery is 43-90%: another $12-30M. Total is about $160-240M/yr of revenue for the whole industry at commodity margins. Realistic case: only the Southeast coastal plants can take Florida PG without killing freight economics, about 3-5 Mt, so $75-175M. Upside levers: a stack-closure or tipping fee from Mosaic or Nutrien for removing material (Mosaic carries billions in asset retirement obligations, but spread over about 1 Bt that is only about $1-3/t), plus cement set-retarder and agricultural gypsum sales (low value). Abroad (India and Indian cement imports at $30-50/t; OCP's 100% PG valorization target for 2037-40) the volume is bigger but local players own it. Path to $300M/yr: not credible at commodity gypsum prices. You would need about 10 Mt/yr at $30/t with zero competition from quarries.

**Prior art.** FIPR Institute at Florida Poly with DOE's Critical Materials Innovation Hub (Patrick Zhang): radionuclide reduction, REE recovery above 90%, PG-based cement and fertilizer, flowsheets tested at PNNL (https://www.ameslab.gov/cmi/cmi-project-total-utilization-of-phosphogypsum-waste-ree-fertilizers-construction-materials ; https://techconnectworld.com/World2025/a.php?i=159). FIPR acid flotation purification (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42461-025-01419-6). ORNL/FIPR REE and gypsum upgrade (https://www.ornl.gov/publication/rare-earths-recovery-and-gypsum-upgrade-florida-phosphogypsum). Technion, Kovler: Ra purification for construction use (ENA-NORM link above). ICL's open Ra-reduction challenge (InnoCentive link above). Carbon Cycle (UK): patented ammonium sulfate recrystallization of PG into high-grade gypsum for plasterboard and cement, with Nd/Pr recovery; seeking pilot funding (https://www.chemistryworld.com/news/carbon-cycles-gypsum-purification-process-cleans-up-radioactive-fertiliser-waste/4010670.article). OCP/UM6P: aims for 100% PG valorization by 2037-40 through sulfur recovery, roads, set retarder and REE (https://en.7news.ma/ocp-phosphogypsum-recovery/). Prayon/UM6P Technophos (former EcoPhos process): HCl route making 'EcoGypsum', at pilot scale of 200-500 kg/h (https://ureaknowhow.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/2025-Prayon-Ecophos-process.pdf). Mosaic: EPA-approved PG road pilot (https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2025-12/epa-notification-of-small-scale-pilot-projecct-pg-sampling-results-9-9-25.pdf). There are also decades of patents, e.g. US4388292 and US4421731 (https://patents.google.com/patent/US4421731/en). Arianne Phosphate (Quebec igneous apatite) is not relevant to Florida stacks.

**Eight-week proof.** A small team could measure the following in 8 weeks, but it would not rescue the business. Get 50 kg of central Florida PG (FIPR has samples). Size-classify it, reject the fluorite and fines, then recrystallize in hot ammonium sulfate. Gamma-spectrometry gives Ra-226 by fraction: the target is 3 pCi/g or below at 85% mass yield or better, with REE in the leachate measured by ICP-MS. Build a TEA showing all-in cost per tonne against $13/t quarry and $30/t import parity. Then try for an LOI from a Southeast wallboard plant (USG, National Gypsum, CertainTeed) and an informal EPA Region 4 / ORIA read on a 61.206 indoor-use petition. My expectation: the chemistry hits 2-5 pCi/g, the cost lands at $20-40/t, and wallboard makers refuse a PG LOI because of liability and labeling. The result would be a negative proof.

**Killer risk.** This is a $13/t commodity with an indoor-use regulatory wall. Wallboard grade needs about 1-3 pCi/g, a 10-25x reduction. Every published route costs at least what quarried gypsum sells for ($13/t f.o.b.) once you add drying and freight. EPA has never approved indoor use of PG under 61.206 and is being sued over a single private road pilot. Wallboard makers can replace lost FGD supply with quarries and imports they already use (6.8 Mt/yr imported), without taking on reputational risk from 'radioactive drywall'. The rare-earth credit (a few dollars per tonne) is too small to change the math. Many groups have already worked on this, so it is not a missed gap.

**Skeptic.** I tried to overturn the investigator's kill and could not. Its main numbers hold up, and the regulatory news since then helps only outdoor uses.

(1) Price. USGS MCS 2026 puts crude gypsum at $13/t f.o.b. mine in 2025, up from $12 in 2024. Imports were 6.8 Mt (Spain 38%, Mexico 30%, Canada 28%). (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-gypsum.pdf)

(2) The supply gap is closing more slowly than the pitch needs. USGS estimates 2025 synthetic gypsum at about 17 Mt and calls it "relatively flat" despite coal retirements. Wallboard sales fell from 27.2 to 26 billion sq ft. So the FGD shortfall PG is meant to fill is growing slowly, against a shrinking wallboard market. That makes the investigator's 50%-by-2035 FGD decline look generous. (same USGS source)

(3) Regulation is the only place I found movement. On Aug 12, 2026 the Eleventh Circuit denied CBD's petition and upheld EPA's approval of Mosaic's PG road pilot, ruling that Subpart R allows "other purpose" approvals and that EPA's risk method was reasonable (https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/files/202510515.pdf). Separately, EPA proposed on Apr 24, 2026 to deny CBD's petition to regulate PG as RCRA hazardous waste (https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08097.pdf). Both point to looser outdoor and road uses. Neither creates a path for indoor use: 40 CFR 61.206 approval is still case by case (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-61/subpart-R/section-61.206), and EPA has still not approved any indoor use.

(4) Novelty. My searches turned up the same crowd of academic, government, patent and incumbent work: decades of US radium-removal patents (US4421731, US4388292, US4282192, US4328193), Chinese patents on purifying PG for building materials (CN117819849B), Penn State's REE-from-PG work, and FIPR/CMI coproduct papers at TechConnect 2025 and 2026 (https://techconnectworld.com/World2026/a.php?i=143 ; https://www.psu.edu/news/engineering/story/engineers-sustainably-mine-rare-earth-elements-fertilizer-byproduct). This gap is well known, not unnamed.

(5) Size. The bottom-up math holds. At most about 12 Mt of FGD-to-wallboard volume, priced at $13/t quarry to roughly $30/t delivered import, caps the whole industry near $150-350M/yr even if PG takes all of it. Radium removal at the ICL target of $10-20/t would use up most of that margin.

Revised ceiling: About $75-240M/yr of revenue for the whole industry at commodity margins, assuming indoor PG were ever approved, which no rule allows. A single startup would realistically get a fraction of that. There is no credible path to $300M/yr.

## sterile-insects: investigator killed, skeptic killed

**Thesis.** The pitch would be a private company that rears and irradiates sterile insects as a service. It would start with the New World screwworm (NWS) emergency, where USDA puts eradication demand at 500-700M sterile flies a week against roughly 200M a week of current supply, and then move into fruit-fly and mosquito programmes. The shortage is real, but it is short-lived and the government is already closing it. Metapa in Mexico (100M a week) opened in June 2026. The US-government-owned Moore Air Base plant in Texas ($750M, contract awarded to Mortenson, up to 300M a week) reaches initial capability in November 2027. That gives about 500M a week by 2028, enough to close the gap just as a startup could have plants built. The 'irradiator bottleneck' is not real. Co-60 works, and the National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA) with Texas A&M (e-beam) and the University of Florida (X-ray) are already giving USDA cobalt-free options, with e-beam claimed to sterilise 100M flies in 24 hours. The real constraints are rearing, larval diet and labour, and USDA is solving those in-house. Every paying customer is a government agency that runs its own plants. My bottom-up estimate of total worldwide sterile-insect spending is about $0.4-0.7B a year, and most of that will never be outsourced. So there is no credible route to $300M+ a year of private revenue.

**Evidence.** SCREWWORM SUPPLY AND DEMAND: According to USDA APHIS, COPEG in Panama makes about 100M sterile flies a week. Metapa in Mexico was finished in late June 2026 for 100M a week at full capacity, after USDA put $21M into converting it from a fruit-fly plant. Moore Air Base in Texas will make 300M a week and will be the only US production site. Dispersal centres are running at Tuxtla, Tampico and Edinburg (https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animals/animal-health/livestock-and-poultry-disease/stop-screwworm/sterile-fly-production-dispersal). The Texas plant costs $750M, starts at 100M a week in November 2027 and scales to 300M a week (https://www.beefmagazine.com/livestock-management/ground-broken-for-sterile-fly-production-facility-in-texas). A Minnesota firm, apparently Mortenson, holds a $610M contract to build it (https://www.feedstrategy.com/animal-health-veterinary/news/15819227/construction-firm-contracted-to-build-sterile-fly-production-facility-to-fight-nws). Eradication needs 500-700M flies a week. Emergency funding stands at $1.3B, the first Texas case was confirmed in early June 2026, and USDA screwworm staff grew from 10 to more than 120 (https://www.hppr.org/hppr-news/2026-06-26/as-new-world-screwworm-reaches-texas-trump-officials-race-to-breed-more-sterile-flies). Mexico has had more than 30,000 animal cases, and the Metapa plant was a $50M US-Mexico project (https://www.thecattlesite.com/news/mexico-and-us-open-screwworm-fly-plant-in-chiapas).
ECONOMIC STAKES: Losses from the 1976 Texas outbreak were $732M, about $1.8B in today's dollars. APHIS says the barrier saves the cattle industry $2.3B a year. Mexico supplies about 1.25M head of imported cattle a year. Emergency funding was $109.8M in 2023 and $165M in 2024 (https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-06-02_IN12558_247c11dc058a0f50011884b14e67bedd0beb25a5.html). A Texas A&M estimate puts the cost of a Texas outbreak at $1.8B (https://www.hppr.org/hppr-news/2026-06-12/weeks-before-texas-screwworm-cases-state-lawmakers-were-warned-of-devastating-consequences). Before the outbreak, COPEG cost about $15M a year at roughly 20M flies a week (https://jalopnik.com/the-u-s-spends-15m-every-year-to-airdrop-worms-on-cen-1851526031).
IRRADIATION: Co-60 is the main workhorse. E-beam (NNSA and Texas A&M) and X-ray (NNSA, USDA and the University of Florida) are being developed as cobalt-free options. Most Co-60 comes from reactors in Canada, Argentina and China (https://www.ans.org/nuclear/screwworm/). E-beam is claimed to sterilise more than 100M flies in 24 hours, against about a week with Co-60 (https://www.drovers.com/news/industry/new-technology-combat-new-world-screwworm). USDA is funding Texas A&M's e-beam work and the University of Florida's tests of two US-made X-ray irradiators. The Grand Challenge put $105M into 40 projects out of 226 applications that asked for $664M (https://www.aphis.usda.gov/news/agency-announcements/usda-invests-projects-strengthen-new-world-screwworm-preparedness). The irradiation industry's own June 2026 deck describes Co-60 as standard for full-scale plants, with Guatemala treating 2B medflies (20 tonnes) a week (https://iiaglobal.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Advancing-SteriIe-Insect-Technique-Using-Co-60-June-2026.pdf).
FRUIT FLY ECONOMICS: California's medfly preventive release programme costs about $16M a year over 1,750 square miles (https://www.cdfa.ca.gov/plant/PDEP/prpinfo/). A USDA economic analysis models a 180M-a-week oriental fruit fly programme at $16.08M a year (rearing $7.38M, eclosion $4.54M, release $3.4M), which works out to about $1,700 per million flies delivered. The same analysis says the programme is not economically justified at current incursion levels (https://aphis.prod.usda.gov/sites/default/files/oriental-ff-sterile-insect-technique-economic-analysis-2021.pdf).
USDA'S REAL BOTTLENECK: USDA issued a request for information on larval and adult diet ingredients for the Edinburg plant, closing June 12, 2026 (https://fundinglandscape.com/opportunity/cmqg8o4fd006qju04qfn0kudf). That points to inputs and rearing as the constraint, not irradiation.

**Market ceiling.** Bottom-up estimate of what is actually paid for sterile insects worldwide. (1) Screwworm: getting to 500-700M flies a week takes about $1.3B of emergency money plus $750M of capital spread over several years. Running cost in steady state is my estimate of about $150-300M a year, at roughly $5-15k per million flies based on COPEG's history. After eradication, demand drops back to barrier maintenance, historically about $15-60M a year. The buyers are USDA APHIS, COPEG and SENASICA, and all three run their own plants. (2) Fruit flies: California's medfly programme is about $16M a year. Guatemala/Mexico Moscamed, Spain, Israel, South Africa and Australia are each in the range of $5-50M. Altogether this is about $100-200M a year, mostly in government plants. (3) Codling moth and other moths: tens of millions. (4) Mosquito SIT: pilots funded by the IAEA and by city governments, probably under $50M a year. Total worldwide is about $0.4-0.7B a year, and perhaps 10-20% of it is open to contractors. A private company would need about half of all worldwide sterile-insect spending to reach $300M a year, and the biggest piece, US screwworm production, is government-owned and contracted only through construction. Realistic private ceiling: a $20-80M a year contract-rearing, diet-supply or equipment business.

**Prior art.** Government plants: COPEG in Panama, Metapa in Mexico (SENASICA/USDA), Moore Air Base in Texas (USDA/USACE, built by Mortenson), Moscamed El Pino in Guatemala, and the Hawaii and Los Alamitos plants. Genetic alternatives to radiation competing for the same budgets: Flyttr (Precigen, more than $65M raised, partnered with Texas Biomed) and Agragene (more than $5M), both at https://sciencenews.org/investors-lab/screwworm-neutralizer-insect-sterilization, plus NovoFly, a male-only strain in field tests (https://www.hppr.org/hppr-news/2026-06-26/as-new-world-screwworm-reaches-texas-trump-officials-race-to-breed-more-sterile-flies). Small private sterile-insect firms: M3 Agriculture Technologies (codling moth and navel orangeworm, about $700k a year in revenue) per the same Science News piece; I also believe Bioflytech (Spain) and Biofeed (Israel) sell medfly sterile insects, though I did not check those two. Irradiation: Nordion for Co-60; e-beam from NNSA, Texas A&M and the National Center for Electron Beam Research; X-ray irradiators tested by the University of Florida (https://www.ans.org/nuclear/screwworm/). The IAEA/FAO Joint Division backs mosquito sterile-insect work worldwide.

**Eight-week proof.** Only a reduced version can be tested in 8 weeks, and it is a services business, not a $300M company. (a) A cost model per million flies for screwworm, comparing Co-60, e-beam and X-ray, built from the USDA oriental fruit fly cost structure and COPEG figures. (b) Calls with 10-15 buyers (APHIS screwworm programme, SENASICA, the Texas Animal Health Commission, Texas and Southwestern Cattle Raisers, Brazil's MAPA) to test whether any would pay a private supplier for flies or a modular rearing unit before Moore Air Base reaches 300M a week. (c) A letter of intent from Mexico or a Central American agency for contract rearing. The likely result, given that USDA owns and runs every plant and is closing the gap by 2028, is that nobody signs.

**Killer risk.** There is one buyer, the government, and it insources. USDA, COPEG and SENASICA own and run every screwworm plant. The $750M Texas plant is government-owned, with construction contracted to Mortenson, and once it is running from November 2027 onward, US supply plus Metapa plus COPEG reaches about 500M flies a week. Demand then falls to barrier maintenance after eradication. Irradiation is solved with public money through NNSA, Texas A&M and the University of Florida. Male-only genetic strains (NovoFly, Flyttr, Agragene) could halve the number of flies needed. The total worldwide market, about $0.4-0.7B a year, is too small for one private company to make $300M.

**Skeptic.** I tried to refute both the novelty and the size findings. Neither refutation worked, and the case for killing the idea is a bit stronger than the investigator made it.

1) The investigator's timeline is too optimistic about supply, which makes the gap look shorter than it is. HPPR (https://www.hppr.org/hppr-news/2026-06-26/as-new-world-screwworm-reaches-texas-trump-officials-race-to-breed-more-sterile-flies) gives these figures:
- Panama (COPEG) produces about 100M flies a week, not 200M.
- Metapa expected only about 9M a week by the end of July 2026, against a planned peak of 100M.
- Moore Air Base opens at 100M a week in late 2027 and only later ramps up to 300M.

AgNavigator (https://www.agnavigator.com/Article/2026/03/11/usda-prepares-for-new-world-screwworm-threat-with-texas-facility/) also gives Moore's capacity as 100M a week. So supply in 2028 is more like 300M a week than 500M, and a 200-400M a week gap could last until about 2029-2030.

That is the strongest point for the thesis, but it does not save it:
- The gap is still paid for entirely with government emergency money: $1.3B, plus the $105M Grand Challenge across 40 projects (https://www.drovers.com/news/usda-awards-105-million-grand-challenge-funding-eradicate-new-world-screwworm). None of the sources I found give fly production to a private company. The only contractors are the builder (Mortenson, through the Army Corps of Engineers) and research grants such as e-beam at Texas A&M and X-ray at the University of Florida.
- A new private plant would also take about 2 or more years to permit and ramp up, so it would arrive about when Moore does.
- Once eradication succeeds, demand falls back to maintaining a barrier.

2) Novelty is low, possibly lower than 2/10. Selling reared insects privately as a service already exists at scale in mosquitoes:
- Oxitec, a private company, has built a factory in Campinas, Brazil, with capacity for 190M mosquito eggs a week, enough to cover about 100M people a year. It is waiting on approval from Brazil's health regulator, Anvisa (https://olhardigital.com.br/2025/10/05/medicina-e-saude/brasil-ganha-segunda-fabrica-de-mosquitos-para-combater-dengue/).
- Wolbito do Brasil already runs a factory at 100M eggs a week (same source).
- Singapore's environment agency (NEA) contracts out automation and sex-sorting for its mosquito programme to Orinno Technology and Verily, but runs production itself at 2-5M males a week (https://www.nea.gov.sg/corporate-functions/resources/research/environmental_health_institute/wolbachia-aedes-mosquito-suppression-strategy/automation-and-partnerships-to-scale-capacity).

So the 'move into mosquitoes' step would mean competing with Oxitec and the World Mosquito Program, which are funded and already a step ahead.

One correction to the investigator: Bioflytech (Spain) makes insect protein and ingredients, not sterile medflies (https://petfoodprocessing.net/articles/18073-bioflytech-pioneering-insect-ingredients-in-spain). As I understand it, Spain's medfly sterile-insect programme is run by the state company TRAGSA (the CTIFL page I found covers Spain's programme but I did not open it: https://www.ctifl.fr/en/application-de-la-tis-pour-la-gestion-de-la-mouche-mediterraneenne-ceratitis-capitata-en-espagne).

3) Size: I found no source that pushes worldwide sterile-insect spending meaningfully above about $0.5-1B a year. Oxitec's revenue is not public. Singapore does not publish a budget for its programme.

The only route to $300M+ would be a contract to run Moore (government-owned, contractor-operated) or COPEG. Nothing I found says operations will be contracted out. If they are, large government contractors would be the likely bidders, not a startup; that last point is my inference and is not sourced.

Doable in 8 weeks? A team could build a cost model or a prototype for rearing or diet, but it could not get an LOI from a buyer for fly production, because there is no buyer outside government.

Revised ceiling: About $20-80M a year, as a niche supplier of contract rearing, larval diet, automation (the Orinno/Verily model) or irradiation equipment. A temporary bump during the screwworm emergency is possible only if USDA chooses to contract out production, and I found no sign that it will.

## low-alpha-packaging: investigator killed, skeptic killed

**Thesis.** The hypothesis was that HBM4/4E stacks of 12 to 16 dies, plus AI-scale fleets, would make alpha emitters in packaging a soft-error problem big enough to support a new materials or metrology company. It doesn't hold up, for four reasons. (1) The money is too small. Ultra-low-alpha (ULA) tin is plated in milligram amounts per HBM stack. My rough bottom-up estimate is a few tonnes per year for all HBM, and probably tens to low hundreds of tonnes for all flip-chip bumping. The premium over commodity tin is therefore a pool of roughly $10-50M a year, not billions. Analyst figures for the 'ultra-low alpha metal market' disagree by about 400x ($4.7M in 2024 vs $1.85B in 2025), so neither is a sound basis. (2) The suppliers are entrenched and already sell below spec: JX Advanced Metals, Mitsubishi Materials, Solstice (ex-Honeywell), MacDermid Alpha, Indium, Senju, Pure Technologies and Furuchi. MacDermid Alpha relaunched a <0.002 cts/khr-cm2 tin line in March 2025 and pitches it directly at HBM and AI. (3) The technology trend shrinks the problem. Hybrid bonding replaces solder microbumps between dies with Cu-Cu bonds, removing the main tin alpha source inside the stack. On-die ECC plus system ECC already absorbs most alpha-induced bit flips. (4) Metrology is a small instrument niche. XIA's UltraLo-1800 already got multi-site variation down to counting statistics. No piece here plausibly gets a startup to $300M/yr.

**Evidence.** - Suppliers already market ULA tin for HBM and AI: MacDermid Alpha (Element Solutions) launched a Low Alpha Tin portfolio on 24 Mar 2025: <0.002 cts/khr-cm2, >99.99% Sn, <10 ppm trace metals, Sn-MSA plus soluble anodes, aimed at DRAM/AI/2.5D/3D. Sources: https://3dincites.com/?p=108548 , https://semiconductor-digest.com/?p=30444 , https://www.macdermidalpha.com/news/mitigating-soft-errors-advanced-packaging (names HBM at >1 TB/s as especially vulnerable and cites JEDEC JESD89A SER testing), https://www.3dincites.com/?p=110772 . Every batch is already checked by alpha spectrometry, so the spec is a solved commodity.
- Market sizes disagree badly. Transparency Market Research: ULA metal $2.53M (2015) growing to $4.72M by 2024 (https://prnewswire.co.uk/news-releases/ultra-low-alpha-metal-market-estimated-to-reach-us-472-mn-by-2024-transparency-market-research-613690453.html). Spherical Insights: $1.85B in 2025 growing to $3.74B by 2035 at 7.3% CAGR, top players JX, Mitsui Kinzoku, Indium, MacDermid Alpha, Senju (https://www.sphericalinsights.com/blogs/top-25-companies-in-global-ultra-low-alpha-metal-market-2026-2035-expert-view-by-spherical-insights). The billion-dollar figure almost certainly counts total high-purity metal or solder revenue, not the low-alpha premium.
- Rough volume check (my own estimate, not sourced). Assume about 1e5 microbumps per die, 12 dies, and a SnAg cap about 20 um across by 10 um tall. That is about 4e-3 cm3, or about 30 mg Sn per stack. HBM revenue was about $54.6B in 2026 (https://siliconanalysts.com/analysis/hbm4-market-share-race-2026), which I take as roughly 1e8 stacks. That gives a few tonnes of Sn for all HBM, even after plating overhead.
- The tin inside the stack is on its way out. TrendForce (7 Jul 2026) says Samsung and SK hynix pushed hybrid bonding to 16-high HBM4E at the earliest, because JEDEC is relaxing stack height (720 um for HBM3E, 775 um for HBM4, possibly 900-1000 um next) (https://www.trendforce.com/news/2026/07/07/news-samsung-sk-hynix-reportedly-reconsider-hybrid-bonding-timeline-16-high-hbm4e-may-be-earliest-adoption/ ; https://bits-chips.com/article/besi-faces-longer-wait-as-sk-hynix-pushes-hybrid-bonding-to-hbm5/). So solder microbumps (TC-NCF/MR-MUF) stay for a few more generations, still served by today's ULA tin. Once hybrid bonding arrives, the tin between dies goes away.
- The pain at fleet level is real but probably not alpha. Meta's Llama 3 run (16,384 H100s, 54 days) had 419 interruptions; 72 (17.2%) were HBM3 and 148 (30.1%) were GPU (https://www.sdxcentral.com/news/meta-report-details-hundreds-of-gpu-and-hbm3-related-interruptions-to-llama-3-training-run/). Nobody has publicly attributed these to alpha soft errors. Those are counted interruptions, which points to uncorrectable or hard faults; ECC-corrected alpha flips would not stop a job. Nothing public ties fleet failures to package alpha emission.
- Metrology is already good enough. XIA UltraLo-1800 has background suppression to about 0.0001 alpha/cm2/hr (about 50x better than conventional counters), 707 or 1800 cm2 electrodes, and energy resolution for isotope ID (https://xia.com/products/ultralo-1800/). A round-robin across sites found low-alpha variation came down to counting statistics only, with ULA variation down 3x and the remainder from cosmogenic background (https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.1845v1).
- The physics is well understood. Pb-210 has a 22-year half-life; Po-210 a 138-day half-life and a 5.3 MeV alpha; emission rises for about 28 months after smelting. Specs already sit near 0.0015/cm2/hr (Technic: https://www.technic.com/sites/default/files/resources/Low%20Alpha%20Lead%20Wafer%20Bump%20Applications_0.pdf). Lead-free SnAg and the ingrowth problem were handled about 15 years ago.

**Market ceiling.** This is a rough bottom-up estimate. Most of the per-unit figures below are my own assumptions, not sourced numbers.
(a) ULA tin and solder premium: roughly $10-50M/yr worldwide. Basis: perhaps tens to about 200 t/yr of low-alpha Sn for wafer bumping (HBM, logic C4 and microbumps, FCBGA), at a premium of about $100-500/kg over commodity tin. A new entrant would have to displace JX, Mitsubishi, Solstice, MacDermid Alpha or Indium in plating chemistries that are qualified per fab and per product, which takes 12-24 months each. A realistic share of a few percent means low single-digit $M.
(b) Low-alpha spherical silica filler for EMC/MUF/underfill: this is the largest alpha-related material pool, probably low hundreds of $M. It is owned by Denka, Admatechs (Toyota Tsusho) and Nippon Steel's Micron Co. Making low-alpha silica is a mineral and furnace business with high capex and long qualification, and the low-alpha grade is a small slice of it.
(c) Alpha-counting instruments and testing: buyers are perhaps 100-300 fabs, OSATs, material suppliers and labs. Instruments sell in the low hundreds of $k, and outsourced testing costs about $1-3k per sample. The total is plausibly under $30M/yr.
(d) SER characterization and modeling services for HBM vendors (SK hynix, Samsung, Micron) and accelerator makers (NVIDIA, AMD, Google, AWS, Meta): this is consulting-scale, $1-10M contracts at most, and these companies do it in-house with JESD89A beam testing.
Added together, every alpha-related slice stays well under $300M/yr for any single new entrant.

**Prior art.** - ULA metals: JX Advanced Metals, Mitsubishi Materials, Mitsui Mining & Smelting, Solstice Advanced Materials (ex-Honeywell; the Honeywell low-alpha page now redirects to https://www.solstice.com/), MacDermid Alpha (https://www.macdermidalpha.com/products/wafer-level-packaging/advanced-interconnect-metallization/low-alpha-materials), Indium Corp, Senju Metal, Pure Technologies, Furuchi (https://wwwcdn.furuchi.co.jp/english/products/metal/low_a_tin.html), Technic (low-alpha Pb).
- Low-alpha fillers: Denka, Admatechs (Admafine; https://www.specialchem.com/polymer-additives/product/toyotsu-chemiplas-admafine-so-e1), Micron Co; long patent history (e.g., US4816299 ultra-pure fused-silica fillers, https://patents.google.com/patent/US4816299).
- Metrology: XIA UltraLo-1800 (https://xia.com/products/ultralo-1800/), ICP-MS for U/Th/Pb-210 at commercial labs, IBM and others running alpha round-robins (https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.1845).
- SER modeling: academic and patent work on 3D-IC and DRAM alpha SER (https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3048629/ ; https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/modeling-of-alpha-particle-induced-soft-error-rate-in-dram/), plus JEDEC JESD89A and JESD221.
- The 'gap' has been named and served for more than 20 years. It is not overlooked.

**Eight-week proof.** If someone wanted to falsify the kill quickly, the cheapest test is a field-attribution study. Take public or partner HBM error logs (correctable/uncorrectable ECC events by stack position and age) and test whether CE rates follow die position next to microbump or MUF layers, and Po-210 ingrowth timing (rising for about 28 months after smelting), rather than altitude-dependent neutron flux. Add 3-4 UltraLo measurements of decapped HBM packages ($5-15k of outsourced counting). If alpha caused a material share of uncorrectable errors, that would reopen a niche in diagnostics or screening. It still would not reach $300M/yr. Getting LOIs within 8 weeks is unrealistic: materials qualification at SK hynix, Samsung and Micron takes 12-24 months.

**Killer risk.** There is no independent pool of value. ULA tin is a qualified, multi-sourced commodity already shipping below spec (<0.002 cts/khr-cm2), worth tens of $M in premium. The alpha-relevant solder inside HBM stacks disappears once hybrid bonding arrives (16-high HBM4E or HBM5). ECC hides alpha flips, and fleet-level HBM failures are not attributed to package alpha. Fillers belong to Japanese mineral incumbents with capex and qualification moats, and metrology is a sub-$30M niche that XIA has already solved.

**Skeptic.** I tried to overturn the kill and could not, on either novelty or size. Two of the investigator's premises need correcting, but neither saves the thesis.

(1) Novelty: the gap is already claimed, and in the thesis's own words. MacDermid Alpha's 2025 note 'Mitigating Soft Errors in Advanced Packaging' (https://www.macdermidalpha.com/news/mitigating-soft-errors-advanced-packaging), carried by 3D InCites (https://www.3dincites.com/?p=110772), argues that HBM stacks are especially exposed to alpha soft errors because they are compact and densely interconnected. It sells MICROFAB SN 002 A and NOVAFAB Sn pellets at <0.002 cts/khr-cm2, verifies every batch by alpha spectrometry, and pitches the line at memory, AI and HPC (https://www.macdermidalpha.com/products/wafer-level-packaging/advanced-interconnect-metallization/low-alpha-materials). The incumbent's marketing already states the 'HBM plus AI makes alpha matter' idea word for word.

(2) Size: my own sanity check is even smaller than the investigator's. Assume a microbump about 25 um across with a solder cap about 10 um tall, roughly 4e-8 g of Sn per bump. Assume about 10k bumps per die interface and 12 interfaces, which gives about 4 mg of Sn per stack. At 100-200M stacks a year, that is about 1 t/yr of ULA Sn for all HBM microbumps (my assumptions, not sourced numbers). Even with a 10x error and all logic C4 bumping added, the premium pool stays in the tens of $M. Published market sizes swing from a few $M to a projected $5.4B by 2028 (https://prnewswire.com:443/news-releases/the-worldwide-ultra-low-alpha-metals-industry-is-expected-to-reach-5-4-billion-by-2028--301460346.html), so none of them are usable.

(3) Correction to the investigator: HBM4 kept microbumps and postponed hybrid bonding (https://semiengineering.com/tag/hbm/page/4/). So 'hybrid bonding removes the tin' only applies from HBM4E/HBM5 onward. That delays the shrinkage by one generation. It does not change the volume math.

(4) The demand signal is real but points elsewhere. On the Delta A100/H100 fleet, H100 memory MTBE per GPU was 3.2x worse (88,768 h vs 283,271 h), with 24 uncorrectable errors on H100s, eight row-remapping failures, and recovery that worked 92% of the time (https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11901v4). The authors blame capacity, lower HBM3 voltage, stack count and heat. They do not attribute the errors to alpha, or even separate soft from hard faults. Any startup opening in HBM reliability lies in fleet RAS, error telemetry and spare-row management, not in alpha materials or metrology.

(5) An 8-week demo is possible, for example an alpha-SER model of a 16-high stack. But the buyers are SK hynix, Samsung and Micron, who already do JESD89A testing in-house, and NVIDIA. That is a consulting ceiling, not a path to $300M/yr.

Revised ceiling: An alpha-specific materials or metrology entrant could plausibly reach low single-digit $M a year. The most optimistic case, a SER modeling, testing and consulting business, is perhaps $5-20M a year. Nothing like $300M a year. The adjacent HBM fleet-reliability and RAS software angle would be a different hypothesis that needs its own test.

## neutron-scrap-copper: investigator killed, skeptic killed

**Thesis.** The pitch is to use neutrons (PGNAA or pulsed neutrons) to measure copper through the full depth of shredded steel on a belt, then sort it, so shredders can sell low-copper shred to EAF flat-steel mills at a premium. The problem is that this is not new. Thermo Gamma-Metrics' CrossBelt Recycled Metal analyzer has done exactly this since 2001, sold into recycling by Gamma-Tech LLC. River Metals and Morris Recycling sold "Gamma Shred" at 200–400 t/h, there were 9 units by 2006 and 15 by 2008, and the device costs about $1–3 per ton processed. A patent on sorting shred in bulk increments by PGNAA reading (Shulman, US7886915, priority 2008) has already expired. A 2021-priority patent on lowering copper in scrap steel even calibrated against a Gamma-Tech crossbelt PGNAA, and dismissed these analyzers as having "no sorting capabilities" and needing "substantial capital costs". Twenty-five years of a working product staying niche shows the bottleneck is not seeing the copper. It is physically removing copper that sits in discrete pieces (motor armatures, wire), which needs piece-level detection and ejection, not tonne-scale measurement. The sales logic also caps the business: even at 100% of the world's roughly 100–150 Mt/yr of shred, a fee of $1–3/t comes to about $100–450M/yr in theory, and realistic penetration is a small fraction of that.

10) Physics, my own calculation from standard thermal-neutron capture cross sections (Cu about 3.8 b, Fe about 2.6 b). That cannot single out the roughly 1 kg armature that causes the contamination, and diverting a whole 1–5 t increment to catch it wastes far more good steel than piece-level ejection does.

## muon-imaging: investigator killed, skeptic killed

**Thesis.** The pitch would go like this: cosmic-ray muons pass through tens of metres of concrete, steel or rock, so a passive muon detector could become a general NDT (non-destructive testing) and imaging platform for blast furnaces, post-tensioned bridges, cargo, spent-fuel casks and CO2 storage. The evidence goes against it. The gap is not unnamed: at least six funded companies have worked on it for up to 20 years. The best-funded, Decision Sciences, has raised about $187M since 2006 and is now moving into mineral exploration and commodity verification, after cargo screening failed to scale. The underlying problem is physics, not software. Natural muon flux at sea level is only about 1 per cm² per minute (standard PDG figure; I could not extract the exact quote from the PDG review). Useful images therefore take 15 minutes for cargo, about 100 hours for a bridge tendon, and 9 months for a blast furnace. That rules out throughput markets like cargo, where X-ray takes seconds, and leaves slow, bespoke, project-sized jobs. Added up bottom-up, every non-mining segment together comes to roughly $150-450M/yr of spend. No single segment credibly supports more than $300M/yr for one vendor. The only thing that would break the flux limit is artificial muon beams, such as the laser-plasma accelerator work Berkeley Lab and Ideon are doing for ARPA-E. That is a 3-year national-lab research program, not something a small team can show in 8 weeks.

## c14-pharma: investigator killed, skeptic killed

**Thesis.** The hypothesis says the Russian C-14 cutoff leaves a gap, and that CANDU resin waste could fill it and grow into a >$300M/yr business once labelled synthesis is included. Both halves are wrong. (1) The gap has already been found and is being filled. OPG's Western Waste site, CCNuclear and RC-14 (feedstock), ASP Isotopes (enrichment in Pretoria, take-or-pay contract with RC-14 at $2.4-3.8M/yr), Kinectrics (SCEPTER resin stripping) and CNNC are all on it, and OPG plans commercial sales by 2027. (2) The market is too small. A human mass-balance study doses about 4 MBq per subject, roughly 1 mCi of product for a whole study. Total enriched-C-14 demand is therefore tens to a few hundred Ci per year. The only sizing I found (a low-quality syndicated report) puts the whole C-14 market, labelled compounds included, at about $240M in 2025, with Ba14CO3 precursor at about $47M. Supply is not the constraint either: Ontario's reactors alone produce about 200-450 Ci a year, plus decades of stored resin, far more than pharma uses. The real constraint is enrichment, and ASP and RC-14 already hold that position. Reaching $300M/yr would mean taking more than 100% of the whole C-14 market, including the labelled-synthesis services that Pharmaron, Almac, Selcia, Moravek and others own through GMP track records and pharma relationships, not through raw C-14 access.

**Evidence.** - OPG pilot with CCNuclear and its subsidiary RC-14 to harvest C-14 from intermediate-level waste stored at its Bruce County facility. OPG says Russia was historically the sole international supplier and that supply has been constrained since 2022 (https://www.opg.com/story/opg-enters-partnership-to-supply-important-medical-isotope-faced-with-global-shortage).
- Ontario government: OPG at the Western Waste Management Facility plans commercial C-14 sales by 2027, pending a successful trial (https://www.ontario.ca/page/nuclear-isotopes).
- ASP Isotopes has started commercial enriched C-14 production in Pretoria. RC-14 holds a multi-year take-or-pay tolling contract: minimum about $2.4-2.5M/yr, target $3.8M/yr, 2-year term extendable to 10. ASP says it has a strong order book (https://stocktitan.net/news/ASPI/asp-isotopes-inc-commences-commercial-production-of-enriched-carbon-98eopxu1mves.html ; https://www.accessnewswire.com/newsroom/en/chemicals/asp-isotopes-inc.-enters-into-multi-year-supply-agreement-for-highly-enriched-carbon-14-753410 ; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001921865/000147793225001265/aspi_ex991.htm).
- Fera describes CCNuclear harvesting C-14 residue from CANDU reactors worldwide and RC-14 enriching it as a "paradigm shift" away from Mayak (https://content.fera.co.uk/radioisotope-supply).
- Kinectrics SCEPTER strips more than 90% (one paper says more than 99%) of C-14 from spent CANDU resins at ambient conditions. Its main value is reclassifying intermediate-level waste as low-level waste, "hundreds of thousands of dollars" saved per m3, with recovered C-14 available for medical and research use (https://www.kinectrics.com/capabilities/products/radioactive-waste-management-processes/scepter-selective-carbon-extraction-process ; https://proceedings.cns-snc.ca/index.php/pcns/article/view/7578).
- Ontario Hydro estimated its reactors produce about 200-450 Ci/yr of C-14, more than 95% of it in the moderator and mostly captured on ion-exchange columns (https://proceedings.cns-snc.ca/index.php/pcns/article/download/3041/3040/3076).
- Almac: mass-balance studies need about 4 MBq of labelled drug per subject, AMS microdose studies about 10 kBq. Mayak has been the only commercial reactor supplier since NRU stopped in 2009 (https://almacgroup.com/knowledge/wp-content/uploads/sites/10/2021/02/API_Carbon-14-labelled-API-manufacturing_Publication-1.pdf).
- Quotient (now part of Pharmaron) built a C-14 recycling plant, using thermal diffusion to about 80% enrichment. It reported a roughly 5x price rise after Mayak supply was disrupted (https://chemistryworld.com/news/quotient-ups-carbon-14-quota-with-recycling-plant/9523.article).
- Market sizing (low quality, syndicated): whole C-14 market $239.7M in 2025, of which labelled compounds $169M, Ba14CO3 $47M and ADME uses $132M; forecast $372M by 2032 (https://pmarketresearch.com/worldwide-carbon-14-market-research/).
- Korea imported only about 28.6 Ci of C-14 in 2005 (https://kns.org/files/pre_paper/14/426홍선욱.pdf).

**Market ceiling.** Bottom-up. There are roughly 50-150 human ADME/mass-balance studies a year. Each needs about 1 mCi of labelled drug, synthesised from tens to a few hundred mCi of Ba14CO3. Preclinical ADME, agrochemical and cosmetics radiolabelling add more, so total enriched-C-14 demand is plausibly 100-500 Ci/yr. At an assumed post-shortage price of about $20-100k per Ci (unverified; Quotient reported a 5x rise), the precursor market is about $5-50M/yr, consistent with the $47M Ba14CO3 figure. Labelled-compound synthesis is about $170M/yr. A custom GMP C-14 API campaign costs roughly $150-500k; at about 300-600 campaigns a year that comes to about $100-250M. Whole stack: about $240M in 2025, about $370M by 2032 on optimistic forecasts. Getting to $300M/yr would mean owning essentially all of global C-14 supply and all labelled synthesis at the same time, against incumbents Pharmaron, Almac, Selcia, Moravek, ARC, Eurofins and Curachem, and against funded supply-side entrants RC-14/CCNuclear, ASP and CNNC. A realistic outcome for a new entrant: $5-30M/yr.

**Prior art.** - CCNuclear and RC-14 (Canada): harvest C-14 from CANDU waste and enrich it via ASP; partners with OPG (https://www.opg.com/story/opg-enters-partnership-to-supply-important-medical-isotope-faced-with-global-shortage ; https://content.fera.co.uk/radioisotope-supply).
- ASP Isotopes (NASDAQ: ASPI): C-14 enrichment plant in Pretoria (https://stocktitan.net/news/ASPI/asp-isotopes-inc-commences-commercial-production-of-enriched-carbon-98eopxu1mves.html).
- OPG Nuclear Sustainability Services: Western Waste Management Facility pilot, commercial sales targeted for 2027 (https://www.ontario.ca/page/nuclear-isotopes).
- Kinectrics: SCEPTER resin C-14 extraction (https://www.kinectrics.com/capabilities/products/radioactive-waste-management-processes/scepter-selective-carbon-extraction-process).
- Older art: US patent US5286468, producing C-14 from spent resin waste (https://patents.google.com/patent/US5286468), and OSTI work on supercritical-CO2 C-14 removal from resins (https://www.osti.gov/biblio/476561).
- Quotient/Pharmaron: C-14 waste recycling plant (https://chemistryworld.com/news/quotient-ups-carbon-14-quota-with-recycling-plant/9523.article).
- CNNC: named as a supplier (https://pmarketresearch.com/worldwide-carbon-14-market-research/).
- Labelled synthesis: Pharmaron, Almac, Selcia, Moravek, ARC (same source).

**Eight-week proof.** Within 8 weeks a small team could build a demand model from 100+ pharma ADME study disclosures and radiolabelling CRO price quotes, and could run a resin-to-CO2 extraction bench test on surrogate resin. Neither would change the verdict: the model would only confirm that the market is under $300M and already being served. The only things worth testing are adjacent: (a) whether betavoltaic C-14 demand (Kinectrics mentions nuclear batteries) could reach hundreds of Ci or more at a price that pays for enrichment; (b) SCEPTER-style ILW-to-LLW reclassification as a waste-cost-savings service across the about 47 CANDU/PHWR units worldwide, which is also crowded (Kinectrics). Neither is a YC-scale pharma isotope play.

**Killer risk.** Two problems are each fatal on their own. First, the total addressable market including labelled synthesis is about $240M in 2025, and pharma's C-14 consumption is measured in curies, so the ceiling is structurally below $300M. Second, the exact play (CANDU resin feedstock, enrichment, sale to radiolabellers) has been publicly running since 2023-24: CCNuclear/RC-14, ASP Isotopes and OPG, with OPG targeting commercial sales by 2027, plus Kinectrics IP. Price also caps the opportunity: once Western supply returns, the shortage premium (about 5x) collapses toward pre-2022 prices, shrinking the precursor market further.

**Skeptic.** I tried to refute the investigator on both counts and could not. Most of their case holds up against primary sources, and the novelty picture is more crowded than they reported.

NOVELTY: the gap is already named and being filled, and there are more players than the investigator found.
(a) The Ontario government says C-14 "will be harvested and concentrated from the existing supply being stored... at OPG's Western Waste Management Facility" and that OPG "expects to bring Carbon-14 to the market for commercial sale by 2027" (https://www.ontario.ca/page/nuclear-isotopes).
(b) OPG partnered with CCNuclear/RC-14 and launched a pilot in March 2024 (https://www.opg.com/story/opg-enters-partnership-to-supply-important-medical-isotope-faced-with-global-shortage).
(c) ASP Isotopes' SEC 8-K exhibit states that "Russia has historically been responsible for the entire global supply of enriched Carbon-14". It also reports commercial production from February 2025 and a take-or-pay deal with RC-14 at a minimum of about $2.4M/yr (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001921865/000147793225001265/aspi_ex991.htm). So the Russian-cutoff thesis is the incumbents' own public pitch.
(d) Prior art the investigator missed: EnergySolutions holds US12451264B2, filed Feb 2025 and granted Oct 2025. It claims separating mixed-bed spent resin, then releasing C-14 from the anion fraction as CO2 for medical and industrial use (https://patents.google.com/patent/US12451264B2/en). Anyone entering via US light-water reactor resin would face a patent wall from a large waste-services incumbent.
(e) Also missed: US patent 10163536 on recovering nuclides from spent resin (https://patents.justia.com/patent/10163536). Korean KNS conference papers on C-14 recovery from Wolsong CANDU resin (https://kns.org/files/pre_paper/39/18S-609박현오.pdf). A CNS proceedings paper on recovering C-14 from spent resin (https://proceedings.cns-snc.ca/index.php/pcns/article/view/5440). Pharmaron/Quotient recycling C-14 waste (https://www.chemistryworld.com/news/quotient-ups-carbon-14-quota-with-recycling-plant/9523.article).
Novelty is at most 1-2/10.

SIZE: I could not build a bottom-up path to $300M/yr. The anchor is the RC-14 contract: the only disclosed take-or-pay deal for enriched C-14 is about $2.4M/yr minimum. Suppose the whole post-Russia world market for enriched C-14 were 10-20x that contract. Even then the precursor layer is tens of millions of dollars, which matches the investigator's roughly $47M Ba14CO3 figure. Human mass-balance studies use microtracer or about 1 mCi doses (https://www.pharmaron.com/knowledge-center/human-radiolabeled-mass-balance/), so demand per drug programme is tiny. Labelled synthesis brings the stack to only about $240M in total, and that layer is held by GMP incumbents.

I checked the one plausible demand expander, C-14 diamond betavoltaics (https://www.arkenlight.co.uk/news). It is microwatt-scale, pre-commercial and already has a feedstock incumbent (Arkenlight/UKAEA), so it does not change the ceiling within the YC horizon.

Correction to the investigator's numbers: the RC-14 minimum I verified is $2.4M/yr. I did not confirm the upper end of their "$2.4-3.8M" range. The OPG story page itself gives no date; the 2027 date comes from the Ontario government page.

Revised ceiling: About $5-30M/yr for a new entrant. The global enriched-C-14 precursor market is about $20-50M/yr, and the whole C-14 stack, labelled synthesis included, is about $240-370M/yr and already held by incumbents. There is no credible path to $300M/yr.
