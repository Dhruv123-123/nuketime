# Gap hunt, round 1 (raw agent output, 2026-10-07)

Twelve domain hunters, one triage judge and three skeptics (novelty, market, feasibility) per finalist. Agent-written; claims carry the agents' URLs and should be re-checked before use. Synthesis is in `PICK4.md`.

## Triage notes

Merges: the three hafnium hypotheses (non-nuclear bolt-on, OSU-precipitation refinery, USGS-based capture) were collapsed into one, with fab-exhaust Hf as a later module. Five fuel and inventory leasing variants plus the microreactor AerCap became one lessor finalist. The fusion Li-6 lessor was kept separate (different customers, and the sharpest quantified hook, the 70x cost-of-capital ratio) and absorbs tritium-production-as-a-service. Grain e-beam absorbs the India X-ray programme and post-CIPC potato sprout control as a post-harvest irradiation platform. QUICC absorbs the polymer radiation-aging model. Cost-overrun MGA (two copies), liability MGA, decommissioning assurance and the MLP were merged mentally and cut: insurance needs loss history and capacity, the MLP has no assets before 2031, and Tokio Marine HCC and the federal backstop bill crowd the space. Security-as-a-service variants (fleet conversion, remote physical protection for SMRs, unattended ISFSI, module transit custody) were cut for low novelty (TNC NOS Security launched May 2026, incumbents) and capped near-term markets. Cl-37 plus N-15 'Gen-IV light isotopes' was the strongest runner-up: a real 'no production beyond grams' gap, but demand is 2035+ and tied to one reactor family, and 3M/PNNL hold the position. Heavy water from electrolyser brine was an interesting runner-up, but the electrolyser build-out stalled and Indian supply is cheap. Tails-to-fluorine was cut because INIS already tried and failed to finance it. Fission Ru/Rh/Xe was cut because it depends on non-existent US reprocessing. Formally verified I&C was cut because an NRC topical report makes it non-YC-shaped. Heat-import and heat-sink ideas were cut as small (about 6% recovery physics) and owner-captured. CdZnTeSe, the He-3 harvest, thorium custody, the DU flow battery, IFE target foundry, MC&A and tritium accountancy were cut for size or doability.

Ranking rationale: Hafnium and Zr-91 lead because both are physics- or chemistry-grounded value inversions created by 2025-26 price shocks, and each can be tested within weeks (bench precipitation on real liquor; OpenMC lattice economics). They also form a coherent combined company: one Western zirconium refinery selling hafnium, dehafniated Zr and isotope-tailored Zr. Fusion Li-6 is third: a sharp, quantified, uncontested structural insight with a fusion-timing risk. Grain e-beam has a dated 2026 window and a large commodity market. Carbon-12 diamond is a surprising transfer but has a weak moat. HLW reclassification has huge value but a legal bottleneck. QUICC faces regulatory acceptance risk. The fuel lessor is large but least novel.

Verification gaps the next stage must close before claiming novelty: (1) Hf: whether Chinese oxychloride producers already side-extract Hf, the price elasticity, and whether anyone has licensed the OSU IP since the 1 Oct 2026 ANS article. (2) Zr-91: rerun the absorption saving with ZIRLO/M5 compositions, and search for recent laser zirconium programmes (Hexium, ASP Isotopes, LIS Technologies, Chinese AVLIS). (3) The Li-6 leasing prior-art search was incomplete. (4) The QUICC ion-plus-ML startup search was incomplete. (5) Grain e-beam: check that no ag-tech accelerator startup has emerged since the BRIN pilot, and validate lethal-dose throughput at a 1-3 kGy uniformity ratio.

## Finalists and verdicts

### Hafnium harvest: recover the more than 99% of mined hafnium the zirconium chain throws away, priced as a hafnium business with zirconium as the co-product

About 1.2 Mt/yr of zircon (USGS MCS 2026) carries roughly 10-15 kt of hafnium at Hf:Zr of about 1:50, yet only about 70-80 t/yr is recovered, because hafnium is separated only on nuclear-grade zirconium lines (Framatome Jarrie, ATI, Westinghouse WZI, CNNC, Chepetsky). Hafnium went from about $5,000/kg to about $13,100/kg by April 2026 after China's H2-2025 export controls, with about 12 t of reported unmet demand from HBM/DRAM high-k and roughly 80 GW of gas-turbine superalloy orders (SMM, Argus). At those prices the hafnium in a tonne of zircon ($40k-190k) is worth 20-100x the zircon ($1,800-2,150/t). CPM sets $5-6k/kg as the threshold for hafnium-primary production, and prices have been above it since late 2025. OSU's 2026 JACS aqueous precipitation reaches a separation factor of 33, against 6-7 for MIBK solvent extraction, with no organic solvent, and its PI said publicly (ANS, 1 Oct 2026) that commercialising it 'would likely require a start-up'. The wedge is a low-capex bolt-on at non-nuclear zirconium-chemical hosts, whose liquor is already dissolved, that returns dehafniated zirconium at no penalty. The scale-up is a Western Zr/Hf refinery whose hafnium revenue subsidises nuclear zirconium sponge for SMR cladding. Fab-exhaust ALD precursor recovery is a later, higher-margin module.

*Why interesting:* This is the most specific, quantified and timely gap in the set: a 100x value-ratio inversion that the industry still frames as 'Hf is a nuclear byproduct', a separation chemistry published days ago with its PI asking for a startup, and no bolt-on or merchant-refinery competitor found. A small team can test it within weeks: replicate the precipitation on a real zirconium oxychloride liquor, get a host LOI (Tosoh, Daiichi Kigenso, Saint-Gobain, Luxfer MEL) and an offtake LOI from a precursor maker. Revenue reaches $200-600M/yr at 30-50% share of a 150-250 t market. Main risks: price elasticity in a thin market (2021 price was $781/kg), China copying it at its own oxychloride plants, and possible quiet side-extraction already happening in China. All three need checking before committing.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty about 4/10, down from the implied 8-9/10. The specific wedge, a precipitation-based bolt-on at non-nuclear zirconium-chemical plants (MEL, Daiichi Kigenso, Saint-Gobain, Tosoh) and fabs, still looks unoccupied as far as limited fetching could check. The broader 'Western hafnium-first Zr/Hf refinery' is not novel: Energy Fuels/ASM Dubbo already plans it, and incumbents ATI and Westinghouse can license the OSU route. Confidence is low because the search budget was exhausted, so patent, SBIR, ARPA-E and startup-database searches were not done. Re-run before relying on this.

  Counter-evidence: Energy Fuels (NYSE American/TSX/ASX) completed its acquisition of Australian Strategic Materials on 28-31 Aug 2026. The combined company names 'zirconium, niobium, and hafnium production' as a product line (https://asm-au.com/energy-fuels-completes-acquisition-of-asm/). ASM's Dubbo Project explicitly plans hafnium chloride, oxide and metal as 'an alternative global source of hafnium' (https://asm-au.com/products/hafnium/). So a well-funded Western player already runs the 'hafnium as a value product from non-nuclear zirconium' strategy. Separately, USGS puts hafnium at about $3,800/kg ex-works China in 2025, falling year on year (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-zirconium-hafnium.pdf). That suggests the $13k/kg economics depend on export controls and the Western/Chinese price gap lasting, not on a structural shortage.

- **market: weakened.** Steady-state revenue ceiling for a hafnium-led company: about $50-160M/yr (central about $100M). This assumes a 20-40 t/yr share of an estimated ~100 t/yr world market at a post-entry price of $1.5-4k/kg. About $300M+ needs both world demand to roughly double and Western-origin prices to hold near $8k/kg. It is possible but speculative. This is below the ~$300M bar unless it is re-scoped as a broader Western Zr/Hf refinery, where the nuclear sponge incumbents are integrated.

  Counter-evidence: The best case for the thesis is that demand is no longer fixed:
- HBM/DRAM high-k, ferroelectric HfO2 memory and the gas-turbine build-out (thesis cites roughly 80 GW of orders) could keep growing.
- China's export controls could create a durable 'Western-origin' price premium, the way gallium/germanium ex-China pricing behaves.
- If world demand reached about 200 t by 2030 and Western-origin Hf held about $8k/kg, a 30-40% share would be $500-650M/yr.
- Separately, US Imports have risen from 23 t (2021) to 72 t (2025) (USGS), so demand is growing.
- The cheap aqueous route could also undercut incumbents on Zr dehafniation cost for nuclear sponge, adding Zr revenue.

- **feasibility: weakened.** About 60% chance an 8-week sprint produces convincing evidence: bench replication plus a techno-economic model plus at least one buyer LOI, with a host LOI unlikely. About 15-25% chance of commercial spec-grade hafnium sales by 2030. A hafnium-only business looks capped at about $50-150M/yr revenue at normal prices for a 10-30 t/yr producer. Clearing $300M+/yr depends on the Zr/Hf refinery and nuclear sponge scale-up, which needs $200M+ capex and 4-6+ years.

  Counter-evidence: Hafnium has crashed before after spikes ($6,150/kg in 2023 to $4,600 in 2024 per USGS), and it is already about 24% below the thesis's April 2026 peak ($9,965/kg on 5 Oct 2026). The market is thin, so any meaningful new supply, including the startup's own, plus restarted idle incumbent capacity or relaxed Chinese controls, would erode the 20-100x hafnium-to-zircon value gap the thesis relies on. Also, the zirconium-chemical hosts own the feedstock and can license OSU's process directly, so the startup has little defensible position.

### Zr-91-stripped zirconium: isotope-tailored cladding that needs no requalification, revived by the 2024-26 doubling of uranium and SWU prices

NIST cross-sections show that Zr-91, the only odd-mass stable zirconium isotope at 11.2% abundance, causes about 71% of natural zirconium's thermal neutron absorption (0.131 of 0.185 b). Because its mass sits between Zr-90 and Zr-92, centrifuges cannot cut it cleanly, but polarization and hyperfine odd/even laser selectivity can: Japanese work reported a separation coefficient of 2460. Westinghouse (EP0104760B1) claimed 5-7% fuel-cost savings, and AECL patented odd-mass zirconium photoionization in 1993 and abandoned it in 1997, when uranium was about $10-40/lb. Uranium is now $86/lb and SWU about $109, so each pcm of parasitic absorption is worth roughly twice as much, and HALEU SMRs and natural-uranium CANDU cores value it even more. The cladding is chemically identical to Zircaloy, ZIRLO or M5, so there is no new-material qualification campaign of the kind FeCrAl or SiC requires. At about 2,000 t/yr of reload zirconium and a $150-300/kg premium, the addressable revenue is about $300-600M/yr.

*Why interesting:* This is a real physics asymmetry (one odd isotope dominates absorption) inside a commercially dead idea that 2026 prices may have revived, which suits a technical founder. The first proof costs almost nothing and is fast: an OpenMC/SCALE lattice calculation with modern Nb/Sn alloys priced at 2026 fuel costs, in about 2 weeks. If the saving exceeds about 2% per reload, follow with a laser-selectivity demo. It pairs naturally with the hafnium refinery, since one Western zirconium plant could sell both dehafniated and isotope-tailored zirconium. Killer risks: AVLIS-class throughput on refractory zirconium at under about $100-150/kg, export controls on laser isotope separation, and fuel-vendor buy-in. The novelty is the timing and the business case, not the concept, and that should be stated plainly.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty: low to moderate, about 3/10. The core concept has well-documented prior art from four major organisations (Westinghouse, DOE/LLNL, USEC, AECL) plus Russian Zr separation capability. The only novel part is the timing and economics argument for a commercial revival. In the limited checks I could run (ASP Isotopes' site, the patent citation trails), I found no active commercial competitor, so the "revival" angle survives as a possible opportunity. The "gap nobody found" claim does not.

  Counter-evidence: Westinghouse (EP0104760B1, 1983), DOE/LLNL (US5376246A, later assigned to USEC), and AECL (US5316635A, US5527437A, WO1997014496) all developed Zr-91 depletion specifically for reactor cladding economics. USEC, the actual commercial enrichment company, held the DOE Zr-91 patent and let it lapse in 2006 after cancelling AVLIS in 1999. Rosatom's Electrokhimpribor plant already separates all five Zr isotopes by electromagnetic separation (RU2160153C1). The idea is well known to incumbents. They abandoned it on economics, not because they missed it. The burden is to show what has changed in laser cost and throughput, not to claim discovery. Westinghouse's own estimate was only about $80/lb of Zircaloy-4. Even if that value roughly doubles with fuel prices, the margin is thin against laser-separation cost per kg at roughly 2,000 t/yr scale.

- **market: weakened.** Theoretical ceiling: about $280-330M/yr at 100% of the Western LWR+CANDU market at $250/kg (about 1,100-1,300 t Zr/yr), or about $500M/yr global, which can't be reached because Russia and China supply their own zirconium. Realistic revenue in the mid-2030s: about $100-200M/yr after 5-6 years of lead-test-assembly cycles and partial share with a three-firm monopsony. It only clears the $300M bar if you assume near-100% Western share plus premium pricing, and it is unproven that the material can be made at kiloton scale for $150-300/kg.

  Counter-evidence: The strongest case for the thesis is that Westinghouse's own 1983 break-even ($176/kg Zr-4) scales to about $450-800/kg gross value at today's $90-97/lb uranium and $3.8-4.3k/kgU fuel cost. CANDU natural-U cores and HALEU-scarce SMRs value each pcm even more. If a molecular-laser process (not atomic vapor) can reach under $100/kg at kiloton scale, margins are large, and selling to three vendors keeps go-to-market concentrated. The physics value is real. What keeps it from being a large company is the market ceiling and the cost of production.

- **feasibility: weakened.** Feasibility: low, about a 10-15% chance a startup reaches commercial supply within 6-8 years. The binding constraint is separation cost below about $150/kg at 1,000+ t/yr, plus export-control and classification review of an AVLIS-like plant. A realistic early market is a niche premium tier at tens of tonnes per year (CANDU, HALEU SMRs or research reactors), worth about $10-30M/yr, not the $300-600M thesis. In 8 weeks a team can produce a neutronics value model and possibly a conditional utility LOI, but not credible separation-cost evidence. I'd call it weakened rather than killed, and it is killed if a back-of-envelope laser photon-cost and e-beam evaporation-energy model can't reach below $200/kg.

  Counter-evidence: For: the patented value case is real, about 5% fuel-cost saving for removing Zr-91 (https://patents.google.com/patent/EP0104760B1/en), and the NIST data confirms Zr-91 dominates zirconium's absorption (https://www.ncnr.nist.gov/resources/n-lengths/elements/zr.html). The product would need no new material qualification. ASP Isotopes markets a laser "Quantum Enrichment" process and a nuclear-energy arm (https://aspisotopes.com/), so commercial odd/even laser separation is being pursued. A partial cut, say 50% Zr-91 removal sold as a premium tier first to CANDU or HALEU SMR vendors, would need less throughput and selectivity.

Against: laser isotope separation has never reached commercial tonne scale for any element, even uranium where each kilogram is far more valuable. AVLIS was cancelled in 1999 and SILEX is still pre-commercial after about 30 years with heavy classification (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_vapor_laser_isotope_separation, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_isotopes_by_laser_excitation). Atomic-vapor zirconium needs e-beam evaporation at about 4400 C. The value of roughly $300-700/kg leaves little room for the separation cost. ASP Isotopes is also a likely competitor: it already owns odd/even laser capability (it may be first-to-market or partner).

### Fusion breeder-inventory lessor: own the enriched Li-6, tritium and beryllium that fusion plants barely consume but must finance

Ward et al. (arXiv 2605.04707, May 2026) estimate about 100 t of enriched Li-6 per GWth of fusion blanket, of which only about 100 kg/yr (0.1%) is consumed. At 7% interest the cost of capital is 70x the consumption cost, and at $5k/kg enriched lithium can be about 25% of plant overnight cost (today's price is about $50k/kg in small lots). Tritium start-up inventories of 1-4 kg per plant at $25-33M/kg are a wasting asset (5.5%/yr decay) that venture-funded developers (CFS $863M, Inertia $450M) currently hold on equity costing 15-20%. Every supplier found (Hexium, Molten Salt Solutions, OPG/Laurentis, AGENCORE, Materion) sells material; none leases it, and the paper names the financing problem without proposing a lessor. A lessor charging 8% saves about $100M/yr per GWe, roughly a 10-20% cut in LCOE. A sibling tritium-sourcing arm (contracting CANDU or PWR fleet neutrons, or brokering stockpiles) secures the scarcest input.

*Why interesting:* The structural insight is sharp and quantified: blanket inventory is a financing problem, not a fuel problem. It also sits outside the excluded fusion-control territory. It is provable in weeks with lease LOIs from 2-3 DT pilot developers plus a warehouse or offtake term sheet with an enricher such as Hexium. The ceiling is $1B+/yr at 10 GWe. Risks: fusion timing (DT pilots slipping past 2035), developers wanting title to tritium for licensing, and a Li-6 leasing prior-art search that was cut short and must be finished.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty: about 5/10. In the sources I could reach, nobody leases fusion breeder inventory, and the paper names the financing problem without a fix. But the financing structure itself is well known in fission and metals, and the check was cut short by the search cap, so this needs a full search before anyone relies on it. Market timing also weakens it, using the thesis's own numbers: about $500M of enriched Li per GWth, leased at 8%, earns roughly $40M/yr per GWth, plus about $5-15M/yr per plant from tritium. Reaching $300M/yr needs about 7-8 GWth (about 3 GWe) under lease. That is plausible in the early-to-mid 2040s, with almost no revenue before about 2033. As a YC company today it is a balance-sheet business waiting for fusion to arrive, unless it starts as an enrichment or tritium-sourcing business.

  Counter-evidence: The approach is already standard practice in fission: lending uranium and UF6, nuclear fuel lease trusts, AEC leasing of enriched uranium before 1964, and bank leasing of precious-metal catalysts. A fusion version needs no invention, and a lithium enricher like Marathon Fusion (https://www.marathonfusion.com/) or a commodity trader could bundle leasing with sales at almost no cost, so the startup has no moat. Separately, the FIA position (https://www.fusionindustryassociation.org/ensuring-the-security-of-fuel-supplies-for-fusion-energy/) is that plants breed their own tritium and lithium is abundant, so developers may not feel the pain until about 2035. These precedents still need citations because searches were blocked.

- **market: killed.** Lessor revenue ceiling:
- 2030-2035: under $50M/yr, from 0-2 plants.
- 2035-2040: about $50-250M/yr gross on optimistic D-T deployment. Real economic margin, the spread over the lessor's cost of funds, would be about $10-60M/yr.
- $300M/yr or more: around 2045 at the earliest, on the paper's own 10-plants-by-2050 path.
- Per-plant financing saving versus project-finance rates: about $5-15M/yr, not the claimed $100M/yr per GWe.
The tritium-brokerage arm adds at most about $50-100M/yr, capped by physical supply.

This is not a venture-scale business on a YC timeframe unless it becomes a Li-6 enrichment company, which is on the excluded known-ground list.

  Counter-evidence: The thesis has two real points in its favour:
- The material is recoverable, so lithium inventory makes good collateral, and the 70x cost-of-capital ratio is the paper's own number. A durable asset with a small consumption rate is the classic case for leasing.
- If CFS ARC (early 2030s), the IFE developers and other D-T developers all hit their dates, and Li-6 prices fall to about $5k/kg, a first mover could aggregate supply and pre-sell lease capacity. Fusion developers are richly funded (CFS about $4.7B), so they could anchor offtake.

An optimistic 2035 still gives only about $125-300M/yr of gross lease revenue, and the economic margin is a small fraction of that.

- **feasibility: killed.** Feasibility as a YC startup is about 1/10. Revenue before 2032 is roughly zero. It needs $0.5-5B of material per GWth that cannot be bought at scale today, against collateral that cannot be recovered and under weapons-related export controls. The financing edge reduces to the plant's own credit risk, which project lenders will price once plants exist. The defensible business underneath it is Li-6 enrichment supply, which is on the excluded list and is what the paper actually calls for. Credible 8-week evidence would be a model plus a soft developer LOI, which is not enough to survive.

  Counter-evidence: The numbers are real and large. At 7%, financing costs exceed $2k per kg-year on 100 t per GWth, about 70x the consumption cost (https://arxiv.org/html/2605.04707). No named supplier leases material, and venture-funded developers (CFS has raised over $1B for its plant alone, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Fusion_Systems) are carrying long-lived inventory on expensive equity. If a lessor could get government-backed or infrastructure-cost capital (for example a DOE Loan Programs Office facility or a sovereign co-investor) plus a fusion-grade (lower-enrichment) supply route, the structure could save tens of millions of dollars per year per plant. Li-6, unlike tritium, does not decay and could in principle be reused across plants if it can be recovered. Designs that run on natural lithium (pulsed and inertial concepts such as First Light, General Fusion, Zap and Renaissance, per the paper) would shrink the market but would not invalidate it for tokamak blankets.

### Resistance-proof e-beam grain disinfestation as a per-tonne utility, starting with state grain reserves

Phosphine, the global default grain fumigant, is failing: on 30 Sep 2026 ICAR and FCI signed an MoU stating that 72-100% of storage insects in Indian grain warehouses are resistant, and the alternatives they are testing (N2, CO2) leave out irradiation entirely. Strong resistance in Australia rose more than fivefold from 2000 to 2021. Indonesia's BRIN and Bulog began an e-beam pilot in 2026 at only 4-5 t/day, which shows demand pull with no one supplying at elevator scale. The physics is cheap: 1 kGy is about 0.3 kWh of beam energy per tonne, a 400 t/h port-elevator radiation plant has operated before, and FDA permits up to 1 kGy for disinfestation. A build-own-operate MeV accelerator utility at $1-2/t on state reserves and export elevators gives $0.3-1B/yr, against a $1.9B fumigant market. India's subsidised 50-unit programme and post-CIPC potato and onion sprout control add product lines.

*Why interesting:* A 2026 resistance crisis plus a dated government MoU creates a real buying window. The technology is proven but nobody operates it commercially at elevator scale. It is a cross-industry transfer of accelerator physics into a large commodity flow. A pilot dose-mapping study on a flowing grain stream plus LOIs from FCI or Bulog is doable in weeks. Risks: import and labelling rules (the EU bars irradiated grain), the need for lethal rather than sterilising doses to meet nil-live-insect specs, no residual protection against reinfestation, and slow state buyers. Starting with domestic state reserves avoids the trade problem.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty: low to moderate, about 3/10. The specific business model (build-own-operate, per-tonne pricing, state reserves as first customer, sold because phosphine resistance is spreading) appears unoccupied among the companies I checked. But the core idea is decades old and has been run at elevator scale before. To survive, the pitch must explain why the earlier plant failed and what changed since (phosphine resistance, cheaper solid-state accelerators, India's subsidy scheme). It cannot be pitched as a gap nobody has seen. This is provisional: the search budget ran out before the full check.

  Counter-evidence: Grain e-beam disinfestation is established science. It is named in standard references as a known application (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron-beam_processing) and was built at port-elevator scale decades ago (the 400 t/h plant the thesis itself cites). So the gap was found before and abandoned, not missed. Two things likely explain why, and both still apply (from memory, unverified): consumer labelling and acceptance, and EU rules that allow irradiation of only dried herbs, spices and seasonings (Directive 1999/3/EC). Reveam (https://reveam.com/) already sells e-beam phytosanitary treatment as a service, just not for grain, and STERIS/Mevex sell the hardware for it, so incumbents could enter quickly if state demand appears.

- **market: weakened.** Realistic ceiling about $50-200M/yr, global and mature, which is below the $300M bar. India central pool at most about $60-90M/yr at 100% penetration of one intake treatment, and more like $10-30M in practice. The $0.3-1B figure assumes grain stored in bags gets treated once and stays protected, and that export buyers accept irradiated grain and a price 3-10x phosphine's. Neither looks likely. I am rating this 'weakened' rather than 'killed' only because the search limit stopped me from checking the FCI fumigation budget, the phosphine cost per tonne, and actual EU/Codex trade rules for irradiated cereals. If phosphine really costs under $0.5/t all-in, as I estimate, this should be killed.

  Counter-evidence: The strongest case for the thesis: resistance is real and spreading (Wikipedia/Phosphine confirms Asia, Australia and Brazil), and the ICAR-FCI MoU shows state demand for an alternative. Beam physics really does allow about 100+ t/h per 100 kW at 1 kGy, so the cost floor could fall below $1/t at high utilisation. India's subsidised irradiation-plant programme lowers the capex barrier. If phosphine resistance turns into documented grain-loss figures (for example, FCI losses priced in hundreds of crores), a state buyer could accept a premium. Against all of that: grain irradiation has been technically available and legal for about 40 years. A 1980s plant at Odessa port reportedly handled about 400 t/h using two accelerators. That this did not spread is strong evidence that buyers will not pay, rather than evidence of an overlooked gap. I recall the Odessa plant but could not re-verify it this session.

- **feasibility: weakened.** Feasibility: low to moderate. Technically doable, commercially fragile. The realistic addressable revenue is domestic state reserves only (India, Indonesia, perhaps China), and only for intake into sealed storage. That suggests about $50-150M/yr at maturity, not $0.3-1B, because export elevators are largely blocked by EU and US labelling and reinfestation caps the share of storage spend you can win. Time to first revenue-generating plant: 2-3+ years once procurement and AERB-equivalent licensing are counted. Probability that a small team gets convincing external validation (an LOI or a paid pilot) in 8 weeks: below 15%.

  Counter-evidence: For the idea: phosphine resistance is genuinely rising (the ICAR-FCI MoU cites 72-100% resistance, and Australia's strong resistance is up more than 5-fold), the operating cost of an e-beam per tonne is truly cents, and FDA, Codex and India permit up to 1 kGy. For intake-only use cases, the thesis could be rescued by a hybrid model. One example is treating grain as it goes into hermetic silo bags or sealed silos, where reinfestation is physically blocked. Another is treating grain released from reserves for subsidised distribution (India's public distribution system) just before it is distributed, which domestic labelling exemptions or public-sector channels might allow. Indonesia's BRIN-Bulog pilot shows that at least one state buyer is engaging.

### Carbon-12 isotope-engineered polycrystalline diamond heat spreaders for AI accelerators

Measured data show polycrystalline CVD diamond grown from 99.96% carbon-12 conducts about 35% more heat (about 2,500 W/mK, up from 1,860) than natural-isotope polycrystalline diamond, making cheap, large-area polycrystalline perform like expensive single crystal. Diamond thermal management is entering GPU supply chains: Akash reports H200 deployments and a reported $300M MI350 order, and Diamond Foundry and Element Six are also selling thermal diamond. None of them sells an isotope-enriched thermal product, and CIL's new 99.99% carbon-12 methane line targets quantum NV centres only. GE's patent on isotopically pure polycrystalline diamond (US5540904) has expired. At 10-20M accelerators/yr and a $20-50 premium, revenue reaches $200M-1B/yr. The defensible position is owning cheap bulk carbon-12 through CO cryogenic distillation, a nuclear-style isotope-separation competence, plus closed-loop methane recycling in CVD.

*Why interesting:* A nuclear-adjacent isotope-separation skill applied to the hottest thermal bottleneck in compute is a surprising transfer with a measured physics gain. It is provable in weeks: buy carbon-12 methane, have a CVD house grow 12C polycrystalline coupons, measure thermal conductivity with TDR/laser flash, and test on a GPU thermal rig. Risks: no moat if incumbents simply buy CIL methane, boundary resistance swamping the bulk gain at hot spots under about 15 um, and early diamond-packaging adoption. The weak link to nuclear may matter for how the application is framed.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty about 3/10. The concept and its thermal-management use have been public since the early 1990s through GE's patents, so the idea itself is not novel. What may still be open is "the first commercial isotope-engineered thermal diamond grade in the AI-accelerator supply chain", backed by a low-cost 12C supply. I found no one selling that, but my search was cut short. Treat it as a timing and cost-curve bet: the pitch has to show what changed since GE shelved it (cheap 12C, AI thermal limits). It is not an undiscovered gap. An incumbent (E6, DF, Akash) could replicate it by switching feed gas, so defensibility depends on owning 12C supply at prices well below today's.

  Counter-evidence: GE's expired patents US5419276 and US5540904 (https://patents.google.com/patent/US5540904A/en) claim exactly this: polycrystalline CVD diamond of at least 99.5% 12C with improved thermal conductivity, explicitly for heat sinks on electronics. GE measured large gains in the 1990s and never commercialised it. On top of that, Diamond Foundry (https://www.df.com/) now sells 100 mm single-crystal diamond for AI chips, which weakens the "cheap polycrystalline that performs like single crystal" angle.

- **market: killed.** Realistic revenue ceiling: about $10-50M/yr (best case about $180M/yr), below the $300M bar. The thesis's $200M-1B depends on three things the evidence doesn't support: 1) a 35% gain, when it is about 14% against today's 2,200 W/mK polycrystalline; 2) a $20-50 per-unit premium, when the gain is worth about 1 K or less; 3) broad diamond attach on 10-20M accelerators a year, when verified paid demand is in the tens of millions of dollars (the NxtGen contract is $27M).

  Counter-evidence: The best case for the idea rests on hotspots. Hotspot spreading resistance scales with 1/k, and in 3D-stacked or chiplet packages with HBM, local heat fluxes approach 1 kW/cm². There, lateral spreading through the diamond matters more than the through-thickness term, and a 14-35% conductivity gain could be worth a few kelvin at the hottest spots. Element Six already markets diamond for AI accelerators and chiplet/HBM packages, and Diamond Foundry is spending billions, so diamond thermal management is a real and growing market. If diamond became standard in packages and carbon-12 feed fell to well under $1/g through closed-loop recycling, a premium on high-end parts might be defensible. Even then the likely outcome is a product line or licence for Element Six or Diamond Foundry, not an independent company above $300M.

- **feasibility: weakened.** Feasibility for a small team: low to moderate. Physics is feasible at material level, but junction-temperature benefit as a GPU spreader is likely 0.5-2 C, so a realistic premium is $0-10 per part rather than $20-50. Revenue potential falls to about $0-150M/yr unless chip-on-diamond hotspot cases show more than 3 C. Carbon-12 supply at 50-800 t/yr would need a new $100M-plus multi-year cryogenic distillation plant (my estimate, not verified). An 8-week model and coupon test is doable; a paying LOI is unlikely. Probability the thesis survives the 8-week model is about 20%.

  Counter-evidence: GE's expired patent data show a bigger polycrystalline gain than the thesis assumes, 50% (8 to 12 W/cm-K), with isotope effects dominating over grain boundaries (https://patents.google.com/patent/US5540904A/en). In chip-on-diamond or GaN-on-diamond designs, where diamond is bonded right at the hotspot (Akash sells a 'Chip-on-Diamond' product, https://akashsystems.com/), heat flows through the diamond close to the junction and the 1/k diminishing-returns argument weakens. That could give 2-4 C instead of under 1 C, which is meaningful for RF power amplifiers and possibly for GPU hotspots. There is also no regulatory or export-control barrier I know of.

### AI evidence engine for reclassifying DOE high-level tank waste (vitrification to grout)

GAO-26-108018 (March 2026) puts remaining DOE EM cleanup above $500B and 90M gallons of tank waste. About 11M gallons at Hanford are potentially below low-level waste limits, and grouting Hanford low-activity waste could save $73-210B (SRNL: $95B, Idaho calcine: $11-14B). Grout costs $153/gal against about $1,081/gal for vitrification. Each WIR or Section 3116 evaluation takes 3-5 years and several million dollars, and GAO and ANS say there is no consistent threshold. DOE's AI work is lab-level (PNNL glass ML, SRNL digital twins), and no startup builds uncertainty-quantified classification evidence packages. Moving 1M gallons saves about $0.9B, so a gain-share or AI-native technical-contractor model can reach $300M-1B/yr.

*Why interesting:* The value per decision is enormous and quantified by GAO, the bottleneck is evidence synthesis over decades of messy sampling data, and it is distinct from the crowded licensing-copilot space because the customer is DOE EM and its M&O contractors, not reactor licensing. A small team could rebuild one public tank's classification case from released data within weeks. Risks: the binding constraint is legal and political (GAO suggests a Blue Ribbon Commission, Washington and South Carolina opposition, post-Chevron litigation), and procurement runs through M&O contractors. It is penalised slightly for adjacency to the excluded regulatory-AI space.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty: probably real as a product category (about 65% that no startup does it; I could not check SBIR, Crunchbase or LinkedIn). Novelty of the insight is low: GAO, ANS, SRNL and DOE have known about the $73-210B reclassification lever for 20 years, and what blocks it is legal, statutory and political, not analytical. Realistic market as a software or analysis vendor is about $10-50M/yr, capped by DOE-EM contractor budgets. The $300M-1B/yr gain-share case depends on a federal contracting mechanism that does not exist and on regulatory acceptance the product cannot drive alone.

  Counter-evidence: The strongest case for novelty: GAO explicitly asks EM to "systematically evaluate reclassification opportunities," and no startup or lab tool that I know of produces standardized, uncertainty-quantified, litigation-defensible evidence packages across all tanks. A credible, consistent, transparent methodology could lower litigation risk, which GAO does name as a barrier. That is the one place the product might move the real bottleneck. Political tailwinds (EM cost pressure, DOE pushing grout and offsite disposal) could also open the window. I could not verify any 2025-2026 policy change because the search budget was gone.

- **market: killed.** About $10-50M per year realistic ceiling, as a technical-evidence contractor working through EM primes and labs. Today's addressable spend is about $5-25M per year (1-5 evaluations at several $M each). A $300M+ outcome depends on Congress redefining HLW and the FY2026 NDAA Hanford bar being lifted. A gain-share model is not a standard DOE contracting mechanism and would pay out over decades.

  Counter-evidence: GAO itself says systematic reclassification could save "at least tens of billions of dollars." It recommends both an EM-wide review and a congressional panel to rewrite the HLW definition within 12 months (https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108018.pdf). If Congress clarifies "highly radioactive" in 2027-28, a backlog of waste-stream evaluations could open up across Hanford, Idaho and West Valley quickly. A vendor already holding uncertainty-quantified (UQ) evidence packages and NRC-review experience could then win a value-engineering share or a large services subcontract. In that case the per-evaluation cost (several $M) times dozens of streams, plus savings-linked fees, could exceed $100M per year. A second point in the thesis's favour: the Hanford Test Bed Initiative (from memory, not verified this turn) showed that grouting tank waste and sending it to commercial LLW sites works physically, so the barrier is only legal and political. Even so, that scenario is a bet on legislation, not on product pull.

- **feasibility: killed.** Feasibility is low. The main lever, reclassification at Hanford, is blocked by the 2024 settlement and by the FY2026 appropriations bar, and the bottleneck is legal and political, not technical. Realistic revenue for a startup is about $5-30M/yr in subcontract analytics services to DOE primes and labs, not $300M-1B/yr. Gain-share contracting with DOE EM is not plausible within the YC timeframe.

  Counter-evidence: GAO does put the Hanford LLW saving at $73-210B (EM's 2020 estimate). It also recommends that EM systematically evaluate remaining cost-saving opportunities. So there is federal demand for better analysis, and WIR evaluations at Hanford remain legally available. If Congress acts on GAO's expert-panel recommendation, or a future administration reverses the 2024 forbearance, defensible classification evidence could become valuable quickly. Even then, the work would most likely go to PNNL, SRNL and the prime contractors rather than a startup.

### QUICC-as-a-service: a commercial ion-beam plus ML lab that qualifies reactor and fusion materials in months

Michigan's QUICC method (Feb 2026) qualifies irradiated materials about 1,000x faster at about 1/1000 the cost of test-reactor campaigns, and ASTM E521 is on its third revision driven by this work. ASME (July 2026) and Gary Was say reactor-based qualification is impractical because ATR is the only viable US facility, has limited capacity, and dose campaigns take decades. Meanwhile AI self-driving alloy labs (VTT RADIANT screening more than 100k variants, Wisconsin AlloyBot) generate candidates far faster than anyone can irradiate them. No company sells QUICC-style qualification with an ML ion-to-neutron transfer function; Avalanche FusionWERX sells neutrons and the MIT LMNT cyclotron is philanthropic. A lab of record plus a data moat, expanding into polymer and cable aging for 80-year licence extensions and Co-60-to-e-beam sterilisation transfers, targets $100-400M/yr.

*Why interesting:* It sits at the throughput bottleneck between AI materials discovery and deployment for 50-70 fission vendors and 40+ fusion companies, with a clear performance lever (higher-temperature, higher-dose alloys). The proof is a partnership with the Michigan Ion Beam Lab plus a published ion-to-neutron model on existing NSUF data. Risks: NRC and ASME have not accepted ion-only data without a code case, so it may stay an R&D service for years, and the founders likely need to partner with Was's group rather than compete with it. The final prior-art search was incomplete.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty: partial. I found no direct commercial QUICC service, but the method owners (EPRI consortium, Michigan) and free DOE beam access already fill most of the gap, and my searching was limited. On market size, a realistic service-lab ceiling is about $20-60M/yr. The $100-400M/yr figure only works if expansion into polymer and cable aging and the sterilisation transfers succeeds, and that is a separate, more contested market.

  Counter-evidence: QUICC is a public method built by an industry and national-lab consortium (DOE, EPRI, ORNL, Framatome, Rolls-Royce) and run at Michigan's university ion beam lab: https://news.engin.umich.edu/2026/02/new-approach-to-qualifying-nuclear-reactor-components-rolling-out/. On top of that, DOE's user-facility programme already gives ion-beam irradiation to researchers, industry partners included, free and on fast-turnaround calls: https://nsuf.inl.gov/. So the method's owners and the reference lab are already in place, and the main US customers can get the beam time for free. A startup would be selling on price and credibility against free, standards-anointed university and national labs.

- **market: killed.** Core ion-plus-ML qualification service: about $30-60M/yr realistic ceiling, about $110M/yr in an aggressive case. The cable-aging and sterilisation add-ons don't use the ion asset and face entrenched incumbents, so they add little defensible revenue. Total is well below the $300M/yr bar. Search budget was exhausted, so these figures rest on partial sourcing and domain estimates.

  Counter-evidence: The thesis's own sources say ATR is the only viable US test reactor, its capacity is limited, and dose campaigns can take decades. If NRC or ASME formally accepted an ion-to-neutron transfer function (the ASTM E521 revision is a step toward that), the lab of record could capture part of today's multi-million-dollar test-reactor spend per material and charge regulatory-grade prices. Fusion first-wall materials have no fission-reactor substitute, and around 40-50 funded fusion companies need dpa-plus-helium data. A data moat across many alloys could also become a licensing business. Even so, NSUF's free access and existing commercial beamlines (DCF, MIBL) cap pricing. Getting regulatory acceptance would take years, and a national lab or university could publish the transfer function as a public good.

- **feasibility: weakened.** Feasibility: low-to-moderate. Regulatory or code acceptance is unlikely before about 2030 (my judgement). Realistic standalone revenue is about $10-40M/yr as a screening and data lab, not $100-400M. In 8 weeks a team could produce a credible transfer-function model and screening letters of intent, but no evidence on the core qualification claim. Still alive only as a fusion-first screening play; the 'qualification lab of record' thesis is weakened, near killed, because the inventor lab and free NSUF and Argonne access already exist.

  Counter-evidence: The test-reactor bottleneck is real: ATR is effectively the only large US fast-flux-relevant option and the Versatile Test Reactor was not funded. Fusion companies need first-wall materials data at 100-200 dpa that no reactor can deliver on any reasonable timeline. QUICC also has heavyweight industrial backers (EPRI, Framatome, Rolls-Royce) plus ASTM momentum (Michigan release above). If ASTM and then ASME formally adopt ion-based swelling qualification, the first NQA-1 lab of record could charge a premium and win multi-year vendor contracts. Fusion vendors, who face no NRC Part 50/52 materials requirement (they are regulated under the byproduct-material framework), could become paying customers sooner. A capex-light version (an ML transfer function sold as software, with beam time bought from NSUF partner labs) is buildable by a small team.

### Nuclear fuel and module lessor: an AerCap for enriched product, HALEU first cores and factory-built reactors

HALEU costs about $24-26k/kgU (NIA), and TerraPower and X-energy alone need about 20 t, roughly $500M of fuel working capital before any revenue, funded today with 15-20% venture equity. INL projects about 520 MTU/yr of HALEU (about $12B/yr) and its market study says nothing about who holds title or finances it. US operators hold 118-126M lb U3O8e of inventory (about $10B), built up because of the Russian ban and the 2028 waiver cliff. Yet SPUT ($7.4B) and Yellow Cake hold only idle U3O8, and no vehicle leases enriched product, fresh fuel or HALEU cores; the 1980s utility fuel trusts disappeared. A lessor funded at 7-9% secured, with yield-seeking uranium capital as the funding base, captures a 3-5% spread on a $5-10B book, about $250-700M/yr. It extends later to leasing transportable microreactor modules, where vendors (Radiant, NANO) currently carry fleets on venture balance sheets.

*Why interesting:* Financial engineering over a real structural mismatch (decades-long assets on venture equity) is created by 2025-26 policy: the Russian ban, the HALEU ramp and new non-utility owners. Dhruv's uranium background gives credible founder-market fit without touching prospecting. It is provable in weeks with LOIs from 2-3 advanced-reactor developers and a term sheet from a commodity or aircraft-leasing financier. It is ranked last among finalists because it is the least novel (historical fuel trusts, traders doing U3O8 loans), capital-heavy rather than YC-shaped, and irradiated fuel has little collateral value, so credit risk concentrates on the weakest developers.

- **novelty: weakened.** Novelty: partial, about 4/10. No dedicated independent lessor of enriched product, HALEU cores or modules turned up, though my search was cut short. Functionally equivalent pieces do exist: in-house utility fuel SPVs (Entergy), physical uranium loans through traders, DOE buying and allocating HALEU, and vendor build-own-operate (Oklo, DoD energy-as-a-service, from prior knowledge). The lessor angle could still be fundable as an advanced-reactor HALEU and first-core finance company. It is not a gap nobody has found, and the spread is likely compressed by DOE involvement and utilities' cheap corporate credit.

  Counter-evidence: Entergy has kept nuclear fuel company SPVs on its 10-K every year from 2010 to 2025, which shows utility fuel-financing structures never went away. Utilities that want off-balance-sheet fuel already set this up themselves with cheap corporate credit, so a 3-5% spread lessor has no obvious opening with large operators. DOE's HALEU Availability Program buys HALEU and allocates it to developers, putting the near-term first-core inventory on the government's balance sheet, which is the exact working-capital gap in the pitch. Uranium loan agreements such as enCore's (FY2024 10-K exhibit 10.5) show that in-kind lending through traders is already standard. And fabricated fuel is reactor-specific: one design's assemblies cannot be re-leased to another, unlike aircraft, so the AerCap redeployment and residual-value logic does not hold.

- **market: weakened.** About $50-120M/yr of net spread at early-2030s run-rate (LWR merchant book $2-4B at about 1.5% plus HALEU book $0.5-1.5B at about 3%; modules negligible). $300M/yr+ needs an $8-10B book backed by about $1B of equity, and spreads are compressed by investment-grade counterparties and existing captive fuel vehicles. On novelty, the claim that fuel trusts disappeared is wrong: AEP and Entergy still use nuclear fuel lease and fuel-company VIE structures.

  Counter-evidence: The strongest case for the idea: HALEU working capital is real and hard to fund. Pre-revenue SMR vendors pay venture-equity cost for something debt-like. DOE allocations are conditional and limited, and the statute requires sale, so DOE is not a long-term financier. Once commercial HALEU enrichment scales (Centrus, Urenco USA, Orano Oak Ridge, General Matter), someone has to hold title between enrichment and the reactor. A first mover that sets standard fuel-lease paperwork and credit underwriting could become the default counterparty. Also, the existing captive vehicles (Entergy VIEs, AEP fuel leases) show the structure is bankable, so it is not a novel legal risk.

- **feasibility: weakened.** Feasibility for the leasing framing is about 25%. Legal: yes. Economics at the stated 7-9% cost of funds with a 3-5% spread: unlikely. Repossession needs an NRC licence or licence transfer, fabricated fuel cannot be redeployed and irradiated fuel has negative residual value. Realistic revenue is about $10-25M/yr net interest margin by 2030 on a $0.3-1B book. Reaching $300M+/yr only happens post-2035 and needs a bank-scale balance sheet, so it is not venture-scale under YC timelines. The 8-week proof (a capital provider's term sheet at or below 9% on FOAK fuel) is unlikely to land. To survive, the pitch has to narrow to financing enriched UF6/HALEU UF6 inventory before fabrication.

  Counter-evidence: The best case for the idea: 10 CFR 70.44 explicitly allows liens and pledges on special nuclear material, so the structure is legal. Fuel up to and including enriched UF6 is fungible and was valued as a commodity during the Russian-ban dislocation. A narrower business could work: a specialist inventory financier or warehouse lessor for enriched UF6 and HALEU UF6, before fabrication, for developers and enrichers such as Centrus and Orano USA. Utility-style fuel VIEs (still used by Entergy) show the structure works when the counterparty is creditworthy. Developers carrying fleets on venture balance sheets (Radiant, NANO) are a real pain point. Later, once a design has several operating units and NRC licence-transfer precedent exists, module leasing could resemble rolling-stock leasing.

## All surviving hypotheses

### [chokepoint-materials] Hafnium harvest bolt-on: recover the >99% of mined hafnium that commodity zirconium chemical plants already dissolve and throw away (novelty 4, size 3, doability 3)

A modular Zr/Hf separation unit placed inside existing non-nuclear zirconium chemical plants (zirconium oxychloride, zirconia, catalyst and ceramic-pigment makers). It pulls a hafnium-rich side stream out of liquor these plants already digest, sells the hafnium at $5-13k/kg, and hands the slightly dehafniated zirconium back at no penalty to the host.

- Gap evidence: Supply side: world zircon output was about 1.2 Mt in 2025 (USGS MCS 2026, https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-zirconium-hafnium.pdf). Zircon holds 1-3% HfO2 (https://criticalstrategicmetals.com/minerals/hafnium/supply-chain/), and Hf:Zr runs about 1:50 (https://hub.truesourcemetals.com/metals/hafnium). That puts roughly 10-15 kt of hafnium inside mined zircon every year, against about 70-80 t actually recovered (https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO202506954078352.page?lang=en ; CRML puts it at 70-75 t: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/05/12/3292835/0/en/CRML-Set-to-Become-the-Market-Leader-for-Hafnium-Production-and-Supply-Security-Taking-Away-China-s-Current-75-Market-Share.html). So the recovery rate is under 1%. Hafnium is recovered only where nuclear-grade zirconium is made (https://criticalstrategicmetals.com/minerals/hafnium/supply-chain/). About 54% of zirconium goes into ceramics as undissolved flour (https://www.ceramicindustry.com/articles/93863-ceramics-account-for-54-of-zirconium-demand). The chemicals fraction, though, is already cracked into solution, so separation is the only extra step. Price side: hafnium was about $5,000/kg from 2024 to Oct 2025, then about $13,100/kg in April 2026 after China's H2-2025 export controls (https://news.metal.com/en/newscontent/103938701-from-stability-to-surge-the-overseas-hafnium-market-caught-between-ai-demand-and-supply-controls). Traders reported 12 t of unmet demand, driven by HBM/DRAM and by about 80 GW of gas-turbine orders for AI data centers (https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2740115-hafnium-approaches-two-year-high-on-fresh-demand). The headline ratio: premium zircon sells for $1,800-2,150/t (https://markets.financialcontent.com/stocks/article/marketminute-2025-12-24-the-zirconium-renaissance-nuclear-surge-and-coating-innovations-drive-market-transformation). One tonne of zircon contains about 8-15 kg of Hf, worth $40k-190k at 2025-26 prices, which is 20-100x the value of the zircon itself. Chinese producers name 'technological bottlenecks in the separation of hafnium and zirconium' as the limit on output (https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2486868-output-cuts-tight-feedstock-to-rein-in-china-s-hafnium).
- Gain: A host processing 10 kt/yr of ZrO2 equivalent contains about 150 t of HfO2 (~125 t Hf). Recovering even 20% gives about 25 t Hf/yr, roughly a third of today's world supply, worth $125-325M/yr at $5-13k/kg. The host pays nothing: zirconia users outside nuclear and electronics do not care about 1-2% versus 1.5% Hf. A selective precipitation step (OSU, 2026: separation factor 33 vs 6-7 for the MIBK industry standard, no organic solvent) makes a small, low-capex aqueous bolt-on credible (https://www.sflorg.com/2026/09/chm09232601.html).
- Market: The direct hafnium market today is about 75-150 t/yr x $5-13k/kg, or about $0.4-1.9B/yr. CPM puts electronics-and-aerospace capacity at most 140-150 t against about 130 t of demand (https://mmta.co.uk/hafnium-demand-dynamics-and-capacity-constraints/), and CRML projects demand +70% by 2030. A low-cost supplier taking 30-50% share of a 150-250 t market at $3-6k/kg (prices will fall as supply rises) earns about $200-600M/yr. Upside: the same unit can pull Sc, Nb or REE where present, and supply electronics-grade HfCl4 (semis use about 30 t/yr: https://cpmgroup.com/2020-cpm-in-the-news/the-concentration-risk-of-hafnium-a-wake-up-call-for-the-electronics-industry/).
- Prior art: Incumbents recover Hf only from nuclear-grade Zr lines: Framatome Jarrie (about 43%, expanding), ATI Albany, Westinghouse WZI Ogden, CNNC Jinghuan and other Chinese producers, and Chepetsky in Russia (https://hub.truesourcemetals.com/metals/hafnium). New entrants are all mine-based: Critical Metals' Tanbreez eudialyte with a Romanian refinery targeting 120-150 t by 2030; ASM's Dubbo project, still seeking investors (https://www.mining.com/web/strategic-materials-seeking-investors-for-dubbo-rare-earths-project/); and Quest Metals' gravity pre-concentration (https://www.questmetals.com/blog/hafnium-production-breakthrough). The OSU precipitation chemistry is academic, and its PI says it 'would likely require a start-up company and/or industry investment' (https://www.ans.org/news/2026-10-01/article-8451/study-finds-a-better-way-to-separate-zirconium-and-hafnium/). Searches for startups doing bolt-on Hf from commodity zirconium chemical streams, or for 'hafnium startup funding 2025/2026', returned nothing. Truesource notes 'no other dedicated hafnium mining or separation projects announced as of mid-2026'. Old Zr/Hf separation patents exist (e.g. US7635396B2, US6737030B2), so the chemistry is not new IP. What has not been done is the bolt-on business model at non-nuclear hosts.
- Killer risk: Price elasticity and host access. The market is thin: adding 25-100 t could push prices back toward the 2021 level of $781/kg, which caps revenue. Most zirconium-chemical capacity (oxychloride) sits in China, which has export controls and would capture the idea itself. Western hosts (Tosoh, Daiichi Kigenso, Saint-Gobain, Luxfer MEL, Tronox) are fewer and smaller. Electronics-grade purity qualification takes 12-24 months. Some Chinese oxychloride producers may already side-extract Hf when prices spike, which would partly explain China's reported 75% share.

### [chokepoint-materials] Hafnium-paid Western nuclear zirconium: a precipitation-based Zr/Hf refinery for the SMR cladding wave (novelty 3, size 4, doability 2)

Spin out and scale the 2026 OSU aqueous thiocyanate-choline Hf-selective precipitation (5x the industry separation factor, no MIBK) into a new US refinery. Its economics are inverted: hafnium is the main revenue line and nuclear-grade dehafniated zirconium sponge for SMR and new-build fuel vendors is the co-product.

- Gap evidence: Only two US facilities do industrial Zr/Hf separation, both on solvent extraction (https://www.sflorg.com/2026/09/chm09232601.html). Industry processes use MIBK, of which about 4% is lost to air, and are energy-intensive (https://www.ans.org/news/2026-10-01/article-8451/study-finds-a-better-way-to-separate-zirconium-and-hafnium/). The new process reaches a separation factor of 33 versus 6-7 (JACS 2026, Roseborough & Nyman, DOE-funded), and its PI explicitly calls for a startup. CPM's analysis says $2,500-3,000/kg Hf justifies expanded dehafniation, and $5,000-6,000/kg supports hafnium-primary production even with zirconium disposal (https://mmta.co.uk/hafnium-demand-dynamics-and-capacity-constraints/). Prices have been above that line since late 2025: about $13.1k/kg in April 2026 (https://news.metal.com/en/newscontent/103938701-from-stability-to-surge-the-overseas-hafnium-market-caught-between-ai-demand-and-supply-controls). Framatome has reportedly struggled to make electronics-spec HfCl4 (https://cpmgroup.com/2020-cpm-in-the-news/the-concentration-risk-of-hafnium-a-wake-up-call-for-the-electronics-industry/). Nuclear zirconium demand is rising: China alone took more than 2,700 t for reactor construction in 2025, and analysts expect a 50 kt zircon shortfall in 2026-27 (https://markets.financialcontent.com/stocks/article/marketminute-2025-12-24-the-zirconium-renaissance-nuclear-surge-and-coating-innovations-drive-market-transformation). The industry still frames hafnium as a nuclear byproduct, but at 2026 prices the Hf in a tonne of zircon is worth far more than the Zr.
- Gain: A precipitation route with SF 33 needs far fewer stages than SF 6-7 solvent extraction to reach <100 ppm Hf in Zr and >99% Hf purity. That means lower capex and opex, and no MIBK permit burden. Revenue per tonne of nuclear Zr: about 20 kg Hf co-produced (1:50) x $5-13k = $100k-260k. Nuclear Zr sponge sells for tens of $/kg, i.e. tens of $k per tonne. The hafnium stream can therefore subsidize Western nuclear zirconium for SMR fuel vendors and still clear margin. A 3 kt/yr Zr plant co-produces about 60 t Hf, close to doubling Western supply.
- Market: Hafnium: $0.4-1.9B/yr today, with +70% demand projected by 2030 (CRML press release: https://www.criticalmetalscorp.com/?p=1422). Nuclear Zr sponge and alloys plus aerospace and chemical-process Zr add several hundred $M/yr, and that segment grows with the SMR buildout. A 2-4 kt/yr Western refinery has a credible path to $300-600M revenue. It is capital-heavy (a chemical plant), but it can be staged with a pilot in 2027 and offtakes from Western fuel fabricators, Entegris-type precursor makers and superalloy melters.
- Prior art: Incumbent separators: Framatome Jarrie, which is investing in new separative capacity (https://hub.truesourcemetals.com/metals/hafnium); ATI; Westinghouse WZI; CNNC; Chepetsky. Mine-integrated entrants: Critical Metals (Romania refinery, 2030 target) and ASM Dubbo (unfunded). Academic: OSU JACS 2026, the MIBK-HSCN-glycine system (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07366299.2025.2600312), and extractive distillation patents (US7635396B2). I found no company licensing or commercializing the OSU precipitation chemistry, and no startup building a greenfield Western Zr/Hf separation plant. Searches for 'hafnium startup funding 2025 2026' returned only market reports.
- Killer risk: Scale-up risk of a fresh bench-scale chemistry, especially thiocyanate handling and whether the Hf-rich precipitate can be cycled to nuclear spec. A chemical plant needs $100M+ and 4-6 years, which is hard for YC. Incumbents (Framatome, ATI) could license the same IP or expand. A hafnium price crash after CRML and others come online around 2030 would collapse the inverted economics. NQA-1/ASTM qualification of new Zr sponge for cladding is slow (excluded territory covers supplier-qualification tooling, not the material itself).

### [chokepoint-materials] Fab-exhaust hafnium mining: recover Hf and Zr from chip-fab ALD/CVD precursor waste (novelty 5, size 2, doability 4)

Install capture-and-recovery cartridges on ALD/CVD tool exhaust and abatement in DRAM, HBM and logic fabs. The cartridges trap the large unreacted fraction of hafnium (and zirconium) precursors, and the company refines it back into precursor-grade HfCl4, closing a loop that today sends a $5-13k/kg metal to scrubber sludge.

- Gap evidence: Semiconductors consume about 30 t/yr of hafnium (https://cpmgroup.com/2020-cpm-in-the-news/the-concentration-risk-of-hafnium-a-wake-up-call-for-the-electronics-industry/). ALD precursor utilization is notoriously low: 'You're lucky to use one percent and typically it's much less' (Muneshwar & Cadien, via https://www.cheminst.ca/magazine/article/curtailing-inefficient-semiconductor-precursors/). Hafnium recycling today covers only targets and machining chips, via EB melting (https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO202506954078352.page?lang=en), and USGS says 'hafnium metal recycling remained minimal' (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-zirconium-hafnium.pdf). Irony: fabs pay for ultra-pure Hf separated from Zr at great cost, then remix Hf and Zr (ZrO2 is also used in DRAM capacitor stacks) in their exhaust waste. Precedent for exhaust metal recovery exists for tungsten CVD (EU LIFE96 project: https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/LIFE96ENV-B-000484/it), but I found none for hafnium.
- Gain: If even a third of the roughly 30 t/yr of semiconductor hafnium leaves tools unreacted, about 10 t/yr of Hf (worth $50-130M at $5-13k/kg) is recoverable. Fabs get a supply hedge against Chinese export controls, a lower precursor bill and a smaller hazardous-waste load. A cartridge-and-refining service can be piloted on one tool in about 8-12 weeks to measure capture yield.
- Market: The direct Hf value is about $50-150M/yr, which is capped. Extending to the same exhaust streams for other critical precursor metals (Zr, Ta, Ru, Mo, W, Co, La) and selling as fab 'precursor circularity as a service' lifts the ceiling toward $300M+. This needs a multi-metal roadmap to clear the bar.
- Prior art: Precursor makers (Entegris UltraPur HfCl4: https://www.entegris.com/content/dam/product-assets/hfcl4/datasheet-ultrapur-hfcl4-7952.pdf; Merck/EMD, Air Liquide, SK Trichem) sell precursors but I found no take-back of exhaust Hf. Precursor-recovery module patents exist (e.g. a liquid precursor recovery module, USPTO 12668874) that recycle unused liquid precursor upstream, not metal from exhaust. Process optimization that cuts precursor dose by 50% (U Alberta) competes. Supra Elemental Recovery (UT Austin spinout, $2M, backed by Rio Tinto) does modular waste-to-critical-mineral recovery but targets Ga and Sc (https://www.cbinsights.com/company/supra-elemental-recovery). Gannon & Scott recovers precious metals from semis, not Hf. Searches for 'recover hafnium semiconductor fab waste scrubber 2026' found nothing specific.
- Killer risk: Production ALD tools may already run far higher precursor utilization than the academic 0.01-1% figure, which would shrink the recoverable pool. Abatement chemistry (burn/wet scrubbers) dilutes and contaminates hafnium, making refining expensive. Fabs resist any exhaust-line hardware change because of yield and qualification risk. Hafnium alone is under $300M, so the company must become multi-metal.

### [chokepoint-materials] Thorium custody for Western rare-earth processors (novelty 3, size 1, doability 3)

A licensed thorium-receiving company that takes the radioactive thorium byproduct off new US and allied rare-earth separation plants for a fee. It removes the NRC/EPA liability that blocks Western rare-earth processing, then aggregates the thorium as a future fuel feedstock for thorium reactor developers.

- Gap evidence: NRC regulates thorium-bearing process streams, and that regulation has been 'a genuine deterrent to domestic rare earth processing investment', with thorium disposition central to the economics of Mountain Pass and Pea Ridge (https://energyfromthorium.com/2026/04/15/unr-ree-2026/). Western rare-earth buildout is accelerating after China's April 2025 controls on medium and heavy rare earths (https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/china-imposes-export-controls-on-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-materials).

### [stable-isotopes] Zr-91-stripped zirconium: isotope-tailored cladding via odd-even laser photoionization (novelty 3, size 4, doability 3)

About 71% of natural zirconium's thermal neutron absorption comes from one isotope, Zr-91, which is also zirconium's only odd-mass stable isotope. A polarization-selective laser can strip it out without touching the even isotopes. The cladding produced is chemically identical to Zircaloy, so the fleet gets roughly 2.5-7% fuel-cycle savings without having to qualify a new material. The idea was patented and dropped between 1984 and 1997, when uranium was cheap. Uranium and SWU prices have roughly doubled since then, which changes the economics.

- Gap evidence: PHYSICS: NIST data gives natural Zr sigma_a = 0.185 b. Zr-91 is 11.22% abundant with sigma_a = 1.17 b, so it contributes 0.131 b, about 71% of the total. Zr-90 is 0.011 b, Zr-92 0.22 b, Zr-94 0.050 b (https://www.ncnr.nist.gov/resources/n-lengths/elements/zr.html). Teledyne's patent states that 'nearly 60%' of Zircaloy's neutron absorption is in Zr-91 (https://patents.google.com/patent/US4487629). Zr-91 sits in the middle of the mass range (between 90 and 92), so gas centrifuges, which sort by mass, cannot cut it out efficiently. Odd/even selectivity from laser polarization plus hyperfine structure gets around this: Japanese work reached a separation coefficient of 2460 for Zr-91 with a 4-step polarized scheme (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-38423-4). ECONOMICS THEN: Westinghouse EP0104760B1 (1987) claimed a 5% fuel cost saving from removing Zr-91 (7% for Zr-90 enrichment), worth about $115 per lb of Zircaloy-4 in 1987 dollars (https://patents.google.com/patent/EP0104760B1/en). AECL filed CA2112973 in 1993 on exactly this odd-mass Zr laser photoionization method for reactor fuel efficiency. It was abandoned in 1997 (https://brevets-patents.ic.gc.ca/opic-cipo/cpd/eng/patent/2112973/summary.html). ECONOMICS NOW: In 2021, front-end fuel cost was $1,663/kg UO2: U3O8 at $94.6/kg, which is about $43/lb, and SWU at $55 (https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/economic-aspects/economics-of-nuclear-power). In 2026 the uranium spot price is $86.36/lb and US utilities paid an average of $108.70/SWU in 2025 (https://www.ans.org/news/2026-08-05/article-8270/uranium-prices-steady-as-eia-releases-annual-market-report/). The uranium and enrichment components have each roughly doubled, so each pcm of parasitic absorption removed is now worth about twice what it was. ORNL ATF neutronics put the cladding-absorption lever at about 0.1 wt% U-235: near-zero-absorption SiC needs about 0.1% less enrichment than Zircaloy (https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/neutronic-analysis-of-candidate-accident-tolerant-cladding-concep-2/). The reverse case shows the value scale: absorbing steel cladding costs 15-25% more in fuel (https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2013/09/f2/2-Pint.pdf). I found no 2015-2026 company, DOE program or paper aiming this at commercial cladding. Searches for Zr-91 or isotope-tailored zirconium alongside Hexium, ASP Isotopes or LIS Technologies returned only the 1980s-90s patents.
- Gain: Removing about 85-90% of Zr-91 cuts Zircaloy parasitic absorption by about 60%. That is worth roughly 0.06-0.1 wt% lower U-235 enrichment for the same cycle length, or longer cycles and higher burnup at the same enrichment. My estimate: about 2.5% less natural uranium, conversion and SWU per reload at minimum, and Westinghouse estimated 5-7% total fuel cost savings. At 2026 prices the front end is at least about $2,600/kg UO2. With roughly 0.25 kg of Zircaloy per kg of U in a PWR assembly, the tailored zirconium is worth about $250-400/kg to the utility. That is several times the cost of nuclear-grade Zircaloy feed. Because the alloy is chemically identical, there is no new-material qualification campaign, unlike FeCrAl or SiC. The value is higher still in natural-uranium CANDU cores, which AECL's original motivation, and in leakage-heavy HALEU SMRs and microreactors, where every pcm displaces very expensive HALEU.
- Market: Value pool: global LWR enriched fuel loading is about 7,000-8,000 tHM/yr, and the front end is worth about $20-26B/yr at 2026 prices. A 2.5-5% saving gives roughly $0.5-1.3B/yr of value created. That is my estimate, derived from the WNA cost structure and the 2026 prices above. On volume, about 2,000 t/yr of Zircaloy for LWR reloads plus CANDU pressure and calandria tubes, sold at a $150-300/kg premium, gives about $300-600M/yr of revenue at full penetration. It could extend to Zr in UZrH microreactor fuel and hydride moderators (https://neup.inl.gov/content/uploads/14/2024/07/CFA-23-29834_TechnicalAbstract_2023CFATechnicalAbstract23-29834.pdf). The same odd-isotope laser can also strip or enrich other odd isotopes, such as Gd-155/157 and Er-167 absorbers, and Cr-53 and Mo-95 in alternative claddings. It credibly clears the $300M/yr bar.
- Prior art: Teledyne US4487629 (1984): ZrI4 route plus a generic separation step (https://patents.google.com/patent/US4487629). Westinghouse EP0104760B1 (1987): IR multiphoton dissociation of Zr alkoxides (https://patents.google.com/patent/EP0104760B1/en). Further 1980s patents: US4389292 (aqueous chelate photochemistry), US4496445, US4568436 and US5024749 (https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4389292). AECL CA2112973 (1993, abandoned 1997): odd-mass laser photoionization (https://brevets-patents.ic.gc.ca/opic-cipo/cpd/eng/patent/2112973/summary.html). Laser Zr separation for the waste isotope Zr-93 (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-38423-4) and for thorium reactors in India (https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20073819). A plasma-centrifuge Zr study (https://inis.iaea.org/records/sf641-era92). Russia's MEPhI and Tsinghua pursue isotopically modified molybdenum as an alternative cladding, not Zr (https://en.cand.vn/tech-science/Russian-scientists-create-new-breakthrough-material-for-nuclear-reactors-i462133). Enriched Zr-90 is sold only in research quantities (https://www.buyisotope.com/zirconium-90-isotope.php). HONEST NOVELTY: the idea is not new. It is a well-documented concept that was abandoned. What looks new is the timing argument (the 2024-26 price doubling, Russian import bans, the HALEU premium) and the observation that every zirconium alloy needs zero requalification.
- Killer risk: Throughput engineering. It needs vaporizing and photoionizing roughly 2,000 t/yr of refractory zirconium (boiling point about 4,400 C) at under about $100-150/kg. That is AVLIS-class engineering, and AVLIS for uranium was cancelled in 1999 after about $2B. Laser isotope separation hardware is export-controlled and draws non-proliferation scrutiny even for non-uranium elements. The benefit may also be smaller than Westinghouse's 5-7% under modern ZIRLO/M5 alloys, which contain Nb and Sn; that has to be checked with OpenMC or SCALE before anything else. Fuel vendors (Westinghouse, Framatome, TVEL, which make the cladding) must agree to buy the feed. A credible 8-week proof is a lattice-physics value model at 2026 prices plus a single-pass laser selectivity demo on Zr vapor.

### [stable-isotopes] Cl-37 bulk enrichment for molten chloride fast reactors (novelty 3, size 3, doability 3)

Chloride fast reactors (TerraPower/Southern MCFR, Exodys, Stellaria, Moltex) need roughly 90-99% Cl-37, in tonnes, to avoid Cl-35 neutron capture (which forms Cl-36 waste and S-35). Today nobody produces more than gram quantities, and the only work is national-lab and 3M bench-scale R&D.

- Gap evidence: The DOE-NE project abstract (3M lead, PNNL partner) says 'there is no commercial production of chlorine-37 beyond gram quantities' and that MCFR 'will require metric tons of chlorine-37' (https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-05/ne-abstract-ARD-22-31104.pdf). PNNL's August 2026 final report (CRADA #667) on microchannel distillation shows 264 stages in a 2 m column but only about 84 g/yr projected; isotachophoresis gave about 0.63 g/yr (https://www.pnnl.gov/main/publications/external/technical_reports/PNNL-39669.pdf). PNNL's thermal-diffusion route costs about $13 per gram of H37Cl (https://gain.inl.gov/content/uploads/4/2025/10/PNNL-38411.pdf). Its listed interested parties are TerraPower, Exodys, Stellaria/Orano, NAAREA and Moltex. ORNL has a liquid-liquid process validated only at lab scale (https://www.ornl.gov/technology/202405721). The PNNL campaign is still building columns through FY26 (https://conferences.iaea.org/event/416/attachments/20998/37061/8_Bruce%20McNamara_Vienna%20FY26%20MSR%20Campaign.pdf). A PSI MCFR reference design uses 100% Cl-37 with about 329 t of actinides at 3 GWth (https://conferences.iaea.org/event/218/papers/19007/files/8035-CN291-Krepel_MCFR_final_rev.pdf). From that salt composition I estimate about 200+ t of chlorine per roughly 1.2 GWe core.
- Gain: At PNNL's $13/g, a single core inventory would cost about $2.5-3B, which makes enriched chloride reactors uneconomic. A process at about $200-500/kg turns it into a $40-110M first-core purchase plus makeup. That cost reduction is what makes these breed-and-burn chloride cores bankable. Enrichment also improves breeding and neutron economy and avoids the very-long-lived Cl-36 waste stream, which has a 301,000-year half-life (IAEA/PNNL slides above).
- Market: This depends on chloride reactors being deployed. If 3-10 GWe/yr of chloride fast reactors are built in the late 2030s at about 200 t Cl-37 per GWe and $300-1,000/kg, that is roughly $0.2-2B/yr, mostly first-core inventory. Before about 2032 it is essentially zero beyond demonstration quantities.
- Prior art: 3M (which already does chemical-exchange B-10 enrichment) with PNNL (https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-05/ne-abstract-ARD-22-31104.pdf). PNNL thermal diffusion (https://www.pnnl.gov/main/publications/external/technical_reports/PNNL-38411.pdf). ORNL liquid-liquid (https://www.ornl.gov/technology/202405721). A 2021 ANS proposal to use existing uranium centrifuge plants with HCl or CH3Cl (https://xylenepower.com/ANS%20Summer%202021%20Chloride-37%20FC%20Viewgraphs%20Final.pdf). Academic reviews (https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202408.0677). Searches for a Cl-37 startup returned nothing, and none of the TerraPower/ASP Isotopes agreements found mention chlorine.
- Killer risk: Demand timing. MCFR commercial cores are a 2035+ story, and vendors may start with natural chlorine. Urenco or Orano could run HCl or CH3Cl through centrifuge cascades once demand is real, and 3M already holds the CRADA position. This is a hard bet on one reactor family.

### [stable-isotopes] Western N-15 supply for nitride fuels (UN, MUPN) (novelty 3, size 2, doability 3)

Rosatom announced a commercial N-15 production method in April 2026 for BREST nitride fuel. The West has no bulk N-15 capability, even though Westinghouse is developing UN fuel for ATF, its LFR and SMRs, and UN fuel is uneconomic and makes C-14 waste without roughly 99% N-15.

- Gap evidence: The Rosatom Fuel Division 'has developed a commercial method for producing nitrogen-15 isotope' for MUPN fuel for BREST-OD-300, with a pilot facility at VNIINM that produced the first batch (https://rosatomnewsletter.com/2026/04/20/closed-nuclear-fuel-cycle-news/). Westinghouse is developing UN pellets for ATF, LFR and SMR (https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/development-of-advanced-high-density-uranium-nitride-fuel-pellet-/). The need for 'expensive, highly isotopically enriched 15N' is described as a significant barrier, because 14N(n,p) makes C-14 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_nitrides). The EU FREDMANS project explicitly tries to recycle N-15 because it is 'a costly raw material' (https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101060800/fr). I found no Western bulk N-15 producer or startup; current N-15 supply is lab-scale NO-distillation output for tracers.
- Gain: N-15 makes UN viable. UN has about 40% higher uranium density than UO2 and much higher thermal conductivity, which allows longer cycles and higher power density in LWRs and lead-cooled fast reactors. Removing 14N absorption also lowers required enrichment and avoids long-lived C-14 in the back end. A full LWR reload of UN would need roughly 5-6 t of N-15. That is my estimate from about 100 tHM/reload at about 5.9 wt% N in UN.
- Market: Today it is near zero outside Russia. If UN reaches even about 5% of LWR reloads plus the LFR and SMR fleets in the 2030s, demand is about 20-100 t/yr of N-15. At $1,000-5,000/kg that is roughly $20-500M/yr, and the high end needs real UN adoption. Combining it with Cl-37 (both are light-isotope, high-throughput chemical-exchange or distillation problems) gives a broader 'Gen-IV light isotopes' company with a larger ceiling.
- Prior art: Rosatom/VNIINM, now at commercial method stage (https://rosatomnewsletter.com/2026/04/20/closed-nuclear-fuel-cycle-news/). Old US feasibility work on large-scale N-15 for reactors (https://www.osti.gov/biblio/4384433). A patent on enriched N in actinide fuel (https://patents.justia.com/patent/20100166133). EU FREDMANS on recycling N-15 (https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101060800/fr). No Western startup found.
- Killer risk: Western UN fuel may never leave R&D. Westinghouse's own ATF lead products are Cr-coated Zr plus doped UO2 or LEU+. Without an anchor offtaker this is a tiny tracer-isotope business. The physics is easy (NO/HNO3 exchange and cryogenic NO distillation are known), so the moat would come from cost and scale, not invention.

### [stable-isotopes] 12C isotope-engineered polycrystalline diamond heat spreaders for AI accelerators (novelty 3, size 3, doability 4)

Polycrystalline CVD diamond grown from 99.96% carbon-12 methane conducts about 35% more heat (about 2,500 W/mK) than natural-isotope CVD diamond, which beats even natural single crystal. Nobody sells it as a thermal product, even as diamond heat spreaders enter GPU supply chains.

- Gain: +35% bulk conductivity in the same polycrystalline form factor. That makes polycrystalline wafers, which are cheap and large-area, perform like single crystal, which is small and expensive. In heat-spreader geometries larger than about 15 um, where boundary resistance does not dominate, the gain should translate into a few degrees C lower junction temperature or higher sustained clocks per GPU. At hyperscale that is worth tens of dollars per accelerator.
- Market: If diamond spreaders reach about 10-20M accelerators/yr at a $20-50 isotope premium each, that is about $200M-1B/yr. This depends on diamond becoming a standard in packaging. The feedstock (12C via CO cryogenic distillation, a nuclear-adjacent isotope separation process) must scale from kg to tens of tonnes per year, with closed-loop methane recycling in CVD reactors because CVD carbon yield is low.
- Prior art: GE patent US5540904 on isotopically pure 12C/13C polycrystalline diamond with enhanced conductivity, now expired (https://patents.google.com/patent/US5540904A/en). Element Six has sold high-purity 12C diamond for quantum use since 2009 (https://www.nextbigfuture.com/2009/04/element-six-is-global-leader-europe.html). CIL sells 12C methane for quantum use (https://isotope.com/launch-12c-methane-qg-diamond-for-quantum-applications). Thermal diamond players without an isotope product: Akash, Diamond Foundry (https://www.df.com/diamond-ai-chips), Element Six/Orbray. No company found offering isotope-enriched thermal-grade diamond.

Thermal boundary resistance and die-attach interfaces may swamp the 35% bulk gain at hot-spot scale (the Cahill webinar above notes TBR dominates for hot spots under about 15 um). Diamond packaging adoption itself is still early. The link to nuclear is weak: this is really a thermal-materials company.

- Market: Ru market is about 42 t x about $56/g, roughly $2.3B/yr at 2026 prices. Rh market is about $8B/yr at current prices. A 1,000 t/yr recycling plant yields about 1.9 t Ru plus 0.4 t Rh, about $240M/yr of metal. Orano's own estimate for a 1,000 t/yr facility is about $118M Rh, $39M Ru, $19M Pd and $30M Xe, all at older, lower prices (https://www.orano.group/usa/en/our-portfolio-expertise/orano-white-papers/potential-radioisotope-element-harvesting-from-recycling-of-unf). A refiner/marketer taking all US recyclers' PGM output could reach $300M+ revenue only when two or three plants run, in the 2030s.
- Prior art: Orano white paper on harvesting Xe, Ru, Rh, Pd, Am, Sr and REEs (URL above). UCLan/Lancashire 2025 paper on recovering strategic fission products to offset reprocessing cost (https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/54465/). Curio NuCycle claims Rh, Pd, Kr-85 and Am-241 recovery (URL above). PGM extraction research on liquid-antimony extraction ahead of vitrification (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7700306). Older platinoid-recovery literature (https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/recovery-of-value-fission-platinoids-from-spent-nuclear-fuel/). I found no company focused on the release/certification and offtake layer, and none positioning fission Ru for semiconductors (a search for fission Ru plus semiconductor supply found nothing). The Ru angle is new because of the 2026 price shock; the chemistry is not new.
- Killer risk: It depends entirely on someone else reprocessing spent fuel; no US plant runs today. Chipmakers and catalyst makers may refuse metal derived from spent fuel on reputation grounds even with zero detectable activity. Ru and Rh prices are volatile (Rh was about $29k/oz in 2021 and fell). Curio can integrate this layer itself.

### [back-end-byproducts] Fission-xenon plus mandatory Kr-85 capture as one product for US recyclers (novelty 2, size 1, doability 3)

US federal rules force any domestic recycling plant to capture krypton-85, unlike La Hague, which vents it. Sell recyclers a noble-gas train that captures Kr-85 for compliance and sells the co-captured fission xenon, which is naturally enriched in Xe-136, so the compliance cost turns into revenue.

- Gap evidence: 40 CFR 190.10(b) caps Kr-85 releases at under 50,000 Ci per GWe-yr and I-129 at 5 mCi per GWe-yr (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-190/subpart-B/section-190.10). That makes Kr/Xe capture a hard requirement for Curio, SHINE, Oklo and others. Sorbent work such as MIT/NETL/PNNL's SIFSIX-Cu MOF is still early research with no commercial deployment (https://nse.mit.edu/?p=1692), and radiation-stability studies of noble-gas sorbents were still under way in 2026 (https://gain.inl.gov/content/uploads/4/2026/05/07-P.Thallapally-Imparct-of-Radiation-on-Noble-Gas-Performance.pdf). The xenon market is about $304M (2025), driven by chips and ion thrusters (https://imarcgroup.com/xenon-gas-market). Orano estimates about $30M/yr of Xe from a 1,000 t/yr plant (Orano white paper URL above).
- Gain: Turns a pure compliance cost (cryogenic or sorbent off-gas train) into about $30M/yr of xenon revenue per 1,000 t/yr plant, partly offsetting the Kr-85 system's capex and opex. Fission Xe's high Xe-136 content is useful for double-beta-decay experiments.
- Market: Small. The global Xe market is about $300M/yr. Equipment sales are tied to a few recycling plants. The ceiling is well under $300M/yr; this is a component business, not a company.
- Prior art: Orano off-gas treatment white paper for advanced-reactor fuel (https://www.orano.group/usa/en/our-portfolio-expertise/orano-white-papers/treatment-of-off-gases-arising-from-the-treatment-of-advanced-reactor-used-nuclear-fuel). Old patents on separating Xe and Kr from reprocessing off-gas (https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4400183). PNNL/INL/ORNL sorbent programs. NuMat-style MOF companies could enter.
- Killer risk: Too few customers, too late: no US plant before about 2030, and Orano will sell its own design. The market is capped by the size of the xenon market.

### [back-end-byproducts] Unattended ISFSI: remote security and operations for spent-fuel-only sites under the 2026 NRC security modernization (novelty 2, size 1, doability 4)

Use the new NRC rule that lets general-license ISFSIs run under 10 CFR 73.51 instead of reactor-grade 73.55 security, and offer a remote-monitored, minimal-staff operating package that cuts the roughly $10M/yr cost of each stranded spent-fuel site.

- Gap evidence: The 2026 NRC 'Modernizing Security Requirements' rule lets general-license ISFSIs use 73.51 ISFSI-specific security instead of reactor 73.55 requirements, and projects $557M to $1.01B in industry savings over 30 years (https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-12989.pdf). Maine Yankee's ISFSI alone costs about $10M/yr to operate (https://maineyankee.com/docs/MaineYankee.pdf). DOE has paid about $11.1B in standard-contract damages, with future liability estimated at $37.6B to $44.5B (https://morganlewis.com/blogs/upandatom/2024/12/us-spent-fuel-storage-liability-increases-while-other-countries-develop-geologic-repositories). Spent fuel sits at about 75 sites (DOE fact sheet URL above).
- Gain: Roughly 30-60% of a stranded site's $8-10M/yr cost, assuming guard force and on-site staff are the main cost drivers. DOE reimburses much of this, so taxpayer savings are also a selling point.
- Market: Capped. About 20+ ISFSI-only sites at about $10M/yr gives roughly $200-250M/yr of total spend. NRC's own estimate of the rule's savings is about $45-51M/yr. Realistic revenue ceiling is well under $100M/yr.
- Prior art: Owners of decommissioned sites (Holtec, NorthStar, EnergySolutions) and security contractors (G4S/Allied) will capture the savings in-house. I found no dedicated 'unattended ISFSI' startup, but the incumbents are the natural owners.
- Killer risk: The market is capped and the owners will do it themselves. Because DOE reimburses the costs, utilities have little incentive to cut them.

### [back-end-byproducts] Depleted-uranium redox flow batteries sited behind the fence at licensed nuclear facilities (novelty 3, size 2, doability 1)

Use the US's roughly 750,000 t DOE depleted-uranium stockpile, a material with negative value that DOE pays to dispose of, as a free active material for flow batteries. Deploy them only at sites that already hold NRC licenses, such as enrichment and fuel plants and reactor sites that firm power for data centers, to avoid the licensing problem of putting uranium in the open market.

- Gap evidence: In March 2025 JAEA demonstrated a uranium-anode/iron-cathode flow cell: 1.3 V, 10 cycles with almost no degradation, and a concept of 650 t U for 30 MWh (https://spectrum.ieee.org/depleted-uranium-battery; https://sj.jst.go.jp/news/202503/n0331-01n.html). The US holds about 750,000 t DU in DOE storage and Japan about 16,000 t (same IEEE source). I found no company or US program commercializing it; expert commentary in the same article doubts its competitiveness.
- Gain: Active material is free or even paid for (avoided DU disposal cost), versus vanadium flow batteries, whose electrolyte is largely vanadium and whose supply is concentrated in China and Russia. Deploying at licensed sites reuses existing radiological controls.
- Market: Flow batteries at nuclear and industrial sites could in theory reach a multi-hundred-million-dollar market, but the credible near-term market is near zero until the chemistry is proven.
- Prior art: JAEA prototype (URLs above). Academic actinide redox flow-battery work dating to the 2000s. No startups found.
- Killer risk: The chemistry is unproven at scale: a nonaqueous electrolyte, only 1.3 V, and 10 cycles. Uranium chemical toxicity and NRC Part 40 licensing limit where it can be sited, and lithium and vanadium costs keep falling. It is probably a science project, not a company.

### [finance-insurance-markets] AerCap for microreactors: an independent lessor that owns factory-built reactor modules (novelty 4, size 4, doability 2)

Own transportable microreactor and SMR modules and lease them, NRC-approved and fuelled, to data centers, mines, military bases and utilities. Vendors stay manufacturers (the Boeing role) instead of carrying fleets on venture balance sheets, and the lessor earns from rent plus redeploying modules to new sites.

- Gap evidence: Today every microreactor vendor plans to own and operate its units and sell power-as-a-service, funded by its own equity. Radiant raised a $300M Series D in Dec 2025 to build Kaleidos units (https://www.axios.com/pro/climate-deals/2025/12/17/radiant-microreactor-300-million-series-d), and NANO Nuclear's SEC filings describe the same model (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001923891/000149315226037345/form10-q.htm). Searches for an independent nuclear lessor or an aircraft-leasing analogy found nothing commercial. The only academic financing-vehicle paper (Penn State ICDS) covers pooling, securitisation and government CDS, and does not mention leasing (https://www.icds.psu.edu/de-risking-the-commercialization-of-advanced-nuclear-reactors-through-innovative-financing-vehicles-faculty-junior-researcher-collaboration-opportunity). Passive lessor ownership of NRC-licensed reactors has legal precedent: the 1986 Palo Verde sale-leasebacks, where lessors were NRC-approved owner participants (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000764622/000095014797000207/0000950147-97-000207.txt). Aviation shows how large the separated-ownership model can get: AerCap paid more than $30B for GECAS in 2021 (https://www.boersen-zeitung.de/unternehmen-branchen/aercap-zahlt-mehr-als-30-mrd-dollar-fuer-gecas-cf7dd0f6-81a4-11eb-a53b-b599d0c67797).
- Gain: Vendors swap 10-20 year utility-style balance sheets for recurring manufacturing sales, so they stop burning equity on every unit and their cost of capital drops. Customers sign shorter, cancellable leases instead of 20-year PPAs, which suits uncertain AI-demand horizons and mine lives. The lessor captures residual value by moving modules between sites, which a single-site owner cannot do. Rough numbers (my estimate): a 5-10 MWe unit costing about $50-100M, leased at 10-12% a year, brings in about $5-12M per unit per year.
- Market: My estimate: if 500-1,000 microreactor and SMR modules ship by about 2040 (vendors' public pipelines are larger), at $50-500M each, the asset base is $50-200B+. Lessors in aviation own roughly half the fleet, and lease yields are about 8-12%, so a dominant lessor has a path to more than $1B a year in revenue. The ceiling depends on deployment actually happening.
- Prior art: No independent nuclear-module lessor found in searches. Vendor self-ownership: Radiant, Last Energy, NANO (links above). Developers such as Blue Energy, which Constellation invested in (https://www.nucnet.org/news/us-nuclear-leader-constellation-makes-strategic-investment-in-startup-smr-developer-7-5-2026), and NuScale/ENTRA1 (https://www.power-eng.com/nuclear/smrs/nuscale-partners-with-habboush-group-entra1-on-smr-deployment/) own projects, not portable assets. IAEA has old literature on leasing floating plants (https://inis.iaea.org/records/1v2js-twc96).
- Killer risk: No units exist to lease until about 2028-2030. Vendors may refuse to give up the operating economics. Residual value depends on NRC allowing licensed modules to move between sites, an approach that is still being developed. Price-Anderson places liability on licensees, so the lessor may have to be a licensee. In 8 weeks a startup could only prove demand, through LOIs from vendors and offtakers and a term sheet from an aircraft-leasing financier, not assets.

### [finance-insurance-markets] HALEU first-core fuel lessor ("nuclear fuel trust" revived for advanced reactors) (novelty 4, size 3, doability 2)

A specialty finance company that owns HALEU and TRISO fuel inventory and leases cores to advanced-reactor owners. This brings back the off-balance-sheet nuclear fuel trusts utilities used in the 1980s-2000s, for a fuel that costs about 10x more per kg.

- Gap evidence: HALEU is estimated at $23,725/kgU (oxide) to $25,725/kgU (metal) (https://nuclearinnovationalliance.org/sites/default/files/2023-12/NIA%20HALEU%20Cost%20Report%20SPM%20%2812_15_23%29.pdf). TerraPower and X-energy alone need about 20 t to start their demonstrations (https://www.reutersevents.com/nuclear/terrapowers-natrium-schedule-fuel-remains-concern), which works out to roughly $500M of fuel working capital before any revenue. INL projects about 520 MTU/yr of HALEU by mid-century (https://gain.inl.gov/content/uploads/4/2024/11/Characterizing-an-Emerging-Market-for-HALEU-Production.pdf), about $12-13B/yr at today's cost. That INL market study covers government offtake and production-services structures only. It says nothing about who will hold title to or finance fuel inventory. Utilities used nuclear fuel lease trusts historically (e.g. FirstEnergy and Indiana Michigan Power SEC filings: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001031296/000095015200008145/l83479aex99-d1.txt, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000050172/000000490400000071/0000004904-00-000071.txt). Searches found no equivalent for HALEU. New fuel companies such as Standard Nuclear raise equity for production, not customer fuel finance (https://businesswire.com/news/home/20260126420607/en/Standard-Nuclear-Begins-Production-of-Advanced-HALEU-Fuel-and-Raises-%24140-Million-in-Series-A-Funding).
- Gain: Takes $100M-$1B per plant of fuel capex off developer balance sheets and turns it into a per-MWh lease cost. Fuel-only credit is cheaper than venture equity because the fuel can be repossessed before irradiation. Cutting the cost of capital on fuel from about 15-20% (equity) to about 7-9% (secured) is worth tens of millions of dollars a year per plant.
- Market: My estimate: about $1-3B/yr of HALEU purchases in the 2030s, rising toward $10B+/yr by 2050. A lessor financing 20-30% at a 3-5% net spread over cost of funds, with the book compounding, gives a path to more than $300M/yr of revenue by the late 2030s.
- Prior art: Historical utility fuel trusts (SEC links above). DOE's HALEU allocation program supplies first cores for some awardees (https://www.ans.org/news/article-7325/doe-allocates-haleu-to-antares-standard-nuclear-and-acunatura/). Commodity traders (Curzon, Nukem, Traxys) do uranium loans and inventory finance for U3O8 and LEU, but I found no HALEU or TRISO core leasing. Centrus signs supply deals with Radiant (https://www.placera.se/pressmeddelanden/centrus-energy-centrus-and-radiant-announce-long-term-partnership-for-domestic-haleu-to-supply-kaleidos-microreactors-20260909) without financing them. Not to be confused with HALEU midstream (excluded): this is pure finance, not enrichment or deconversion.
- Killer risk: Once fuel is irradiated it has little or no resale value, so the lessor carries credit risk on the reactor owner, not collateral risk. DOE may keep subsidising first cores. Title and safeguards rules for privately held HALEU (Category II material) add cost. Supply is so scarce that any fuel that exists gets bought by vendors directly. A startup must raise a large debt facility, which is hard to prove in 8 weeks beyond a term sheet and one anchor customer.

### [finance-insurance-markets] First advanced-nuclear MLP: a tax-advantaged public owner of contracted advanced reactors (novelty 4, size 4, doability 1)

Use the new OBBBA rule, effective tax years after 2025, that makes electricity from advanced nuclear facilities qualifying income for publicly traded partnerships. Build the first nuclear MLP or yieldco, buying operating or late-construction advanced reactors with hyperscaler contracts and recycling developer capital, as MLPs did for midstream oil and gas.

- Gap evidence: OBBBA (signed July 4, 2025) added "the production of electricity from certain advanced nuclear facilities" to MLP qualifying income for tax years beginning after Dec 31, 2025 (https://natlawreview.com/article/big-beautiful-bill-and-mlp-qualifying-income?amp; https://insights.nucleationcapital.com/legislation/obbba-2025). Searches found no sponsor that has announced or filed for a nuclear MLP as of Oct 2026. Developers are raising money through SPACs and IPOs instead: newcleo at a $2.4B Nasdaq deal (https://www.ajot.com/news/nuclear-firm-newcleo-to-list-on-nasdaq-in-a-deal-valuing-it-at-2.4-billion), X-energy's S-1 (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002088896/000110465926043799/tm2527015-17_s1a.htm). 45Y/48E credits remain available for nuclear starting construction before 2034, while wind and solar credits end after 2027 (same Nucleation Capital source), so contracted nuclear is one of the few long-lived credit-bearing asset classes left.
- Gain: No entity-level tax plus access to yield-seeking retail capital, which could lower equity cost on operating nuclear by 200-400 bps (my estimate, by analogy with midstream MLP and yieldco spreads). For a $5B plant, each 100 bps of lower cost of capital is about $50M/yr.
- Market: As a sponsor or manager: management fees plus incentive distribution rights on a vehicle that could own $20-50B of contracted nuclear by the late 2030s (my estimate). Midstream MLP sponsors earned hundreds of millions to billions a year from incentive distribution rights at peak.
- Prior art: No nuclear MLP found. Analogues: NextEra Energy Partners and Brookfield Renewable (renewable yieldcos, no MLP status). Advanced-nuclear developers own their own assets (Elementl, Blue Energy, The Nuclear Company). Big infrastructure funds (Brookfield owns Westinghouse) could do this first.
- Killer risk: There are essentially no operating advanced nuclear facilities to own before about 2030-2031 (Natrium grid date 2031: https://neutronbytes.com/2025/11/20/terrapower-natrium-reactor-on-the-grid-by-2031/). Incumbents (Brookfield, Constellation, KKR) can launch one the day assets exist. Not YC-shaped: needs an asset base and investment-bank sponsorship. Its most realistic role is as the eventual take-out buyer behind hypotheses 1 and 2.

### [finance-insurance-markets] Decommissioning and end-of-life assurance wrap for venture-backed microreactor licensees (novelty 3, size 2, doability 3)

A surety plus pre-placed liability-transfer product that satisfies NRC decommissioning financial assurance for small advanced-reactor licensees with no investment-grade parent. It combines a surety bond with a contracted end-of-life buyback or decommissioning by a specialist, so startups don't lock cash in prepayment trusts for every unit.

- Gap evidence: NRC's draft DG-1476 (Sept 2026, proposed Rev. 4 of RG 1.159) gives no microreactor-specific formula. It says Part 53 plants 'may' use generic power-reactor formulas or site-specific estimates, with the same prepayment, sinking fund, surety and parent-guarantee methods as large reactors (https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2534/ML25344A146.pdf). Clyde & Co's April 2026 next-generation nuclear review lists 'decommissioning guarantees' as a needed insurance product (https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2026/april/next-generation-nuclear-global-trends-insurance-ri). Today liability transfer happens only at shutdown, through Holtec, NorthStar and EnergySolutions acquiring retired plants with their trusts (https://riskandinsurance.com/decommissionings-open-opportunities-underwriters/; Holtec's $2.98B fleet trust report: https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2608/ML26085A513.pdf).
- Gain: Frees the decommissioning prepayment cash per unit for a 1-3% a year premium (my estimate). Across a fleet of hundreds of units this is billions of dollars of startup equity released, and it lets licensees without an investment-grade parent qualify at all.
- Market: My estimate: about 1,000 small units with $20-60M decommissioning obligations each gives $20-60B of face value. At a 1-2% premium that is $200M-1.2B/yr of premium, with an MGA keeping about 15-25%. Plausible at the low end of the >$300M bar only if microreactors deploy at scale.
- Prior art: Old patents on nuclear decommissioning insurance products: US 6772128 and 7050985 (https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6772128, https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7050985). Sureties already write decommissioning bonds for NRC materials licensees. Big brokers (Marsh, WTW, Aon) cover advanced nuclear generally (https://www.wtwco.com/en-ke/insights/2026/03/evaluating-risk-for-advanced-nuclear). I found no product built for microreactor fleets.
- Killer risk: The market only exists if hundreds of units are licensed. Sureties may demand full collateral from startups, which defeats the purpose. NRC may let vendors use a fleet-level parent guarantee, or set small per-unit amounts that shrink premium volume. Easily copied by Marsh or Aon once the demand is visible.

### [finance-insurance-markets] Data-driven completion and cost-overrun MGA for first-of-a-kind nuclear builds (novelty 2, size 3, doability 2)

A managing general agent (MGA) that underwrites completion and cost-overrun layers on nuclear construction, priced with a proprietary megaproject cost and schedule model, and backed by Lloyd's and Bermuda capacity. It aims to fill the gap Congress is trying to fill with a $3.6B federal backstop.

- Gap evidence: NEI has lobbied for cost-overrun protection (https://www.energyintel.com/00000190-799f-d424-abb4-ffffc6fe0000). Sen. Risch's $3.6B federal cost-overrun insurance bill was reintroduced but has not been enacted (https://www.energyintel.com/0000019c-6d42-dd1b-a9dc-edd677db0000; https://eenews.net/articles/risch-floats-federal-backstop-for-over-budget-nuclear-reactors). That suggests private capacity for the overrun layer is missing. Meanwhile the conventional construction market has reopened: Marsh placed construction insurance for TerraPower Kemmerer, oversubscribed, in Mar 2026 (https://coverager.com/marsh-secures-insurance-for-advanced-nuclear-plant/), and Markel and WTW launched a nuclear property facility in May 2026 (https://www.insurancenews.com.au/international/markel-and-willis-go-nuclear-together). So the capital is willing, but the overrun risk is not being priced.
- Gain: Lenders price first-of-a-kind cost overrun as equity risk. Insuring a layer above, say, a 20% contingency could move project debt from roughly 50% to 70%+ of capital, lowering the cost of capital by 100-300 bps on $5-15B projects (my estimate), about $50-300M/yr per project.
- Market: My estimate: $30-60B/yr of US nuclear construction spend in the 2030s, if the administration's 10-large-reactor and SMR push materialises. Insuring 10-20% as overrun layers at 3-6% rate-on-line gives $100-700M/yr of premium. MGA revenue (commission plus profit share) is about $30-150M/yr, so the company is borderline against the >$300M bar unless it takes risk on its own balance sheet.
- Prior art: Tokio Marine HCC GX already markets SMR cover for 'construction delays, technology performance shortfalls and regulatory approval challenges' (https://gxprod.tmhcc.com/Products/Small-Modular-Reactors). Ryan MGU writes cost-overrun cover for environmental cleanup (https://www.intelligentinsurer.com/ryan-mgu-cleans-up-with-product-aimed-at-environmental-cost-overruns). MIT thesis on nuclear cost overrun insurance (https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/133049). This is the least novel of the survivors.
- Killer risk: Moral hazard and Vogtle-sized tail losses ($17B+ overruns) scare off reinsurers. Tokio Marine and the big brokers are already in this space. A federal backstop, if enacted, crowds the product out. Loss data is too thin to make a 'proprietary model' credible.

### [operations-economics] Nuclear fuel inventory leasing: a vehicle that pays uranium investors a yield (novelty 3, size 4, doability 3)

Buy enriched uranium product (EUP) and fresh-fuel inventory and lease it just in time to merchant reactor operators. The money comes from investors who already hold idle physical uranium for no yield, and the business earns the spread between the utilities' ~7%+ cost of capital and what those investors would accept.

- Gap evidence: Fleet operators now carry far more fuel inventory than before the Russian ban. US owners and operators held 126M lb U3O8e at the end of 2024, up 11% in a year, bought at a 2024 weighted average of $52.71/lb (https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/articles/2024-us-figures-show-increase-in-domestic-u-purchases). In the EU, most utilities hold more than two reloads, and over 60% of that inventory is enriched uranium or fresh fuel, the most expensive forms (https://wnn.world-nuclear.org/articles/agency-sees-progress-and-challenges-for-eu-fuel-su). Meanwhile physical-uranium vehicles keep raising money to hold yellowcake that earns nothing (SPUT raises: https://sprott.com/investor-relations/press-releases/sprott-physical-uranium-trust-raises-us-2555-million-through-non-brokered-private-placement/ and https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/electric-power/061625-spot-uranium-jumps-almost-9-as-physical-fund-announces-100-million-fund-raise). Nuclear fuel leases were common in US utility finance around 1979-80 (CPUC decisions: https://files.cpuc.ca.gov/LegacyCPUCDecisionsAndResolutions/Decisions/Decisions_D86501_to_D93896_D8201001_to_D8406066/D92555_19801230_A60046.pdf) but have since largely disappeared. I found no current product that leases enriched or fresh fuel to operators using investor capital.
- Gain: Rough estimate: $7-14M/yr per GW unit. Inventory of roughly $100-200M per unit (pre-bought U3O8, UF6 and EUP at converters, enrichers and fabricators) at 7% carrying cost costs $7-14M/yr. Leasing at 3-4% saves an operator about $3-7M/yr per unit. Across the US fleet, the uranium component alone (126M lb at about $50-80/lb, so $6-10B) implies a $250-500M/yr spread pool before counting the enrichment and fabrication value in EUP. Higher SWU prices make it bigger.
- Market: Global utility fuel inventories are plausibly $30-60B including enrichment value: the US is about 20% of world demand, and EU utilities hold more than two reloads. At $10-20B under management with a 1.5-2.5% net spread, revenue is $150-500M/yr. That is a credible path past $300M, but only as a balance-sheet or asset-management business, not software.
- Prior art: Trading houses (Curzon, Traxys) and producers already do uranium loans and title swaps at converters and enrichers. Searches turned up no investor-funded leasing of EUP or fresh fuel. Sprott/SPUT and Yellow Cake hold U3O8 and UF6 but I found no evidence that they lend to utilities; SPUT's mandate likely restricts lending, which is not verified. Kazatomprom launched a uranium fund (https://www.energyintel.com/0000017c-a83f-d387-a97c-fc7f12340000). The UEC warehoused-uranium program holds inventory for itself (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1334933/000143774925029761/uec20250731_10k.htm). The 1980s utility fuel trusts are the closest historical analog.
- Killer risk: Regulated utilities, about half of US output, earn a rate-base return on fuel inventory, so they gain nothing by moving it off their books. Only merchant fleets (Constellation, Vistra, Talen, PSEG) and EU operators are natural buyers, and the investment-grade ones have cheap capital already. The lessor carries uranium and SWU price risk and counterparty and title complexity for material sitting at fabricators. Incumbents such as Sprott or the traders could copy it once it is proven. It needs a large fund, not a YC-style product. The Fuel-cycle and Russia-ban dynamics could reverse inventory build-ups.

### [operations-economics] Performance-based security conversion for the operating fleet (novelty 2, size 3, doability 3)

Package what an operating plant needs to voluntarily adopt the NRC's June 2026 performance-based physical-security alternatives: sensors, remote and unmanned systems, adversary simulation evidence, and law-enforcement integration. Get paid as a share of the guard-force savings.

- Gap evidence: Security is about 10% of plant O&M (INL: https://inldigitallibrary.inl.gov/sites/sti/sti/Sort_17304.pdf). Operating cost is $20.43/MWh (NEI 2024 Costs in Context: https://www.nei.org/getContentAsset/47fa8caa-9b0d-4029-932c-07f902e82f4f/8d8ff8d6-b2ae-401b-a63c-f6b108e809d2/2024-Costs-in-Context-final.pdf), so US security spend is roughly $1.5B/yr. On 26 June 2026 the NRC proposed 'Modernizing Security Requirements', which moves from prescriptive to performance-based, technology-inclusive criteria. Existing licensees may adopt it voluntarily but are not required to (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/26/2026-12989/modernizing-security-requirements ; https://www.ans.org/news/article-8151/nrc-proposes-security-regulation-changes/). UCS says it would let owners reduce armed on-site forces and rely on local law enforcement (https://www.ucs.org/about/news/newly-proposed-rules-expose-us-nuclear-power-plants-terrorism-vulnerability). The NRC's own estimate counts only $45-52M/yr of administrative savings, which suggests the larger protective-strategy savings are unpriced. DOE LWRS still lists deploying remote weapons systems at even one commercial site as a goal (https://lwrs.inl.gov/advanced-security-technologies/).
- Gain: Assumption-based: a 20-30% cut in security spend at a $15-30M/yr site is $3-9M per site per year. That is far above $1M per unit, and it recurs for 40+ years of licence life. Entry-control redesign alone at Diablo Canyon was estimated at about $0.7M in savings (https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/imesp/148).
- Market: About $1.5B/yr of US security spend. With roughly 54 sites, a 30% savings share at a 20% cut is about $90M/yr in the US. Canada, Europe, Asia and new builds (where security design is a cost lever from day one) could take it toward $300M+.
- Prior art: The Nuclear Company launched NOS Security in May 2026: AI monitoring, drones, robotics and unified command, aimed at 73.54/73.55 compliance and seeking partnerships with operating utilities (https://interestingengineering.com/energy/us-nuclear-safety-power-plant). Curtiss-Wright and Teledyne FLIR have offered unmanned systems to the nuclear market since 2022 (https://www.potomacofficersclub.com/news/curtiss-wright-teledyne-to-offer-unmanned-solutions-to-nuclear-power-market/). Sandia and INL run remote-weapons and RISA simulation work (https://www.sandia.gov/labnews/?p=14578 ; https://lwrs.inl.gov/content/uploads/11/2024/03/Physical_Security_Simulation_Software_App_RISA.pdf). ARES Security's AVERT simulation is widely used, and guard-force contractors (Allied Universal, G4S) operate many sites. The niche that looks open is a contract that guarantees savings and converts a site to the new rule, rather than another technology vendor.
- Killer risk: The rule is only proposed; comments closed 27 July 2026, and the final rule and its scope may be weakened under UCS and political pressure. Adoption is voluntary and utilities are very conservative. Guard unions and contractors will resist. TNC and the incumbents already target the same window. Sales cycles are long, and force-on-force validation is needed before any headcount comes out.

### [operations-economics] Measured thermal-discharge headroom for once-through plants (weak survivor) (novelty 2, size 2, doability 3)

Measure the real downstream river or sea temperature and plume mixing continuously, and use that evidence to win dynamic or adaptive thermal-discharge permits and cut heat-wave derates that conservative mixing models now force.

- Gap evidence: In France, July 2025 heat restrictions cost 5-6 TWh and more than EUR 400M (https://www.bluefieldresearch.com/research/cooling-water-thresholds-choke-europe-nuclear-operators-call-for-upgrades/). In July 2026, 3.65 GWe were offline at once (https://www.neimagazine.com/news/extreme-heat-impacts-french-nuclear/ ; https://euronews.com/2026/06/25/france-takes-nuclear-reactors-offline-amid-record-heatwave). EDF has an EUR 8.7B, 15-year climate adaptation plan (same NEI link). Browns Ferry lost more than $50M in 2010 and TVA spent about $160M on cooling retrofits (https://insideclimatenews.org/news/20110414/southeast-extreme-heat-growing-concern-nuclear-power-operators).
- Gain: Event-driven: about $1M/day per affected unit (TVA figure) and hundreds of millions of euros per French summer. Even recovering 10-20% of derated hours is worth $1-10M per affected unit-year at exposed sites.
- Market: Capped. Roughly 100-150 once-through river and coastal units worldwide are exposed. Monitoring, modelling and permitting software is plausibly a $30-80M/yr market. It only exceeds $300M if it grows into cooling hardware, where the incumbents are.
- Prior art: ORNL has released an AI model for US river temperatures (https://www.ornl.gov/news/ai-model-delivers-river-temperature-insights-strengthening-us-energy-security). EDF has large in-house hydro-thermal forecasting. Infinite Cooling works with EDF on tower water recovery (https://www.power-eng.com/nuclear/edf-infinite-cooling-partner-up-to-reduce-water-consumption-in-nuclear-plants/). Hardware incumbents include John Cockerill Hamon, SPX, Sulzer and Xylem. Arbol mentions nuclear thermal risk but offers no specific product (https://arbol.io/post/how-temperature-volatility-threatens-businesses-parametric-solutions-for-the-energy-sector-and-beyond).
- Killer risk: It fails the market-size filter unless it becomes hardware. Regulators may not loosen ecological limits during heat waves, and EDF builds this kind of capability in-house.

### [radiation-applications] Resistance-proof e-beam grain disinfestation utility for state grain reserves and export elevators (novelty 3, size 4, doability 3)

Inline MeV electron-beam treatment at grain intake and outturn (hundreds of t/h) that kills or sterilises stored-grain insects in seconds, sold per tonne to the grain stockpiles where phosphine resistance is now 72-100%. Start with state reserves (India FCI, Indonesia Bulog), where import and labelling rules do not apply.

- Gap evidence: (1) On 30 Sep 2026 ICAR and FCI signed an MoU to find fumigation alternatives because '72-100 per cent of storage insects in grain warehouses are resistant' to phosphine. The options they are testing are N2, CO2 and CO2+phosphine; irradiation is not on the list (https://www.cavalier.in/cds-ota-current-affairs/2026-10-01/icar-fci-fumigation-pest-resistance-grain-storage-2026). Indian resistance is also documented at https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IndFarm/article/view/148819. (2) In Australia, strong phosphine resistance rose more than fivefold, from 1.8% to 9.6% in the east and from 0.4% to 2.2% in the west, over 2000-2021 (https://groundcover.grdc.com.au/grain-storage/management/users-must-heed-rising-phosphine-resistance). (3) Indonesia's BRIN and Bulog started an e-beam rice-weevil pilot in Feb-Mar 2026, but at only 4-5 t/day at 1-3 kGy (https://rri.co.id/en/technology/2229725/brin-bulog-explore-nuclear-irradiation-to-preserve-national-rice-stocks). That shows demand pull, and nobody is supplying at elevator scale. (4) Physics and economics: FDA permits up to 1 kGy for arthropod disinfestation (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-179/subpart-B/section-179.26). Already in 1962 USDA put the energy cost of grain e-beam treatment at about 0.013 cents/lb, with capex as the dominant cost (https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/312262/files/mrr531.pdf). A Soviet-era port elevator was reported running radiation disinfestation at 400 t/h (title: 'Radiation disinfestation of grain in a port elevator with capacity of 400 T/h', https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/6983500), so the throughput has been shown before. I found no current commercial operator.
- Gain: Rough calculation, to be checked: 1 kGy is 1 kJ/kg, about 0.3 kWh of beam energy per tonne, so the energy is cents per tonne. A 100-200 kW industrial accelerator could treat several hundred t/h. Replaces repeated phosphine fumigation (labour, re-entry delays, failures from resistance) and removes residue and worker-exposure issues. Losses avoided: Indian public stocks run to tens of millions of tonnes (https://www.cavalier.in/cds-ota-current-affairs/2026-10-01/icar-fci-fumigation-pest-resistance-grain-storage-2026), and even a 1% loss at about $250/t is $25M+ a year per 10 Mt stored.
- Market: Grain fumigants are a $1.91B market in 2025, forecast to reach $2.63B by 2030 (https://www.gii.co.jp/report/tbrc1775983-grain-fumigants-global-market-report.html). Global grain trade is 449 Mt in 2025/26 (https://millermagazine.com/blog/igc-record-2025-26-grain-crop-wheat-tightens-in-2026-27-6641). At a $1-2/t service fee on even 25% of traded grain plus state reserves, revenue would be $0.3-1B a year. The model is a build-own-operate per-tonne utility, not equipment sales.
- Prior art: Soviet/Ukrainian port-elevator e-beam plant (400 t/h) and a Radiation Physics and Chemistry review 'The use of electron accelerators for radiation disinfestation of grain' (https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20281499). Patents: US3360646A gamma bulk grain (https://patents.google.com/patent/US3360646A/en) and US7513193B2 bulk-material irradiation with a reject path (https://patents.google.com/patent/US7513193). Buhler/Comet LEEB is low-energy surface pasteurisation of grains and spices, which cannot reach insect eggs inside kernels (https://reports.comet-group.com/16/en/04_07_story_5-2/). E-VITA/e-ventus electron seed dressing works on seed only, about 15,000 t/yr (https://www.fep.fraunhofer.de/en/press_media/Pressemitteilungen2021/06_2021.html). A KIPT RF grain disinfestation design exists (https://www.kipt.kharkov.ua/conferences/ihepnp/17workshop_res/N3/41_3.pdf). I found no venture-backed company doing MeV bulk grain disinfestation.
- Killer risk: Market access and acceptance. The EU authorises irradiation only for dried herbs and spices (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/1999/3), and US retail labelling applies (21 CFR 179.26(c)). Sub-lethal doses leave sterile but live insects, which breach importers' nil-live-insect specs, so lethal doses (around 1-3 kGy) and dosimetry at high flow rates are needed. E-beam gives no residual protection, so reinfestation is possible in leaky storage. Cheap hermetic N2/CO2 storage is the competing 'resistance-proof' option. Selling first to state stockpiles avoids the import problems; that buyer is slow-moving but there is a 2026 MoU window.

### [radiation-applications] Co-60-free modular X-ray/e-beam irradiation units for India's 50-unit food irradiation programme (novelty 2, size 3, doability 3)

Standardised, containerised X-ray/e-beam irradiators plus an operating company, built for India's subsidised 50-unit rollout and onion, potato and spice buffer stocks. Avoids gamma-source logistics and turns a subsidy into a recurring service business.

- Gap evidence: In Dec 2025 the Government of India invited proposals for 50 food irradiation units, 14 already approved, through the Kisan Sampada portal (https://www.newsonair.gov.in/government-invites-proposals-for-setting-up-food-testing-labs-and-irradiation-units-across-country). Rs 1,000 crore is allocated under PMKSY. India has only 19 operational plants (4 US-export certified), loses 30-40% of fruit and vegetables after harvest, and names 'high infrastructure costs' and weak supply-chain integration as the barriers (https://agronfoodprocessing.com/indias-food-irradiation-push-faces-infrastructure-and-adoption-challenges/). Large-scale radiation processing of onions has already been planned (https://www.freshplaza.com/asia/article/9628958/india-plans-big-scale-radiation-processing-of-onions). Separately, Co-60 supply is tight and Canada-dominated: Canada processes more than 90% of global Co-60, and domestic US supply does not meet demand (https://www.ans.org/news/2025-11-12/article-7531/co60-tariffs-would-undermine-us-healthcare-sswg-warns/). That favours source-free machine irradiators.
- Gain: Onion storage losses in India run 25-40% (sector-wide post-harvest loss of 30-40% per the source above). Low-dose sprout inhibition, which FDA allows up to 1 kGy (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-179/subpart-B/section-179.26), could recover a large share of buffer-stock losses. Machine sources remove Co-60 procurement and replenishment and the security licensing burden.
- Market: The near-term programme alone is about Rs 1,000 crore (~$115M) of subsidy plus private co-investment for 50 units. Recurring per-tonne service across onion and potato buffer stocks, spices and export mangoes could plausibly reach $100-300M a year in India. A path past $300M needs replication across Southeast Asia, Africa and MENA. This rests on assumptions; I found no bottom-up market data.
- Prior art: Incumbent accelerator vendors (IBA, Mevex and others) and Indian public-sector designs from BARC/BRIT. India already runs 19 plants, several of them private (https://agronfoodprocessing.com/indias-food-irradiation-push-faces-infrastructure-and-adoption-challenges/). An IAEA programme on e-beam/X-ray for food (DEXAFI) exists (https://www.iaea.org/node/43964). I found no India-focused venture-backed modular X-ray operator, but the space is not empty.
- Killer risk: Subsidy-driven demand may go to low-cost local integrators and gamma plants. Farmers and traders may not pay per-kg fees outside government buffer stocks. Uptake depends on utilisation of hub plants, which the cited article flags as weak. It is low-margin infrastructure, which makes it hard to get to venture scale.

### [radiation-applications] Low-dose X-ray sprout control for processing potatoes (post-CIPC) (novelty 3, size 2, doability 4)

In-line 50-200 Gy X-ray treatment at storage intake for fry and chip processors. Replaces chlorpropham (CIPC) and its costly substitutes, and holds sprouting at zero for the whole storage season.

- Gap evidence: CIPC is banned in the EU, and US exporters report a growing number of export markets refusing CIPC-treated potatoes. EPA still registers CIPC, but more than 70% of the 400M cwt US fall crop goes into storage, and storage losses run 10-15% (https://spudman.com/article/searching-for-sprouting-solutions/). The spudman article does not mention irradiation at all. A 2015 Penn State study found that 200 Gy e-beam fully prevented sprouting at every storage temperature while keeping health-promoting compounds (https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-use-of-low-dose-electron-beam-irradiation-and-storage-conditi). FDA permits up to 1 kGy for growth and maturation inhibition (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-179/subpart-B/section-179.26). UK AHDB reviews of CIPC alternatives cover chemicals and oils, not irradiation (https://horticulture.ahdb.org.uk/knowledge-library/alternatives-to-cipc-for-sprout-control-in-potatoes).
- Gain: One treatment replaces repeated fogging of spearmint oil, DMN or ethylene through the season. If sprouting is fully suppressed, processors may be able to store at warmer temperatures, which can lower reducing sugars in fry stocks. That last point is a hypothesis that needs agronomic validation; I did not verify it. It also cuts part of the 10-15% storage shrink on product worth more than $2B a year in the US (spudman source).
- Market: The chlorpropham market is only about $107M in 2026 (https://www.factmr.com/report/chlorpropham-market). The whole sprout-suppressant pool, including newer alternatives, is probably $200-400M worldwide. As a standalone business this caps out below the $300M bar. It only gets bigger as one product line of a post-harvest irradiation platform (onions, garlic, grain; see the other hypotheses).
- Prior art: Japan's Shihoro potato irradiation plant has run commercially for decades; this is general knowledge and I did not fetch a source in this session. The Penn State 2015 e-beam study (link above). Patent 'Irradiation of food products' (https://patents.justia.com/patent/20190320671). I found no company selling irradiation-based sprout control to processors in North America or Europe.
- Killer risk: The EU and Japan bar imports of irradiated potato products, and frozen fries are a major export. Fresh-market potatoes would need retail labelling. Irradiated tubers can heal wounds poorly, which raises rot risk (literature risk I did not verify here). Processors may prefer cheaper chemical substitutes. The ceiling is small unless it is bundled into a platform.

### [radiation-applications] Helium-3 harvesting from non-Canadian CANDU tritium stockpiles (Wolsong, Cernavoda, others) (novelty 3, size 2, doability 3)

Build and operate He-3 recovery and purification modules on the tritium removal facilities in Korea and Romania (and later other PHWR fleets), collecting He-3 that today accumulates unharvested as tritium decays. Sell into a market with a 20,000-40,000 L/yr structural deficit at $2,000-10,000/L.

- Gap evidence: Annual terrestrial He-3 supply is about 22,000-30,000 L against demand of 40,000-60,000 L. Bulk prices are $1,900-2,600/L, dilution-refrigerator grade exceeds $10,000/L, and Russian supply has been effectively excluded since 2022 (https://postquantum.com/quantum-systems-integration/helium-3-quantum-computing/). Korea's Wolsong TRF has recovered tritium since 2007 and began tritium sales in 2024, but describes decay He-3 only as having 'potential value as a resource', with no recovery operation (https://www.kns.org/files/pre_paper/54/25A-276-한연덕.pdf). Romania's Cernavoda TRF (KHNP EPC, signed June 2023, about 50 months to build) will store tritium for future fusion use, with no He-3 plan mentioned (https://energyindustryreview.com/power/europes-first-tritium-removal-facility-at-cernavoda-npp/).
- Gain: Rough calculation: tritium decays about 5.5% a year, so each kg stored yields about 55 g He-3 a year. At about 7.5 L per gram that is roughly 400 L/yr, worth $1-4M a year per kg of tritium at $2,500-10,000/L. The added revenue is close to pure margin for the TRF owner.
- Market: The whole He-3 market is about $100-400M a year at current prices and volumes (calculated from the postquantum figures). Stockpiles outside Canada are a fraction of that. This FAILS the $300M bar unless demand from quantum computing and neutron detection grows sharply.
- Prior art: OPG's Laurentis Energy Partners already extracts He-3 from Darlington tritium (https://postquantum.com/quantum-systems-integration/helium-3-quantum-computing/). Interlune (lunar He-3) has offtakes with Bluefors, Maybell and DOE from 2028. Linde patented He-3 recovery from natural helium by distillation (https://patents.google.com/patent/US8683825). Pulsar Helium reports He-3 at its Topaz project (https://www.cruxinvestor.com/posts/pulsar-helium-reports-helium-3-concentrations-at-topaz-project-minnesota). Savannah River supplies the US. The method is proven; harvesting at Wolsong and Cernavoda is the open part.
- Killer risk: Small market ceiling. Sovereign and state-utility owners (KHNP, Nuclearelectrica) may do it in-house or with Laurentis. Tritium may be sold to fusion programmes before much He-3 builds up. Export controls on tritium-related technology.

### [fusion-and-space] Fusion fuel and breeder inventory lessor (an 'AerCap / Yellow Cake' for enriched Li-6, tritium and Be) (novelty 4, size 4, doability 3)

A balance-sheet company that buys enriched Li-6 (plus tritium and beryllium) and leases it into fusion plants, because a blanket inventory barely gets used up and is mostly a financing cost, not a fuel cost.

- Gap evidence: Ward, Pearson, Scott and Lopes Cardozo (arXiv 2605.04707, 7 May 2026) put the need at ~100 t of enriched Li-6 per GWth. Only ~100 kg/yr (0.1%) is consumed. At 7% interest, 'the cost of capital is 70x the cost of the consumption'. At $5k/kg, enriched lithium could be ~25% of a plant's overnight cost. Today's price is ~$50k/kg in small lots (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.04707). Tritium also loses ~5.5%/yr to decay and sells at ~$25k/g from OPG (https://scipub.euro-fusion.org/wp-content/uploads/eurofusion/WPPMIPR17_17185_submitted-1.pdf) or ~$33M/kg (LANL, https://techconnectworld.com/World2025/a.php?i=463). That makes it a wasting asset that fusion developers would rather not own. Civil tritium stocks are ~25 kg (https://kleinmanenergy.upenn.edu/commentary/blog/tritium-a-few-kilograms-can-make-or-break-nuclear-fusion/). Fusion companies are raising equity at high cost of capital: CFS $863M, Inertia $450M Series A (https://www.neimagazine.com/news/inertia-secures-series-a-fusion-funding/). Putting a decades-long, low-depreciation asset on venture equity is the mismatch that leasing solves in aircraft engines, nuclear fuel and PGM catalysts.
- Gain: At $5k/kg and ~100 t/GWth, a 1 GWe plant (~2.5-3 GWth) holds ~$1.25-1.5B of Li-6. Moving it off the developer's balance sheet at an 8% lease yield instead of 15-20% equity cost saves about $100M/yr per GWe in capital charges. That cuts LCOE by roughly 10-20%, on the paper's own ~25% capex share. Tritium start-up inventory (1-4 kg, i.e. $25-130M per plant) can be leased with decay priced in, rather than bought outright.
- Market: Per GWe, lease revenue is ~$100-120M/yr on Li-6 alone. Plus tritium start-up lending (1-4 kg/plant, EUROfusion DEMO estimate ~3.9 kg) and Be/FLiBe. 10 GWe of fusion in the early 2040s implies over $1B/yr of lease revenue on a $10-15B asset base. The ceiling is set by fusion deployment, not by a tool market. Near term (2027-2033): leasing to pilot plants (SPARC/ARC-class, STEP, Type One, Thea) and ITER test-blanket-scale quantities (~200 kg, per NEI: https://www.neimagazine.com/analysis/enriched-lithium-and-advanced-nuclear/).
- Prior art: Li-6 production: Hexium (AVLIS laser, $12M, https://www.power-technology.com/news/hexium-enriched-lithium-production/), Molten Salt Solutions (supply agreements, https://www.easternprogress.com/molten-salt-solutions-signs-strategic-agreements-to-supply-enriched-lithium-for-fusion-energy/article_148d1b53-8406-577c-9885-c0854b9b64f4.html), ORNL mercury-free electrochemistry (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/03/250320144810.htm). Tritium supply: OPG/Laurentis (OPG-UKAEA MoU Sept 2025, https://interactive.opg.com/2025-november/watts-new/isotopes-from-opgs-nuclear-fleet-advancing-fusion-energy-research) and AGENCORE of Korea ($4.1M, 2027 IPO plan, https://en.wowtale.net/2026/07/27/234557/). Be: Materion-CFS BeF2 supply deal, Oct 2025 (https://investor.materion.com/news/news-details/2025/Materion-Signs-Supply-Agreement-with-Commonwealth-Fusion-Systems-to-Provide-Beryllium-Fluoride-for-Fusion-Energy-Technology/default.aspx). All of these SELL material; none found LEASES it. A search for tritium leasing or a 'tritium bank' found no leasing business, only suppliers. A dedicated search for Li-6 leasing could not run because the web-search budget ran out, so that prior-art check is incomplete. The arXiv paper names the financing problem but proposes no lessor.
- Killer risk: Fusion timeline: if DT pilots slip past 2035, the asset base never forms. It is a capital-heavy, infrastructure-fund-shaped business: YC-scale equity can only prove it with signed lease LOIs and an offtake/warehouse facility, not the full balance sheet. Cheap laser enrichment ($1-10k/kg) would shrink the leasable asset value, though even at $1k/kg it is ~$300M per GWe. Developers may insist on owning tritium for licensing and accountancy reasons. Possible custody and nonproliferation questions for tritium.

### [fusion-and-space] Civil tritium-production-as-a-service in the existing fission fleet (novelty 3, size 2, doability 2)

An asset-light company that pays existing PWR/PHWR operators (Canada, Korea, Romania, possibly the US) to irradiate lithium targets for civil fusion customers and handles extraction, custody and sale. This turns a few percent of each reactor's neutron budget into a ~$30M/yr side revenue stream per unit.

- Gap evidence: Civil tritium is ~25 kg worldwide, almost all Canadian, and earmarked largely for ITER, which needs ~12.3 kg (https://kleinmanenergy.upenn.edu/commentary/blog/tritium-a-few-kilograms-can-make-or-break-nuclear-fusion/; https://scipub.euro-fusion.org/wp-content/uploads/eurofusion/WPPMIPR17_17185_submitted-1.pdf). EUROfusion's scenarios have CANDU/Korea/Romania stocks possibly reaching ~0 kg by 2049. About 70% of fusion companies plan DT operation. The US-proven route (TVA Watts Bar TPBARs, a goal of 1,400 g per reactor per cycle) serves weapons only (PNNL/ARPA-E 2023, https://arpa-e.energy.gov/sites/default/files/Tritium%20Production%20in%20a%20Commerical%20PWR%2C%20Overview%20and%20Target%20Design%20Considerations.pdf). LANL puts commercial tritium at ~$33M/kg and current commercial production at ~0.1 kg/yr (https://techconnectworld.com/World2025/a.php?i=463). No commercial player found that contracts power-reactor neutrons for CIVIL fusion tritium. Bruce Power already runs in-core isotope production in commercial CANDUs for Co-60/Lu-177 (https://world-nuclear-news.org/articles/bruce-power-looks-to-expand-radioisotope-offering), so the commercial model already works for other isotopes.
- Gain: Each PWR unit at the TPBAR rate (~1.4 kg per 18-month cycle) yields ~$35-46M of tritium per cycle at $25-33M/kg. That is new revenue of roughly 5-8% on top of a ~$500M/yr electricity business, with the extra fuel cost passed through (NNSA already covers it at Watts Bar). For fusion developers, it removes start-up fuel scarcity, which has been the single largest non-physics barrier to DT pilots.
- Market: Pilot and early-plant start-up demand (1-4 kg each across ~10-20 DT plants, plus the ITER shortfall) is roughly 20-60 kg over 2030-2045, i.e. $0.6-2B cumulative at $30M/kg. Ongoing demand covers make-up for plants with tritium breeding ratio below 1 and doubling-time support. The ceiling is about $100-300M/yr and partly transitional, since successful breeding erodes it. It pairs naturally with hypothesis 1 (lessor) to reach a larger combined company.
- Prior art: PNNL markets a 2026 'Tritium for Fusion' brochure, so the lab is positioning TPBAR know-how for fusion (https://www.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/media/file/PNNL-Tritium-for-Fusion-brochure-2026.pdf; fetch returned 403, title only). LANL has proposed an accelerator-driven molten-salt tritium source (https://techconnectworld.com/World2025/a.php?i=463). First Light Fusion pivoted to a 60 MW tritium-production pilot (https://firstlightfusion.com/media/first-light-fusion-targets-60-mw-pilot-plant-to-accelerate-tritium-production). Nuclear has mutual property and liability pools (NEIL, ANI), and NEIL's members are mostly incumbent utilities (https://www.myneil.com/). Neither pool covers capital-cost overruns. MGA commissions and fees of 10-20%, plus a profit share, give a $100-250M/yr fee business. Taking risk on balance sheet later pushes the ceiling to $1B+. Global new build (UK, Poland, Czechia, Canada) adds more.
- Prior art: NEIL (property) and ANI (liability) mutual pools, which do not cover construction cost. Federal loan guarantees through the DOE LPO. State cost-recovery mechanisms such as CWIP. The proposed federal cost-overrun insurance in policy discussions and Liftoff. Surety bonds on EPC contractors, which are capped and small relative to nuclear overruns. I found no private nuclear cost-overrun MGA, but searching was blocked; this needs verification.
- Killer risk: Risk is correlated and lumpy, there are few data points, and adverse selection is a danger: the projects most likely to overrun buy the most cover. Reinsurers may refuse capacity without a federal stop-loss. If Congress creates a federal program it could either crowd the MGA out or become its backstop. It also needs insurance licensing and underwriters with nuclear-construction credibility.

The US imported about 50 t of unwrought Hf in 2024, and only one producer each in Oregon and Utah makes Hf from zirconium intermediates. Zircon holds Zr and Hf at about 50:1 (https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-zirconium-hafnium.pdf). Hf is a critical mineral used in superalloys and HfO2 high-k semiconductor gates. New reactor demand (the Westinghouse AP1000 programme, LEU+ reloads) will raise nuclear Zr output, but Hf supply stays tied to that schedule.
- Gain: Hf at about $4,600/kg is around 100x the value of Zr. Recovering Hf from zirconia and zirconium chemicals that currently keep it could add a meaningful new primary supply without new mining.
- Market: US imports of about 50 t × $4,600/kg ≈ $230M/yr; the global market is perhaps $400-600M (an estimate, since USGS does not publish world production). That is borderline against the >$300M bar unless Zr/Hf separation services for nuclear Zr are added.
- Prior art: ATI (Oregon) and Westinghouse (Ogden, Utah), plus Framatome/CEZUS in France and Chinese producers. New separation chemistries (ionic liquids, solvent extraction) appear in academic papers. I could not run a startup search; there may be stealth materials startups.
- Killer risk: A small, volatile market. Incumbents can raise output, and a price collapse would sink the economics. The link to nuclear is indirect.

### [whats-changed-2026] Specialist insurer (MGA) for advanced-reactor and DOE-pilot nuclear liability and property risk (novelty 3, size 2, doability 2)

US nuclear liability insurance is effectively a single pool (ANI), and property cover is a single mutual (NEIL). Both are priced for gigawatt LWRs. Advanced and microreactors now moving through the Part 53, DOE pilot and Army Janus pathways have no risk-based pricing.

- Gap evidence: Evidence is weak because the ANI site could not be parsed this session (https://www.amnucins.com/). From background knowledge, not verified: ANI is the sole US primary nuclear liability insurer, NEIL is the only property and decontamination mutual, and the ADVANCE Act extended Price-Anderson. The window comes from the number of new reactor types expected under DOE authorization and NRC's EO-driven 18-month reviews.
- Gain: Premiums matched to source term and passive safety could cut insurance cost for microreactors and SMRs, where per-site fixed limits make up a disproportionate share of operating cost (not quantified).
- Market: If hundreds of SMRs and microreactors deploy in the 2030s, nuclear liability, property and construction cover could be a market of several hundred million dollars a year. An MGA earns about 15-20% commission on that. A real ceiling exists unless the business carries the risk itself.
- Prior art: ANI and NEIL incumbents; Lloyd's markets for international nuclear. No startup verified either way: the search budget was exhausted.
- Killer risk: Regulators set the required insurance amounts, and incumbent pools have the capacity and data. Reinsurers will not back a newcomer without loss history. A safety-adjacent value story.
