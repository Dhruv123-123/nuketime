## 2. Is AI used for netlist or firmware reconstruction?
- Academia: yes, heavily. Prof. Bah-Hwee Gwee (NTU, IEEE CAS webinar, 27 Mar 2024) presented GANs for defective-image detection, CNNs for extracting circuit information from IC images, and GNNs for netlist partitioning and identification. Other examples: GRACER (2023, graph-based standard-cell recognition), MLConnect (2024, connection prediction to correct errors in recovered circuits), and arXiv 2603.16548 (2026, SAM2 fine-tuned for metal-line segmentation with a topology loss that penalises shorts and opens; destructive imaging produces "hundreds of GB" of images).
- SoK arXiv 2603.17883 (2026) reviewed 187 papers on hardware reverse engineering. Key results could be reproduced for only 7 (about 4%). It found no benchmarks, few reusable artifacts, unresolved legal barriers to sharing data, and a widening gap to modern process nodes. A 2021 Bochum (CASA) survey says netlist extraction is "well understood" but specification discovery (what the circuit is meant to do) remains OPEN.
- Commercial: Synopsys has a 2025 patent application on extracting a gate-level netlist from cell placement with unsupervised learning. I found NO named startup or DoD award for AI-driven legacy-part re-creation in this run. The re-creation houses (Rochester, Tekmos, KaiSemi) describe deterministic image-to-netlist tooling with humans correcting errors.
- Conclusion (opinion): turning images into a netlist is semi-automated and AI is starting to help there. The work that is still manual and not productised is turning a netlist (or a binary) into a specification and then into evidence of equivalence, and that evidence is the part an approval needs.

## 3. The verification and approval pathway for an FFF replacement microcircuit (defence)
1. Identify the obsolescence: GIDEP notices, DMSMS case, choose a resolution (lifetime buy, aftermarket, emulation, redesign). Run by the program office DMSMS team.
2. Get data: datasheet, original drawing (SMD or source-control drawing), samples. Often there is no design data. Lost IP is the reason for the work.
3. Physical extraction: decap, delayer, SEM imaging, image stitching, netlist. Takes weeks to months. The physics clock is imaging throughput: RAVEN targeted 25 days per cm2.
4. Specification recovery: what the part actually does, including undocumented behaviour, timing margins, power-up states and analog quirks. Mostly MANUAL and the main source of failed emulations (one Rochester example had two failed emulation attempts before SRP worked).
5. Re-implementation: new ASIC, FPGA or mature-process port, with a package and pinout match.
6. Electrical equivalence: characterise the original against the replacement across temperature, voltage and frequency. Test vectors are written by hand. The FPGA prototype is tested in the customer's system.
7. Qualification: MIL-PRF-38535 QML (Class Q/V) or MIL-PRF-38534 hybrids, screening, burn-in, life test (1,000 hr life test is a physics clock), radiation for space parts. Supplier must be listed on QML/QPL by DLA Land & Maritime. Trusted accreditation (DMEA) is needed if the part is in a critical system.
8. Approval as an alternate: DLA Source Approval Request or a new SMD/drawing, or an engineering change through the program office.
9. System requalification: the weapon-system program office decides whether the board or LRU needs requal (flight test, EMI/EMC). This is often the most expensive step, and the reason "exact replicas" sell at a premium over emulation.
10. Counterfeit and provenance controls: SAE AS5553/AS6171 and DFARS 252.246-7007/7008.

What nobody does, or does well:
- (a) Specification recovery is not automated anywhere.
- (b) Test vectors and coverage are written by hand. There is no standard measure of how much of the original's behaviour has been proven to match.
- (c) Nothing records the original part's behaviour in the fielded system so it can be replayed against the replacement.
- (d) Equivalence evidence is not packaged in a form that DLA and program-office reviewers accept quickly. Each approval is argued afresh.
- (e) No benchmarks or shared corpora exist, because of legal limits on sharing data.
- (f) The firmware and FPGA-bitstream side of a legacy LRU (EPROMs, PALs, GALs, early FPGAs) is reconstructed by hand.

## 4. What would need to be done (assuming abundant compute and intelligence)
- An autonomous pipeline from delayering to netlist: robotic decap and SEM, plus topology-aware segmentation and cell recognition with self-checking for opens and shorts. Target: days per legacy die, not months. Legacy parts at 0.35um and above are easy for imaging.
- Automated specification recovery: an AI that infers state machines, protocols and timing from the netlist plus datasheet plus logged traces, and writes the result as a formal specification (SystemVerilog Assertions) and a behavioural model.
- Equivalence evidence generation in three parts. Formal equivalence checking from the extracted netlist to the new RTL. Differential tests generated from coverage and run on a tester (original and replacement side by side, across voltage and temperature corners). In-system shadowing, where the replacement sits next to the original on a live board and every cycle is compared. Output: a quantified "behavioural coverage certificate".
- A reviewer-facing package: auto-generated SAR or drawing package, QML conformance traceability and a requal justification ("no system requal needed because coverage = X with formal proof of Y"). Changing regulator acceptance means getting DLA Land & Maritime, the Navy Crane and AFRL hardware-assurance groups, or a program office to accept a coverage-based argument in place of the requal step. A pilot through a DMSMS Working Group, SBIR, or the OSD Microelectronics Commons hubs is plausible.
- Firmware and PLD lifting: dump EPROMs, PALs and early-FPGA bitstreams, lift them to verified source (as V-SPELLS does), recompile for the new hardware, and prove equivalence by co-simulation.
- A data moat: a library of characterised legacy parts with their extracted models. Each new part reuses the cell libraries and behavioural models of older ones.

Constraints: life test and burn-in are fixed physics clocks (about 1,000 hours). Law: ITAR/EAR on defence parts, and copyright and mask-work rights (SCPA 17 USC 902) when re-creating without a licence. Reverse engineering for interoperability is allowed under 17 USC 906, but Rochester's moat is OCM licences. Liability: the replacement supplier carries the risk of field failures. Data access: samples of the original are scarce, and data cannot be shared.

## 5. Opinion: the AI play on the verification side
Do not compete with Rochester on re-creation. Sell a "Behavioural Equivalence Engine". Inputs: an original part or a board plus traces. Outputs: a recovered specification, a test suite, a formal and differential equivalence proof, and a package ready for the reviewer. Customers: Rochester, Tekmos, DMEA, NSWC Crane, primes' DMSMS teams, and the program offices themselves. Then move into doing the replacement and taking the approval yourself (the Approval Factory). Metric: hours to reach approval-grade equivalence evidence per part number. The same engine applies to board-level LRU FFF replacements (e.g., C-130 avionics cards), not just chips.
