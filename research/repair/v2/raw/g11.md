# g11: Medical device service parts: ISO replacement parts, FDA 2018 servicing report, 2024 remanufacturing guidance, and the pathway for a non-OEM replacement part

Method: 3 WebSearch calls (quota respected) plus WebFetch of law-firm and trade summaries. fda.gov guidance PDFs returned 401/404 to the fetcher, so primary guidance text was NOT read directly. Anything marked (recalled) comes from memory and was not verified in this session.

## 1. Regulatory facts (evidence)

- **FDARA 2017** required FDA to report on servicing. **The FDA servicing report came out in May 2018.** It said the evidence was "not sufficient to conclude whether or not there is a widespread public health concern" and so did not justify "burdensome additional or different" requirements. FDA committed to clarify servicing vs remanufacturing. (Hall Render, https://hallrender.com/2024/06/21/remanufacturing-or-servicing-of-medical-devices-fda-issues-final-guidance-providing-additional-clarification/)
  - RAPS summary: the 2018 report found that many adverse events attributed to "inadequate servicing" were really remanufacturing. (https://www.raps.org/resource/fda-issues-long-awaited-device-remanufacturing-gui.html)
  - (recalled) The report put the number of US firms doing servicing at roughly 16,500 to 20,800. It noted that MDRs attributable to servicing were a very small share of all MDRs, and that the data cannot reliably tell who did the work (OEM, ISO or in-house).
- **The draft remanufacturing guidance came out in June 2021. The final guidance, "Remanufacturing of Medical Devices," came out on May 10, 2024** (FR doc 2024-10230). (Morgan Lewis, https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/05/fda-clarifies-distinction-between-device-remanufacturing-and-servicing-in-final-guidance; RAPS)
  - Remanufacturing is defined as work that "significantly changes the finished device's performance or safety specifications, or intended use." Servicing restores a device to OEM specifications and intended use (21 CFR 820.3).
  - **FDA's six principles:** (1) intended-use change? (2) do the changes, individually and cumulatively, significantly change safety or performance specs? (3) would the change need a new marketing submission? (4) component, part and material dimensional and performance specs; (5) a risk-based approach; (6) document the decision so an FDA investigator or a third party can follow it.
  - **Flowchart, about p.11:** adding, removing or changing a patient-contacting component or material, or changing a component's dimensional or performance spec, triggers deeper evaluation. Changes to sterilization, reprocessing instructions, control mechanism, operating principle or energy type are likely remanufacturing.
  - **Parts (per MedDeviceGuide summary, https://meddeviceguide.com/blog/fda-servicing-vs-remanufacturing-decision-tree-medical-device-guide):** an identical OEM part with the same P/N, spec and supplier is "almost always servicing." Any non-identical part, even one described as "equivalent" or "better," needs analysis of dimensions, materials (biocompatibility, aging) and performance. Appendix A examples:
    - Endoscope lens re-bonded with an undocumented epoxy: possibly remanufacturing.
    - Identical OEM defib battery: servicing.
    - Discontinued CRT replaced by an LCD from another supplier: likely remanufacturing (EMC, power, thermal and display-accuracy effects).
    - Modifying an imaging chain with non-OEM components: likely remanufacturing.
  - **Software (about p.16):** installing non-OEM software or firmware, changing source code, or changing protocols or algorithms is generally remanufacturing. Restoring OEM-validated software is servicing.
  - **Remanufacturers are manufacturers.** They need registration and listing, premarket authorization where needed, QMSR, MDR (Part 803), corrections and removals (Part 806), and labeling including UDI.
  - **Independent servicers doing only servicing are generally not subject to FDA requirements.** They self-assess, nobody registers them, and there is no required documentation.
- **The QMSR took effect February 2, 2026** and incorporates ISO 13485:2016. It applies fully to remanufacturers.
- **Stakeholder positions (RAPS):**
  - MITA says unregistered ISOs are not bound by the guidance and wants FDA oversight.
  - AMDSO (the ISO trade group) says the guidance cannot be implemented unless OEMs provide specs, parts and materials, and OEMs have no obligation to do so.
  - MDMA opposes compelled disclosure of trade secrets.
  - FDA "encourages" OEMs to provide servicing instructions as a best practice, with no mandate.
- **Right to repair:** the federal Critical Medical Infrastructure Right-to-Repair Act (Wyden, 2020) is stalled, and I found no evidence it passed. State laws so far cover mainly wheelchairs:
  - Colorado HB22-1031 (2022)
  - California SB 1384 (2024)
  - Vermont H.160 in progress in 2026 (secondary source)
  - (https://pirg.org/media-center/new-senate-right-to-repair-bill-to-reduce-barriers-to-fixing-medical-equipment-including-ventilators/ ; Vermont intern report 3-27-2026)
- (recalled) The **US Copyright Office DMCA 1201 exemption (Oct 2024)** now covers diagnosis, maintenance and repair of medical devices and systems, including imaging. This removes the TPM-circumvention barrier for repair, but not for making parts or modifying software.

## 2. Pathway for a non-OEM replacement part (synthesized; regulatory status of parts is the murkiest step)

| # | Step | Who | Time/cost | What is undone or broken |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Find a part that is obsolete, sole-source or overpriced (CT/x-ray tubes, detectors, MRI coils, ultrasound probes, boards, batteries, displays, endoscope optics) | ISO / parts broker | n/a | No public demand data. Failure data sits with OEMs. MAUDE attribution is unreliable. |
| 2 | Get the OEM spec | Nobody can. OEMs are not required to provide it. | n/a | **Core gap.** The guidance makes "equivalent to OEM spec" the test but gives third parties no access to the spec. |
| 3 | Reverse-engineer: dimensions, materials, electrical, firmware | Part maker (e.g. Innovatus-style in-house molding/machining) | Weeks to months, engineering hours | Firmware or pairing keys turn it into a software change, which is remanufacturing. |
| 4 | Classify the work as servicing or remanufacturing (6 principles, flowchart) | The ISO itself (self-assessment) | Hours to days | No neutral checker and no FDA pre-determination route short of a 513(g) request (recalled: 60-day target, user fee about $5-6k). The "significant change" test is subjective (AQMDS). |
| 5a | If equivalent: servicing. Document the rationale. | ISO | Low | Nobody reviews the file. The hospital has no proof, and the OEM attacks it as unregulated. |
| 5b | If not equivalent: remanufacturing. Register and list, QMSR, and decide on a 510(k) using the 2017 "Deciding When to Submit a 510(k) for a Change to an Existing Device" guidance. | Remanufacturer + RA consultants | 510(k): 90 FDA review days; (recalled) MDUFA FY2026 standard fee about $24-26k, small business about $6k. In practice 6-12 months and $100k-$500k+ including testing. | Predicate is the OEM device. You need performance data without the OEM design file. |
| 6 | Bench testing: IEC 60601-1 and 60601-1-2 EMC, ISO 10993 biocompatibility for patient contact, IEC 62304 software, 524B cyber (if software), 21 CFR 1020 radiation performance standard plus Form 2579 assembler reports for x-ray (recalled) | Test labs (UL, Intertek, TUV, Element) | Weeks to months. A physics clock: EMC chamber, aging, life cycles. | Labs test to the standard, not to "OEM equivalence." No equivalence test protocol exists. |
| 7 | Hospital acceptance: biomed/HTM, risk, legal, Joint Commission EC standards; CMS (recalled, S&C 14-07) requires OEM-recommended maintenance for imaging and lasers, outside AEM programs | Hospital HTM, GPOs, ECRI | Months of sales cycle | **This is the real gate.** Without an FDA stamp on servicing parts, buyers fall back on OEM. |
| 8 | Postmarket: complaints, MDR (only remanufacturers and manufacturers are required to report) | Remanufacturer | Ongoing | No field-performance registry for third-party parts. |

Precedents where non-OEM parts got clearance (recalled, unverified):
- Third-party compatible ultrasound transducers and repaired probes, and third-party MRI coils (Quality Electrodynamics, later bought by Canon).
- Replacement x-ray and CT tubes sold by Varex and Dunlee with their own clearances or registrations.
- Many replacement parts are sold under the parent device's classification, or as "components" under 21 CFR 807.65(a) exemption claims, with no 510(k).

Compared with aviation, there is **no FAA PMA-equivalent "approved replacement part" pathway in medtech.** A part is either (a) unreviewed servicing or (b) a full remanufactured-device 510(k). The middle tier is missing.

## 3. Players

**OEMs (parts plus multivendor service):** GE HealthCare, Siemens Healthineers, Philips, Canon. They hold the specs, software keys and service tools.

**ISOs and HTM services:**
- Agiliti: (recalled) taken private by THL in 2024 for about $2.5B.
- TRIMEDX, Sodexo HTM, Crothall (Compass), Renovo, BC Technical, Avante Health, Probo Medical.
- Block Imaging: parts, and (recalled) part of Avante.
- ReMedPar: "largest independent provider" of imaging parts.
- Innovatus Imaging: probes and coils. Claims 200k probe repairs, 50k coil repairs, "40-60% below OEM," ISO 13485 certified, in-house molding and machining.
- Richardson Healthcare (recalled), Vcube Parts, LBN Medical, DOTmed marketplace.

**Component makers that sell OEM-compatible parts:** Varex (x-ray tubes and detectors) and Dunlee, both recalled.

**Software and data:**
- CMMS and asset tools: Nuvolo, Accruent, Phynd (recalled).
- ECRI publishes device evaluations.

**Trade groups:** AMDSO and AQMDS on the ISO side; MITA, AdvaMed and MDMA on the OEM side.

**Regulatory consultants:** NAMSA, Emergo by UL, MCRA, among others.

**Not found:** any company doing AI-native reverse engineering of medical parts combined with automated six-principle/510(k) dossiers. Nobody appears to sell a third-party "equivalence certification" for servicing parts. This rests on a limited search and is not exhaustive.

## 4. What would need to be done (nobody does it today)

1. **Equivalence-without-spec engine.** Infer the OEM performance envelope from teardown, CT scans, measurements of in-service fleets, OEM service manuals, IFUs and the OEM's own 510(k) summaries, then generate a guidance-conforming six-principle dossier that an FDA investigator can follow.
2. **Third-party conformity mark for servicing parts.** Think UL or Nadcap for medical replacement parts: an accredited body attests that part X meets the OEM-equivalent envelope. This fills the missing PMA-like middle tier. It needs no change in law and works through hospitals, GPOs, insurers and Joint Commission acceptance. FDA's ASCA program (accredited test labs) is a possible regulatory hook.
3. **Remanufacturing 510(k) factory.** Batch "same predicate, changed component" submissions per platform, using eSTAR templates, so the marginal cost per clearance falls.
4. **Field-performance registry for non-OEM parts.** Link CMMS work orders, MDRs and UDI so the safety case is data-driven instead of rhetorical. Right now neither the OEM nor the ISO side can prove its claims, which the 2018 report itself admitted.
5. **Policy lever.** Ask FDA for a pilot (CDRH has done many) or a 513(g)-at-scale "servicing part determination" pathway. Use the 2018 report's data gap as the rationale.

Binding constraints, and what would change each:

| Constraint | What it is | What would change it |
|---|---|---|
| Physics | EMC, aging and life-cycle tests take wall-clock time | Parallel chambers, model-based accelerated aging accepted by consensus standards |
| Law | No mandate for OEM spec disclosure. Patent and trade-secret exposure. | Right-to-repair bills, DMCA exemption (already exists for repair) |
| Regulator | No middle tier | FDA pilot, ASCA expansion |
| Liability | Hospitals bear risk from non-OEM parts | Insurer-backed warranty or a conformity mark |
| Data | OEM specs and fleet data | Hospital-owned CMMS and UDI data is a lawful source; OEM data is not |

## 5. AI plays on the verification side
- "Six-principle dossier generator" plus a classification audit trail for ISOs. Cheap wedge, SaaS to thousands of servicers and hospital HTM departments.
- AI equivalence verification lab: an automated metrology, electrical and firmware characterization rig plus a model that issues an equivalence certificate, as a third-party certifier.
- MAUDE/MDR re-attribution model: classify reports as servicing, remanufacturing or OEM-caused. Sell to FDA, payers, ISOs and litigators. This addresses the 2018 report's stated evidence gap.
- 510(k) change-assessment copilot for remanufacturers, built on the 2017 change guidance, eSTAR and the predicate's 510(k) summary.

## 6. Opinion: fit with the Approval Factory
- Medtech is **weaker as a permission moat** than aviation or DLA. Most parts need no approval at all (servicing), so an approval is not a barrier competitors must clear. The binding gate is hospital trust and OEM software locks, not regulator sign-off.
- Medtech is **stronger as a verification/certification play.** The regulator has explicitly left an evidence and attestation gap, and both sides (MITA and AMDSO) complain about it.
- Best use for the founder: an adjacent second market for a verification product built for aerospace, not the beachhead. Imaging tubes, probes and coils are the high-value parts. CT tubes sell for (recalled) $100k-$400k from OEMs.
