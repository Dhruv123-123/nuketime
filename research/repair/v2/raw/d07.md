## 4. Where the money and margin sit

1. **Physical testing labs** (vibration, thermal, EMI, fatigue, NDT): capital-heavy, capacity-scarce, with physics-clock lead times of weeks to months. Margins are good (Element ~25% adj. EBITDA) because capacity is the permission.
2. **Attestation/sign-off** (DER signature, ODA unit member, CGD dedicator's Part 21 responsibility, notified body): the highest margin per hour, held by credentialed individuals. This is the "permission" layer.
3. **Document and evidence assembly** (test plans, conformity reports, TDPs, compliance matrices, FMEA/FHA, similarity analyses): about 50–70% of hours (opinion). It is labor-dense, low-moat, and exactly the knowledge work AI makes cheap.

Most incumbents' 15–18% margin comes from mixing a small amount of layer 2 with a large amount of layer 3 at labor rates.

## 5. Growth drivers (2025–2031)
- Defense sustainment and obsolescence: DMSMS, the aging C-130/KC-135/B-52 fleets, and DoD "right to repair" provisions in the FY2025–26 NDAAs (recalled).
- Nuclear restart and uprate (Palisades, Crane/TMI-1, Duane Arnold) plus SMRs: CGD demand.
- Aerospace parts shortage, 2023–26: PMA adoption rising, with airlines accepting more PMA.
- Space and drones: qualification testing capacity is scarce (NTS/Element lab backlogs, recalled).
- New regulated software/AI: EU AI Act conformity assessment from 2026–27, the EU Cyber Resilience Act, CMMC 2.0 (phase-in Nov 2025).

## 6. Implications (opinion)
- TIC is a $260B market with mid-single-digit growth and 15–18% margins. It is held by permission (accreditation, designation, lab capacity) and delivered by people. An AI-native entrant does not need to grow the market. It needs to change the labor ratio: about €80k revenue per employee for incumbents vs a possible €500k+ for an AI-native approval house.
- The best wedge is not "AI for TIC incumbents" (they buy slowly and protect billable hours). It is owning the approvals yourself (Approval Factory) or becoming the evidence engine that regulators and designees trust: machine-checked compliance packages where the DER/DLA reviewer signs off in hours, not weeks.
- Comparable outcomes: Element at $7B, HEICO (~$35–40B market cap, recalled) built on PMA approvals, and Vanta/Drata in software compliance. A venture-scale outcome is plausible if the entrant captures the approvals (asset value) rather than selling hours.

## 7. What would need to be done (assuming plentiful intelligence and compute)
- A machine-readable map from regulation to requirement to evidence for FAA Part 21/33.x, MIL-STD-810/461, DLA SAR categories and 10 CFR 21 CGD critical characteristics. Nobody has this as a public, executable spec today.
- Simulation-plus-test "credit" frameworks: regulator acceptance of model-based evidence (FAA already accepts some analysis in place of test; ASME V&V 40-style credibility frameworks exist for medical devices). The goal is to cut physical-test count, not eliminate it.
- Automated similarity and equivalence analysis (PMA "identicality", CGD "like-for-like", SAR Category I–IV) from CT scans, materials analysis and the existing approved data.
- Lab-capacity orchestration: book, run and auto-report qualification tests across fragmented labs. This is the physics clock, and the lever is utilization plus faster report turnaround.
- A designee-in-the-loop product. AI drafts and checks, and a credentialed DER, CGD dedicator or C3PAO signs. Liability stays with the signer, so the play is either employing the signers or giving them 10x throughput.
