## 1. Where a part enters the funnel: AMC/AMSC coding (PGI 217.7506), the master switch

Source: https://www.acquisition.gov/dfarspgi/pgi-217.7506-spare-parts-breakout-program (fetched).

Every NSN carries an Acquisition Method Code (AMC) and an Acquisition Method Suffix Code (AMSC).

**AMC codes**
- 1 or 2: competitive.
- 3 or 4: buy from the actual manufacturer.
- 5: buy from a sole-source contractor that is not the actual manufacturer.

**AMSC codes that block competition**
- **B**: source-control or selected-item drawing. Only the sources named on the drawing may supply it.
- **C**: needs engineering source approval by the design control activity (DCA), so the item's quality is maintained. Alternate sources must qualify. *This is the SAR code for most aviation and CSI items.*
- **D**: competitive data cannot be obtained and adequate specs cannot be drafted.
- **H**: the Government lacks sufficient, accurate or legible data.
- **A**: data rights are questionable.
- **P**: the Government cannot buy or develop the needed data or rights.
- **Q**: no data or rights, but breakout is expected after 12+ months of full screening. These are *reverse-engineering candidates*.
- **R**: buying data and reverse engineering are both uneconomical.
- **U**: breakout cost exceeds projected life-span savings.
- **K**: class 1 castings or forgings (MIL-STD-2175).
- **M**: master tooling.
- **N**: special test facilities.
- **S**: militarily sensitive.
- **V**: high-reliability.
- **Y**: unstable design.
- **Z**: commercial item.

**AMSC codes that are open**
- G: the Government has full data with unlimited rights. G is the only full-and-open code.
- T: qualified products list (QPL).
- DLA does not evaluate SARs against G or T items.

**Breakout screening**
- Full screening has 65 steps in six phases.
- The economic evaluation uses a default savings factor of 25%.
- Reverse engineering is step 19, used "when cost expected below anticipated savings".
- Time standards:
  - limited-screening codes: suspense of no more than 12 months;
  - full-screening codes: no more than 3 years, or 5 years in exceptional cases;
  - status on a code-review request: within 60 days.

**Implication.** Codes such as U, R, P and L are *economic* judgements made with crude cost models: breakout cost compared with savings, with a 25% default factor. If the cost of reverse engineering and of the package falls 10x, a large share of U, R and Q items become economic to break out. Nobody re-screens them at scale. **The AMSC file itself is a target list.**

## 2. The SAR pathway step by step (DLA Land and Maritime, 2016 NDIA deck, fetched)

Source: https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2016/DLA/SourceApprovalRequestsCastingandForging.pdf

**Definition.** A SAR is "an assembly of information required of a prospective new supplier for a specific item (NSN) that is currently procured as other than full-and-open". It covers AMSC B (source controlled), C (source restricted) and D (code and part number). The package must contain "all technical data needed to demonstrate that the prospective contractor can competently manufacture the NSN to the same level of quality or better than the approved source".

**Clock as published**
1. The contractor finds candidate NSNs using DIBBS, Haystack, SERVA, DLA candidate lists or a PTAC.
2. The contractor assembles the package:
   - company manufacturing and quality information;
   - the current part data and the contractor's own data, either OEM data (SAR categories) or reverse engineering of an OEM part;
   - a business case;
   - actual data and/or a test plan.
3. The package is submitted. With an open solicitation it goes as an Alternate Offer (AO) to the contracting officer and the Competition Advocate Office (CAO). Without one it goes as a SAR to the CAO.
4. The CAO logs it, sends a receipt notice, and forwards it to Value Engineering (VE) **within 5 days**.
5. VE reviews it **within 15 days**, with **7 analysts**.
   - If it is incomplete but reworkable, the contractor gets **30 days** to supply missing data.
   - Otherwise it is rejected to the CAO.
6. The ESA (Engineering Support Activity, the service weapon-system engineers) reviews it within a **90-day response time**. It accepts, rejects, or asks for more data.
7. VE concurs or does not concur within **15 days**. It gathers any extra data (**30 days**) and takes cataloguing action to add the CAGE and part number as an approved source.
8. The CAO sends the official letter (**5 days**).

**Nominal total: about 5-6 months if nothing loops.** The real clock is set by ESA loops, by "Need to Complete Qualification Testing" (a first article or qualification test), and by the wait for the next procurement.

**Government response types**
- Technical data available (the item is competitive).
- Data missing.
- Need to complete qualification testing ("Good on paper, but need to prove will work in application").
- Rejected, for one of these reasons:
  - business case or not in the Government's interest;
  - incomplete;
  - **weapons criticality**;
  - application upgraded or discontinued;
  - **"Can't validate part (no government data to compare)"**.
- Accepted: the CAGE and part number are added as an approved source.

**Metrics in the deck (DLA Land and Maritime, FY16)**
- 211 packages received.
- **55 approved**, worth $2.35M in potential annual savings.
- **184 pending**, worth $28.5M in potential annual savings.
- 90% VE package acceptance rate, 29% higher than the prior year.
- $17.6M in FY16 expanded-competition savings.

Reading these figures: the pending pile was more than 3x the approved count, and **pending items held 12x the savings of approved ones**. The backlog is where the value sits. VE (DLA's front door) accepts 90%; the downstream ESA is the choke point.

**RPPOB (Replenishment Parts Purchase or Borrow, 10 USC 2320(b) lineage), 2014-2016 metrics**
- 742 bailment requests from 99 CAGEs.
- 190 signed agreements.
- 55 SARs submitted: **40 approved, 4 rejected, 11 pending**.
- 17 NSNs procured; savings of $2.72M.
- Rules: "Typical timeline is 12 to 18 months for full approval". Minimum annual buy value is $10K. The item must be in stock. Not available for AMSC B, G or T.

Two conversions:
- Conditional on submission, the approval rate is 40/44, about **91%** of decided cases.
- But only 55 of 742 bailment requests (7%) became a SAR, and only 17 led to a procurement. **The funnel leaks before submission (package cost) and after approval (buy cadence), not at adjudication.**

**DAG 17.7501-6 (17 June 2014)**
- A newly approved source gets a multi-source award clause for at least 1 year.
- If its price is not lowest but is fair and reasonable, **40% of the award goes to the new source**.
- The NSN is removed from auto-solicitation and Long Term Contracts.

This is a demand guarantee for approvals, which helps an Approval Factory's revenue model.

## 3. DLA Aviation specifics (search snippets of DLA docs, not fetched, 403)

- SARs "may not be processed if it is determined that the SAR is not cost effective due to low item demand".
- Declines can be based on low dollar value, years of stock on hand, or multiple sources cited on the Technical Item Record (TIR).
- If an open solicitation exists, the package goes to the buyer.
- Mailed CDs are no longer accepted; packages go through DoD SAFE (DLA Aviation SAR Submit Instructions, SAFE updates, 14 Dec 2022).
- Each service and DLA has its own SAR process and guidance (DSIAC inquiry snippet).

## 4. SAR categories (recalled; DLA and service SAR guides, which could not be fetched)

The joint DLA/Service SAR guide groups offerors by how close they are to the approved source.
- **Category I**: the offeror has previously made the identical item for the OEM, the prime or the Government. Proof is purchase orders and acceptance records. This is the lightest package.
- **Category IA, IB, IC**: variations on Category I (recalled), for example:
  - previously made the item but with process or location changes;
  - approved for the identical item by another service or agency;
  - made it for a foreign or commercial customer.
- **Category II**: the offeror has made a *similar* item (similar complexity, material and process). It needs a comparative analysis and usually a first article.
- **Category III** (in several guides; recalled): never made the item or a similar one. This is **reverse engineering**. It needs a full TDP, material and process specs and a qualification test, and is the most often rejected (no government data to compare).

The categories decide how much evidence is required. Category I or II is a *prior-supply / similarity* argument. Category III is a *full engineering* argument. An Approval Factory that reverse-engineers lives mainly in Category III. It could convert into Category II by building a portfolio of "similar items" already approved: precedent compounds.

## 5. Critical Safety Items (law and DFARS, fetched)

- **10 USC 3243** (formerly 2319; https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/3243):
  - A qualification requirement needs a written justification, and the standards must be in writing.
  - Potential offerors must get a "prompt opportunity" to demonstrate, at their own expense, and must be told promptly why they failed.
  - Requirements must be revalidated within 7 years.
  - If there are fewer than 2 qualified sources, the agency must solicit more and **may bear small-business qualification testing costs**.
  - The agency need not delay a procurement for a qualification.
  - There is a streamlined process for qualifying advanced-manufacturing sources before or during wartime (subsection (e)).
  - **For aviation and ship CSIs, the head of the design control activity decides qualification, not the contracting officer.** The DCA is the systems command responsible for airworthiness or seaworthiness.
- **DFARS 209.270-4** (fetched): the DCA head identifies CSIs, approves qualification requirements, and qualifies CSI suppliers and products. The contracting officer must refer an unapproved source's offer to the DCA, and the determination must be made before award. Quality assurance follows 246.407(S-70) and 246.504.
- (Recalled) The CSI regime comes from FY2004 NDAA §802 (aviation) and FY2007 §130 (ship), after failures in reverse-engineered or nonconforming parts. The Joint Aeronautical Commanders Group (JACG) Aviation CSI Management Handbook and the FLIS criticality code flag CSIs. CSIs make up a small share of NSNs but a large share of the slowest and most-rejected SARs.
- (Recalled) USAF airworthiness rests on MIL-HDBK-516C and the Technical Airworthiness Authority. For CSIs the ESA usually requires a first article plus qualification testing, and sometimes engine or flight testing.

**ESAs for the target platforms (recalled)**
- WR-ALC Robins: C-130 airframe.
- OC-ALC Tinker: KC-135, TF33/F108 engines, much of the engine CSI population.
- OO-ALC Hill: landing gear, A-10, F-16, commodities.
- NAVAIR FRCs / PMA-2xx.
- Army AvMC Redstone.
- Engines and CSIs often sit with AFLCMC/LP or the propulsion directorate.

## 6. Time data points (evidence)

- **Nominal published ESA response is 90 days.** The whole DLA pathway is about 5-6 months without loops (DLA L&M 2016).
- **RPPOB to full approval is typically 12-18 months** (DLA L&M 2016).
- **JMS Southeast, SBIR DLA254-P01, $27,205** (https://www.sbir.gov/awards/217268):
  - SAR first submitted October 2023;
  - refinements with government engineers throughout 2024;
  - the final testing Phase I runs 24 Sep 2025 to 23 Mar 2026 at Tinker AFB against AFMC Form 260 first-article requirements: dimensions 4 weeks, material 4 weeks, form/fit/function 6 weeks.
  - **That is about 2.5 years from first SAR to approval for one NSN.** The physics (14 weeks of testing) is a small share; the rest is loops and queue.
- Prior notes in this repo (c-series), not re-verified:
  - Empirical Systems Aerospace (2022) submitted a SAR about 10 weeks after starting;
  - Dynovas (2024) qualified parts within a 6-month $100k Phase I for NAVAIR PMA-202.
  - **Package build is fast when done well; adjudication is slow.**
- NPS ARS 2024 (Bailey and Rajapaksa, SYM-AM-24-103; https://dair.nps.edu/handle/123456789/5158):
  - Ohio-class submarine parts have lead times up to **1,261 days**, and about 14% of the parts studied have long lead times;
  - Navy engineers "manually scour hundreds of sources" to find alternates;
  - an LLM ranks alternates by form, fit and function plus stock on hand.
  - This is evidence that the *engineering-disposition* labour on the government side is manual.
- (Recalled, low confidence) Practitioners report CSI SARs commonly taking 1-3 years, with ESA engineering backlogs in the hundreds to thousands of packages across the ALCs.

## 7. Approval-rate data points

- DLA L&M FY16: 211 received, 55 approved, 184 pending. The 90% VE acceptance figure is a *completeness* gate, not an engineering approval.
- RPPOB SARs: 40 approved, 4 rejected, 11 pending, so about 91% of decided cases.
- Conclusion: **once decided, most SARs that reach decision get approved. The binding variables are (a) time in queue, (b) packages never submitted because the expected value is below the cost, and (c) items DLA refuses to process for low demand.**

## 8. Who is doing what (players)

**Applicant-side SAR and reverse-engineering houses (one NSN at a time, mostly DLA SBIR-funded)**
- sbir.gov holds **284 awards** matching "source approval". Phase I is typically $27k-$100k per NSN or per small NSN set.
- Examples:
  - JMS Southeast;
  - Micro-Precision Technologies (transistor for UEWR; digital microcircuit);
  - Parts Life Inc (Navy spares, a "SAR Process and Reverse Engineering Risk Avoidance Process", nuclear-enterprise RE);
  - AMG Engineering (11 aerospace bearing NSNs for DLA/USAF approval);
  - Form Fit and Function LLC (RE plus source approval for aging weapon systems);
  - Bascom Hunter (Army wire harnesses);
  - Mentis Sciences;
  - ESSS;
  - Dynovas;
  - A&B Foundry (B-1 flex duct);
  - ORBIS (DLA SBIR obsolescence; earlier notes say one firm doing many NSNs saved 25%).
- Search: https://www.sbir.gov/awards?keywords=%22source%20approval%22
- **This is a cottage industry of hundreds of small machine and engineering shops, each hand-building packages. Nobody runs it as a factory.**

**Government-side AI**
- **ELEVAN LLC with UTSA, USAF STTR Phase I AFX23E-TPCSO1, FA8649-24-P-0130, $109,997, Feb-May 2024** (https://www.sbir.gov/awards/210672). "AI/ML paired with computer vision to expedite the review and approval of supportability analysis requests ... save thousands of unbudgeted hours" of Air Force engineers. **This is the only award found that targets the reviewer side.** No Phase II was found.
- NPS (Bailey and Rajapaksa): an LLM alternate-part finder for the Navy, still academic.

**AI reverse-engineering tools (applicant-side, knowledge not permission)**
- Novineer (Embry-Riddle spin-out, founded 2022; Stratasys partner; Army agreements). Its NoviVision makes CAD from phone photos (0.72 mm mean deviation on 100 mm). It makes **no SAR or TAPS approval claims** (https://thedefensepost.com/2026/06/30/novineer-ai-reverse-engineering-sustainment/).
- Great Plains Innovation Network (Kansas): about $1.8M from DoD to reverse engineer obsolete parts and recreate TDPs.
- Quantum Research Sciences (Purdue): DLA SBIR Phase I for obsolescence prediction.

**Data and analytics incumbents**
- Haystack (Accuris / formerly IHS) and SERVA give FLIS/AMSC lookups.
- Govini does supply-chain analytics.
- None of them generates packages or does disposition.

**Big PMA and DER houses**
- HEICO and Wencor work on the FAA side.
- They do little on DLA SARs at scale (recalled).

## 9. Bottlenecks, ranked

1. **ESA engineer hours.** The published SLA is 90 days; reality is multi-year loops on CSIs and reverse engineering. ESA engineers have no tooling, no structured intake (packages arrive as PDFs on DoD SAFE), and no precedent database.
2. **"Can't validate part (no government data to compare)".** The Government lacks the baseline, so a reverse-engineered package has nothing to be compared against. This is an *information-asymmetry* reject, not a quality reject.
3. **Expected value below package cost.** Only 7% of RPPOB requests became SARs; DLA declines low-demand NSNs; AMSC U, R and L codes come from crude cost models.
4. **Physics and qualification testing.** First article, material, form/fit/function testing, about 14 weeks in the JMS case. Engine and flight tests for CSIs are much longer. These are real, but a minor share of elapsed time.
5. **Procurement cadence after approval.** In the RPPOB data, 23 NSNs were "awaiting next procurement", so approval does not mean revenue until the next buy. DAG 17.7501-6 (the 40% share) helps.
6. **Fragmentation.** Every service and ALC has its own guide and checklist. Each NSN restarts from zero, and there is no portfolio or similarity approval.
7. **Liability and CSI culture.** The DCA owns airworthiness risk, so the incentive is to say "need more data". There is no credit for the risk the Government is already carrying with a sole-source OEM.

## 10. What nobody does (and what it would take)

1. **A machine-readable SAR standard plus an intake portal.** There is no schema: requirement, then evidence, then claim. What it would take: one ESA plus DLA VE adopting a structured SAR, a MIL-STD-31000-style model-based TDP plus an evidence graph. The lever is 10 USC 3243's "prompt opportunity" and written-standards duty.
2. **A reviewer copilot inside the ESA.** It would:
   - pre-check completeness against the guide (VE's 15-day job, with 7 analysts);
   - auto-compare reverse-engineered geometry and material to any government data;
   - auto-draft a disposition, with every claim linked to evidence;
   - flag what physical tests are still required.
   ELEVAN's $110k Phase I is the only evidence anyone has tried. What it would take: an SBIR Phase II or OTA with one ALC (Tinker or Robins), and an Authority to Operate (ATO) on government networks (IL4/5) for controlled technical information (CTI) and ITAR data.
3. **The government's missing baseline ("no data to compare").** Nobody does fleet-population metrology. That means scanning and CT of many serviceable parts from depot and stock, giving the Government a statistical as-built baseline (tolerance populations, material fingerprints) to judge any SAR against. RPPOB already allows bailment of stock, so the legal hook exists. What it would take: a DLA-funded "digital baseline" programme. AI and CT throughput make it cheap per NSN.
4. **Precedent and similarity approvals.** Each NSN restarts from zero. A similarity graph would let one approved Category III reverse-engineered SAR turn sibling NSNs into Category II. These are NSNs with the same material, process, feature class and supplier. What it would take: ESA policy accepting "family qualification", analogous to QPL/QML and to FAA PMA identicality by similarity.
5. **Re-screening the AMSC file with new economics.** The 25% savings factor and the cost-of-breakout assumptions are stale once AI cuts package cost. Nobody systematically mines FLIS/PUBLOG and DIBBS buy history for U, R, Q, P and L items whose breakout is now economic. Doing so would also reopen items DLA refuses as "low demand". What it would take: data access (FLIS is public via PUBLOG; buy history via DIBBS) and a re-coding request channel (the PGI 60-day status rule exists).
6. **Portfolio or batch SARs.** These would cover a whole platform subsystem (for example, C-130 non-CSI hardware) under one test plan with shared coupons. ORBIS data shows a 25% saving from batching. What it would take: DLA/ESA agreement on a portfolio SAR vehicle, plus SBIR or OTA funding instead of per-NSN Phase I awards.
7. **Model-credited testing for non-CSIs, then CSIs.** Use physics simulation plus historical first-article results to reduce or combine first-article tests. What it would take: one ESA's written acceptance, a track record on CSI = No items, then an extension to CSIs with a probabilistic risk argument. Respecting physics, fatigue and engine tests remain, but can be parallelised and run at scale in an owned lab.
8. **Attested evidence chain.** Instruments sign their data at capture, so a DCA can accept remote conformity and remote first-article witnessing. What it would take: AFMC acceptance of remote first-article witnessing (Form 260).
9. **A risk-accounting argument for CSIs.** The DCA currently weighs only the new-source risk, not the risk and readiness cost of sole-source lead times (1,261 days in the NPS paper). What it would take: an analytic framework accepted by the DCA, possibly through NDAA language.

## 11. AI plays on the verification side

- **A. "ESA in a box".** A reviewer workbench sold or given to ALC ESAs and DLA VE/CAO. It does intake, completeness, conformity to the guide, comparison against baseline, disposition drafting and queue analytics. The KPI is reviewer hours per disposition. The wedge is an SBIR Phase I/II at AFX-type topics, after ELEVAN's lapsed lead. The moat is the precedent corpus of dispositions, which no applicant can see.
- **B. A government digital-baseline service.** CT, scan and material fingerprinting of existing stock and fleet parts, sold to DLA as the "comparison data" whose absence causes rejects. It doubles as counterfeit and conformance screening.
- **C. A pre-submission scorer for applicants.** It scores a package against the ESA's guide and past rejects to predict the disposition, and could serve the 284+ SBIR SAR shops. It is a weaker business but a feeder.
- **D. An AMSC re-screening engine.** It mines FLIS, DIBBS buy history and AMSC codes to rank NSNs whose breakout is now economic, then files code-review requests and portfolio SARs. It feeds the Approval Factory directly.
- **E. A similarity-qualification engine.** A graph over NSNs that turns one approval into many. It needs policy acceptance.
- **The integrated play:** an Approval Factory that owns B and D on the applicant side and gives A to the reviewer. The factory's packages are then pre-formatted for the reviewer tool, which cuts its own queue time. The binding constraints are regulator acceptance (one ESA pilot), CUI/ITAR data handling (CMMC L2, FedRAMP/IL4) and DCA liability culture for CSIs.
