## 1. What a test actually costs, and where the time goes

All of the following are (recalled) industry ranges, used as order-of-magnitude anchors:
- Static tensile coupon at a Nadcap/17025 contract lab: about $150-400 per test, plus $100-500 per specimen to machine it. Most of that is labor: machining, measuring, gripping, extensometer setup, reporting and QA sign-off. The machine itself runs for minutes.
- High-cycle fatigue coupon: about $500-2,500 each. Frame time dominates. 10^7 cycles at 20-30 Hz takes roughly 4-6 days on one servo-hydraulic frame. A run-out at 10^8 cycles takes weeks.
- One S-N curve is about 15-30 coupons. A B-basis allowable is about 18+ specimens over 3+ batches, per property and per environment. A-basis needs about 55-75+. A full composite material qualification under NCAMP or CMH-17 has historically cost about $1-3M and taken 1-2 years (recalled).
- Queue time at commercial labs is typically 3-12 weeks. It peaks when aerospace and AM qualification demand surges (recalled). Most queue time is waiting for machining and scheduling, not machine run time.
- Inside a DLA SAR or FAA PMA package, physical testing is usually the smaller share of cost. The larger shares are engineering analysis, dimensional and material reverse engineering, and paperwork. The exception is critical or life-limited parts, where fatigue and spin testing can dominate (opinion, consistent with the prior review's $100k / 300-1,000 hr figure).

Physics clocks in mechanical testing:
1. Fatigue cycles, set by frequency times cycle count. This is the hard clock.
2. Creep and stress rupture, which take 1,000-10,000+ hr by definition.
3. Environmental conditioning: composite moisture conditioning to equilibrium (ETW) takes weeks to months.
4. Full-scale or component tests, for example aircraft wing fatigue tests that run for years.
Tensile, hardness, Charpy, chemistry and metallography have no meaningful physics clock. Their cost is labor and logistics.

## 2. Who is building automated or autonomous testing (evidence)

**Robotic static-test cells (mature, sold for high-volume QC, not for aerospace contract testing):**
- ZwickRoell sells the roboTest family. roboTest R holds up to 400 metal specimens, roboTest L 160, roboTest P 300 heavy-plate specimens, and roboTest I does Charpy. The company claims "Unsupervised 24/7 system operation." The customers it names are Tata Steel, Baosteel, BASF, Covestro, Henkel and KRICT, which are steel and polymer QC, with no aerospace named. https://www.zwickroell.com/products/automated-testing-systems/
- Instron (ITW), Shimadzu and MTS (test business now under ITW (recalled)) sell similar automated tensile and hardness cells. These cells handle high-volume, low-mix work well: one geometry, one material spec, millions of tests. They handle aerospace repair/PMA work poorly, because it is high-mix and low-volume and every part has its own coupon geometry and spec (opinion).

**Contract test labs (the incumbent capacity, little automation):**
- Westmoreland Mechanical Testing & Research (WMT&R) has 300,000+ sq ft, frames up to 1,000,000 lb, and is A2LA 17025 and Nadcap accredited. It markets "high-volume, quick turnaround," writes its control software in-house, and describes no robotic automation. https://www.wmtr.com/
- Element Materials Technology (Temasek-owned, roughly $1B+ revenue, recalled), Metcut Research, Touchstone, and NIAR at Wichita State (with NCAMP) are the other main ones.

**Shared-allowables infrastructure (a regulatory-acceptance precedent):**
- NCAMP at Wichita State/NIAR keeps a public, FAA- and EASA-accepted database of composite allowables. A user proves equivalency with a small test set instead of re-qualifying. The page says the AGATE process cut certification time 4x and cost 10x. FAA and EASA acceptance rests on FAA memo AIR100-2010-120-003 and EASA CM-S-004. https://www.wichita.edu/industry_and_defense/NIAR/Research/ncamp.php
- So a 10x cost cut from avoiding duplicate testing has already been accepted by regulators once. It came from a statistical and data-sharing mechanism, not from robots.
- MMPDS (metals) and CMH-17 are the equivalent handbooks (recalled).

**AI self-driving labs (well funded, aimed at discovery, not qualification testing):**
- Periodic Labs builds "models and autonomous labs to accelerate science," focused on superconductors and chip heat dissipation. Backers include a16z, Felicis, DST, NVentures and Accel; the round was reported as a ~$300M seed in 2025 (recalled). Its site mentions no mechanical testing. https://periodic.com/
- Lila Sciences builds "AI Science Factory" autonomous labs. It lists aerospace and defense and "high-performance structural materials" among its targets, and reports 2,942 catalysts synthesized and screened in 3 months. Its site describes no structural testing. Funding was reported as about $200M seed plus about $235M Series A in 2025 (recalled). https://www.lila.ai/
- Radical AI runs a self-driving materials lab. It does automated sample prep with Makino, tested against C103 refractory alloy at Purdue Applied Research Institute, and partners with Sandia. It describes no mechanical qualification testing. Funding was reported as about $55M seed in 2025 (recalled). https://www.radical-ai.com/
- Emerald Cloud Lab is a remote-controlled lab with 200+ instrument models running 24/7. It covers life sciences and analytical chemistry only; "Material Science" appears as an application example but no mechanical test methods are listed. https://www.emeraldcloudlab.com/
- Academic and government work (recalled): LBNL A-Lab (inorganic synthesis); AFRL ARES (Maruyama, the first autonomous research system, 2016); NIST AM-Bench; and high-throughput mechanical testing work in AM. Senvol (ML for AM material qualification, Navy/ONR and America Makes funded) is the closest company aimed at cutting qualification coupon counts (recalled).

**Gap (opinion, consistent with everything fetched):** no company found combines (a) a robotic, high-mix coupon factory (CNC plus metrology plus test plus report) with (b) Nadcap/17025 accreditation and DER/designee-acceptable data provenance, sold as a qualification and approval service. Discovery-SDL money (more than $800M across Periodic, Lila and Radical) goes to finding new materials. Qualification and approval testing of existing parts and materials is not their focus.

## 3. Could cost and queue time drop 10x? By test type

| Test type | Cost driver | 10x cost? | 10x queue? | What it takes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tensile, compression, shear, hardness, Charpy | Labor, specimen machining, scheduling | Yes, plausible. Robots exist; the missing piece is high-mix lights-out machining from CAD/spec | Yes. Most queue is waiting, not run time | Automated CAM to coupon, inline metrology, robotic test, auto-generated report, accredited |
| Chemistry, metallography, CT, residual stress (material forensics in reverse engineering) | Expert labor | Yes | Yes | Robotic sectioning, mounting and polishing, AI micrograph grading (ASTM E112 grain size, alpha-case, porosity) |
| HCF fatigue | Frame-hours (physics) | Partly. Cheap massively parallel frames (multi-specimen rigs, electrodynamic/resonant frames at 100-300 Hz) cut cost per coupon 3-10x | Yes, through parallelism | Hundreds of low-cost frames; accepted frequency-effect corrections |
| Very-high-cycle fatigue | Physics | Ultrasonic fatigue at 20 kHz reaches 10^9 cycles in about 14 hr (recalled) | Yes | Regulator acceptance of frequency equivalence (not accepted today for allowables) |
| Creep | Physics (1,000-10,000 hr) | Only via parallelism plus accepted accelerated or parametric methods (Larson-Miller extrapolation is already used) | Parallelism | Accepted prediction-plus-confirmation testing |
| Composite environmental conditioning | Diffusion physics | Thinner specimens or accelerated conditioning, which have limits | Partly | Accepted accelerated conditioning |
| Allowables (count of coupons) | Statistics fixed in CMH-17/MMPDS | Yes, about 3-10x fewer coupons with Bayesian/ML priors plus equivalency (NCAMP precedent) | Yes | FAA/DLA acceptance of model-informed allowables. This is the biggest lever, and it needs regulator acceptance, not physics |
| Witnessing and conformity (FAA DAR/ACO, DLA first article) | Human travel and scheduling | Yes | Yes. Weeks of scheduling become hours | Signed, tamper-evident raw data plus video plus remote witnessing (FAA allowed remote inspection during COVID (recalled)) |

Net view (opinion): for a reverse-engineering and approval house, a 10x drop in queue time is very plausible. It comes from owning captive, 24/7, high-mix robotic capacity and skipping the contract-lab queue. A 10x drop in test cost is plausible for static tests and material ID. Fatigue gets about 3-5x from parallelism and cheap frames. Fatigue and allowables get to 10x only if regulators accept fewer coupons through model-informed statistics. That is a regulator-acceptance constraint, not a physics one.

## 4. The binding constraints and what would change them
- **Physics:** cycles and creep hours cannot be compressed except by frequency, which needs equivalence data, or by parallelism, which is a cost question. Changing it: build a huge validated dataset comparing frequency against conventional results across alloys, then petition for accepted ultrasonic/resonant correction factors.
- **Law and regulator acceptance:** 14 CFR 21.303 (PMA test reports), AC 33.70-1 for life-limited parts (recalled), CMH-17/MMPDS statistics, and DLA FAT requirements. Changing it: the NCAMP precedent shows FAA/EASA will accept shared or statistical routes when they are run through an FAA-endorsed body. A "model-informed allowables" route would need an FAA policy memo plus a CMH-17 working-group chapter.
- **Liability and trust:** data provenance is the real blocker for automation. Today trust rests on the accredited lab plus a human signature. Changing it: cryptographically signed sensor streams, continuous calibration traceability, and video, so the evidence itself is auditable. That lets a DER or DAR accept remotely and at scale.
- **Data access:** OEM allowables are proprietary. MMPDS is public for metals. A self-run robotic lab creates its own allowables data asset, which compounds over time.

## 5. AI plays on the verification side (opinion)
1. **Captive "test foundry" inside the Approval Factory.** A lights-out, high-mix pipeline: CAD/spec, then auto-CAM coupon, then CMM, then robotic test, then a signed report. KPI: test turnaround in days rather than weeks, which shortens the approval loop. It can be sold to others later, as Element and WMT&R do today.
2. **Material forensics robot for reverse engineering.** Automated chemistry, microstructure, hardness mapping, CT and residual stress on the OEM part, with AI-written material equivalency rationale. This replaces most of the expert hours in the "what is this part made of and how was it processed" step.
3. **Model-informed allowables and equivalency (an NCAMP for legacy military parts).** Bayesian priors from physics models plus prior data cut the coupon count. Pursue FAA and DLA acceptance through a CMH-17/MMPDS-style body. This is the largest lever.
4. **Verifiable evidence chain and remote witnessing.** Tamper-evident test data plus video plus AI anomaly flags, so DER/DAR/DCMA reviewers approve remotely. This attacks reviewer hours, which is the second half of the KPI.
5. **Massively parallel cheap fatigue frames plus frequency-equivalence datasets.** A hardware play that turns fatigue from frame-limited to capital-limited.
