# s06: Qualification test planning and execution (coupons, component tests, fatigue, environmental). Cost and time structure; what is truly physics-clock bound vs scheduling/queue bound.

Search budget used: 3 WebSearch calls. Several fetches returned 403 (FAA ACT ARC PDF, metal-am). Figures marked (recalled) come from memory, not from a source fetched this session. Everything marked (opinion/estimate) is my reasoning.

## 1. Where testing sits in the approval pathway
For a reverse-engineered replacement part, the route depends on which regulator approves it:
- DLA Source Approval Request (SAR). The applicant shows the part is equivalent to the OEM part using drawings, material and process data, and dimensional inspection of a sample. The Engineering Support Activity (ESA: the Air Force, Navy or Army engineering office) then decides whether a first article test (FAT) or qualification test is needed. For Critical Safety Items (CSIs) the ESA usually asks for more: material certifications, sometimes fatigue or functional tests, and a production lot FAT. (recalled; DLA SAR guidance, the Aviation "SAR package" checklist)
- FAA PMA (14 CFR 21.303, Order 8110.42). The applicant shows the design meets airworthiness requirements either by identicality (rare without a license) or by test and computation. Test and computation means a test plan approved or accepted by the ACO or a DER, conformity inspection of the test articles (by an FAA inspector or DAR), witnessed tests, and test reports signed off on 8110-3 forms. (recalled)
- In practice, most PMA and SAR parts by count are non-critical: brackets, bushings, seals, liners, ducts, panels. For those, "qualification" is dimensional and material equivalence plus a handful of functional or environmental tests. Heavy fatigue campaigns are concentrated in a minority of parts: rotating, life-limited and primary-structure parts. (opinion, consistent with HEICO's mix)

## 2. Test types and their physics clocks
The physics clock is the time the specimen must be under load, cycling, exposure or conditioning. No scheduling change can shorten it; only an accepted acceleration method or analysis credit can.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Static tensile, compression or shear coupon | minutes | 6 to 30 | $150 to $600 each | trivial clock |
| High-cycle fatigue (HCF) coupon, servo-hydraulic, 10^7 runout | 10^7 cycles at 20 to 50 Hz = 2.3 to 5.8 days | 15 to 30 for an S-N curve | $1k to $5k each; one S-N curve $30k to $150k | clock is real but parallelizable across frames |

Physics-clock arithmetic for a "hard" metallic PMA or SAR part (estimate):
- Static plus an S-N curve on 20 coupons at 30 Hz to 10^7: about 4 days of frame time per coupon. On one frame that is 80 days serially. With 20 frames in parallel it is 4 to 6 days.
- Component fatigue on 2 articles: 3 to 8 weeks of frame time.
- An environmental subset (temperature, vibration, fluids, humidity): about 2 to 3 weeks. Run serially on one article, it is driven by the 10-day humidity test.
- Unavoidable physics floor for a fatigue-critical metallic part, with full parallelization: about 4 to 8 weeks. For a non-fatigue-critical part: about 1 to 2 weeks, or days.
- Typical elapsed time actually observed: 6 to 18 months from test-plan start to approved report for a fatigue-critical PMA (recalled industry norm; opinion). For SARs, DLA engineering evaluation alone has historically taken 6 to 12+ months. Time to award has been reported as 1 to 3 years (recalled; GAO and DLA statements about SAR backlog).

=> Physics clocks account for roughly 5 to 25% of elapsed time. The remaining 75 to 95% is queue and coordination (estimate).

## 3. Queue and scheduling clocks (not physics)
1. Test plan authoring and approval. A DER, ACO or ESA reviews the plan. Writing it takes weeks to months; agreeing it with the regulator takes months. Precedent matters ("what did they accept last time?"), and that knowledge sits in DER heads.
2. Material procurement. Aerospace forgings and castings had 40 to 80+ week lead times in 2023 to 2025 (recalled). Allowables need multiple heats or lots. This is the largest single non-physics clock.
3. Specimen and test-article manufacture. Machining, heat treat and NDT take 2 to 8 weeks. Conformity of test articles requires an FAA inspector or DAR visit before testing.
4. Lab slot queue. Independent labs (Element, NTS, LTI, Westmoreland, Touchstone, NIAR, SwRI) quote lead times of weeks to a few months on fatigue frames and chambers (recalled; opinion). LTI added 16 fatigue frames for $3M+ to reach 52 frames in 2024 to meet aerospace and AM demand (metal-am.com, Jan 2024, from search snippet). Element has 200+ environmental chambers, 120 dynamics systems and 32 Nadcap labs, and publishes no lead times (element.com fetch). A frame costs about $190k installed (derived: $3M / 16).
5. Witness scheduling. The FAA or DER or DAR witness must be present at test setup and at critical points. Remote witnessing was used in 2020 to 2022 (recalled) but is not standard.
6. Failure, root cause and re-test loops. One unexpected coupon or article failure adds months: new material, new specimens, a new queue position. This is the biggest variance driver.
7. Data reduction, report writing and 8110-3 sign-off. Weeks of DER time. Reports are long PDFs compiled by hand.
8. Regulator review of results. Months. FAA ACO staffing is thin. DLA ESA engineers handle large SAR queues.

## 4. Cost structure (estimate, for a typical $100k package)
- Engineering (reverse engineering, analysis, plan, report): 40 to 60%
- Test execution (lab fees, specimens, articles): 20 to 40%
- DER/DAR fees plus conformity: 10 to 20%
- Material: 5 to 15%. Small in dollars, but dominant in time.
For a fatigue-critical or CSI part, testing can reach $250k to $1M+ and become the majority of the cost.

## 5. Existing players touching this step
- Test labs: Element Materials Technology (private, Temasek-owned, about $1B+ revenue, recalled), NTS (National Technical Systems), Laboratory Testing Inc (PA), Westmoreland Mechanical Testing & Research, Touchstone Research Lab, NIAR at Wichita State (including NCAMP), Southwest Research Institute (runs USAF ASIP and aging-aircraft work, including C-130 and KC-135 structural integrity, recalled), Wyle (now KBR). These are capacity sellers, not plan optimizers.
- Shared allowables: NCAMP (FAA-backed). It claims the shared-database approach cut composite certification time "by a factor of four" and cost by a factor of 10 (wichita.edu NCAMP page). MMPDS (Battelle-administered) does the same for metals.
- Test data software: Nominal. It raised an $80M Series B-2 at a $1B valuation in March 2026, led by Founders Fund, after a $75M Series B in June 2025. It reports a $53M sole-source IDIQ to standardize data at the Air Force Test Center (newsletter claim, unverified) and 7x revenue growth (siliconangle.com). Sift (telemetry), Epsilon3 (test procedure execution, recalled ~$15M Series A 2023). None of these produces regulator-accepted qualification evidence. They sit on test data plumbing.
- Simulation and test reduction: Monolith AI (UK, ML to cut physical tests, recalled), PhysicsX (UK, recalled ~$135M Series B 2025), Ansys/Synopsys, Dassault 3DS "Smarter Testing" (Airbus wing demonstrator, vendor claim), Hexagon. They push certification by analysis (CbA). FAA AC 20-146A (seats by computer modeling, active, issued 2024-11-29) is the clearest precedent for analysis replacing tests. The FAA ACT-ARC issued CbA recommendations (Rec 21-5; the PDF was 403 in this session). EASA accepts virtual testing case by case (AIAA Aerospace America).
- AM qualification: Senvol (ML for AM qualification, SBIR-funded, recalled), America Makes, the AFRL/DARPA Open Manufacturing legacy. AM parts face the same allowables problem.
- Frame OEMs: MTS (Amphenol? no, ITW/MTS split; recalled uncertain), Instron (ITW), Zwick. Robotic coupon handling exists (Zwick and Instron autoloaders) but is used mostly for static tests.

Gap: nobody I found offers end-to-end, outcome-priced "qualification evidence as a service" for PMA or SAR, meaning one party that owns plan, material, specimens, frames, witnessing and the report and is measured on approval cycle time. Labs sell frame-hours. Software firms sell dashboards. DERs sell signatures.

## 6. What would need to be done (assuming plentiful compute and intelligence)
1. Test plan compiler. Ingest the part's function, loads, material, the regulatory basis (14 CFR 25.xxx / MIL-HDBK-1530 ASIP / ESA SAR checklist) and a corpus of previously accepted plans. Output the minimum sufficient test matrix with a citation to the precedent behind each item. This attacks the plan-approval queue, which may be the largest addressable chunk. Requires data access to past accepted plans, which DERs, HEICO, Wencor and the ESAs hold.
2. Equivalence-by-evidence engine. Prove that a reverse-engineered part's material and process fall inside existing MMPDS/CMH-17/NCAMP allowables, so no new allowables testing is needed. The engine would do chemistry and microstructure matching from CT, EBSD and spectroscopy of the OEM part. This deletes testing rather than speeding it up.
3. Bayesian or sequential test design that regulators accept. Stop testing when the confidence bound is met, using priors from published S-N data. Today N is fixed by handbook. Changing that needs an AC or issue paper, and a test case with a willing ACO or ESA. DoD ESAs have more discretion than the FAA, so start there.
4. Lights-out, massively parallel coupon lab. 100+ frames, robotic specimen loading, 24/7 operation, in-house CNC and heat treat for specimens. This collapses the lab queue and machining clocks to the physics floor. Capex is about $190k per frame, so $20M to $40M for a serious lab (estimate). Owning capacity is also a moat in a capacity-constrained market.
5. Validated acceleration. Ultrasonic HCF with frequency-effect correction models, elevated-temperature creep with Larson-Miller models, thin-traveler moisture conditioning with diffusion models. Each needs a validation dataset plus regulator acceptance, which is an AC or MMPDS-coordination-committee-level effort.
6. Digital witnessing and conformity. Tamper-evident sensor streams, video and signed specimen chain of custody so witnesses can verify remotely and asynchronously. The witness-scheduling queue goes away. Requires FAA/DLA policy acceptance, with COVID-era remote inspection as the precedent.
7. Auto-generated, regulator-formatted evidence packages. Raw data feeds a reduced analysis, which feeds a report draft in 8110-3 or SAR format with full traceability. This cuts DER and reviewer hours, the founder's KPI.
8. Failure-prediction pre-screen. Simulate and pre-test cheap surrogates so the expensive article does not fail. Reduces re-test loops, the largest variance driver.
9. Material inventory hedge. Pre-buy and certify lots or heats of common legacy alloys (7075-T73, 4340, 17-4PH, Ti-6Al-4V, Inconel 718) and own a pre-characterized material bank with allowables. This turns the 40 to 80 week material clock into stock on the shelf. It is a balance-sheet play.

## 7. What it would take to change each binding constraint
- Physics clock: accepted acceleration (validation data plus a committee). Analysis credit (CbA under the AC 20-146A pattern). Parallelism (frames). Deletion (equivalence to existing allowables).
- Law: no statute fixes test counts. N comes from ACs, handbooks (MMPDS, CMH-17, MIL-HDBK-1530) and ESA practice. These are soft law and can be changed through ARC recommendations, issue papers, special conditions and ESA waivers.
- Regulator acceptance: build a track record with one ESA (for example the Robins AFB C-130 or Tinker AFB KC-135 engineering offices) and publish the method so others can copy it. Offer the regulator a tool too: the same plan compiler can serve the reviewer.
- Liability: the PMA or SAR holder carries it. A house that owns test and evidence end to end has clearer accountability, which helps.
- Data access: past accepted test plans and reports are the corpus. Sources are FOIA (limited), DLA Technical Data Packages (TDPs) once acquired, partnerships with retiring DERs, and the house's own growing archive.
