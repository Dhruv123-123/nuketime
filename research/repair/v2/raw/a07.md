## 1. Economics of being a paid verifier

### Big Four audit (evidence: Wikipedia "Big Four accounting firms", FY2025 table)
| Firm | Revenue | Audit and assurance | Consulting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deloitte (FY to May 2025) | $70.5bn | $14.7bn | $43.7bn |
| PwC (Jun 2025) | $56.9bn | $19.8bn | $24.4bn |
| EY (Jun 2025) | $53.2bn | $17.9bn | $22.6bn |
| KPMG (Sep 2025) | $39.8bn | $14.1bn | $16.4bn |
| Total | $219.0bn | $66.5bn (~30%) | $107.1bn (48.6%) |

- About 1.52M employees. Assurance revenue per head is low, because audit is a labour pyramid priced by the hour.
- Concentration: the Big Four audited 99% of the FTSE 100 (2011) and about 95% of the FTSE 250 (2019). For the S&P 500 the figure is around 99% (recalled).
- Enforcement: the PCAOB brought only 18 enforcement cases against the Big Four in 16 years. Over the same period inspectors found about 808 deficient audits (~31% of those inspected), and action followed in about 6.6% of them. The KPMG deficiency rate was 36.6%. In other words, the regulator finds a lot of failures and rarely punishes them.
- Total US public-company audit fees are roughly $15-20bn a year (recalled, Audit Analytics). Audit is a legally mandated purchase: the SEC requires audited statements from every issuer, so demand is set by law, not by how much value the audit adds.
- Who can sign: the signature must come from a PCAOB-registered firm (for issuers), the firm must be majority CPA-owned under state boards and the AICPA (51% rule), and the partner must be licensed. Private equity has moved into mid-tier firms through the "alternative practice structure", which splits the business in two: the attest entity stays CPA-owned and PE owns the advisory and tech side. Examples: Grant Thornton US with New Mountain Capital (2024), Baker Tilly with Hellman & Friedman (recalled).

### Rating agencies (evidence: Wikipedia "Credit rating agency", "Moody's")
- Global share: S&P 50.0%, Moody's 31.7%, Fitch 12.5%, about 94% combined.
- Moody's total revenue was $5.9bn in 2023 (Wikipedia). The Moody's Investors Service ratings unit runs at roughly 55-60% operating margins, and S&P Ratings is similar on about $4.5bn+ revenue (recalled).
- Agencies moved from subscriber-pays to issuer-pays from about 1970 (Moody's first). One reason was the photocopier: subscribers could no longer stop free-riding. Today eight of the nine or ten NRSROs use issuer-pays; Egan-Jones is the subscriber-pays exception. In 2005 Moody's said about 90% of rating-agency revenue came from issuer fees.
- Structured finance rose from 28% of Moody's revenue in 1998 to about 50% in 2007, and the crisis followed. 73% of Moody's 2006 AAA mortgage-backed securities were junk two years later (over $800bn). Penalties: Moody's paid about $864m in January 2017, an EU fine of €3.7m in 2021 over conflicts of interest, and S&P paid about $1.375bn in 2015 (recalled).
- Dodd-Frank (2010) made ratings subject to expert liability. In practice the agencies blocked it: they refused consent to be named in ABS registration statements, and the SEC issued a no-action letter. Section 939A removed rating references from federal rules, yet reliance on the big agencies is still built in through NAIC capital rules, Basel and investment mandates. The proposed Franken "15E(w)" random-assignment board was studied by the SEC and never put in place (recalled).
- New entrants: Kroll Bond Rating Agency (founded 2010) took real share in CMBS, ABS and private credit (recalled). That shows a new designated verifier can win where the regulator has already granted a slot (NRSRO) and the incumbents are slow or conflicted in a niche. The private-credit boom produced "private letter ratings" from small NRSROs (notably Egan-Jones). In 2024-25 the NAIC moved to let its SVO challenge ratings it sees as inflated (recalled). Cheap or loose verifiers draw a regulator backlash.

### Lesson for the Approval Factory
The margin goes to whoever holds the regulator-recognised signature (NRSRO status, PCAOB registration, licensed partner), not to the analysis. Rating agencies earn about 60% margins on what is mostly analytical work because the regulation requires their rating. The same holds on the aerospace side: DERs, ODA holders and DLA engineering support activities are the signature, and the reverse-engineering itself is the commodity.

## 2. Independence problems (and how they map to aerospace)
- Issuer-pays or client-pays: the party being verified picks and pays the verifier, which leads to opinion shopping and rating shopping. In the FAA world, a consultant DER is paid by the applicant, and an ODA (Boeing on the 737 MAX) is an employee of the applicant acting as the FAA. It is the same conflict, and the MAX was its rating-agency moment. After the crashes, the Aircraft Certification, Safety and Accountability Act of 2020 tightened ODA oversight.
- Selling other services to the audit client: Arthur Andersen and Enron led to SOX 2002 section 201, which bans most consulting for audit clients. EY's plan to split audit from consulting (Project Everest) was abandoned in April 2023 (recalled). The EU requires audit-firm rotation every 10-20 years under its 2014 regulation (recalled).
- Rules do not fix weak sign-offs: Wirecard (EY missed €1.9bn of cash), Carillion (KPMG), Autonomy (Deloitte fined), and Deloitte fined £15m by the FRC in 2020.
- Opinion: independence can be bought structurally only by (a) investor-pays or regulator-assigned verifiers, or (b) verification evidence that can be checked independently, so the verifier's honesty matters less. AI makes (b) cheap. Re-running a verification is close to free if the evidence is machine-readable, and that changes the independence problem.

## 3. AI disruption of audit: who is doing what
- **Fieldguide** (fieldguide.com, fetched): an agentic AI platform for audit and advisory firms. Its "Field Agents" handle testing, evidence verification, planning, review and financial-statement drafting. It claims "more than half of the top 100 firms" as customers (Forvis Mazars, BerryDunn, Schellman), a 55% cut in engagement hours, 50%+ better realization and an NPS of 84. The site gives no funding figures. Recalled: about $30M Series B in 2024 (Bessemer, 8VC) and a larger 2025 round.
- **DataSnipper** (Amsterdam): an Excel add-in that matches audit evidence to documents. $100M Series B in 2024 at about a $1bn valuation (recalled).
- **MindBridge** (Ottawa): AI anomaly detection across 100% of the general ledger (recalled).
- **AuditBoard**: internal audit and GRC software, acquired by Hg for about $3bn+ in 2024 (recalled).
- **Vanta, Drata, Secureframe, Delve (YC), Thoropass**: they automate evidence for SOC 2 and ISO 27001, but a licensed CPA firm still signs the report. Thoropass bundles its own affiliated audit firm, which vertically integrates platform and signer. Vanta was valued at about $4bn in 2025 (recalled). This is the closest working analogue to an "AI-native verifier": software produces the evidence and a captive or partner licensed signer issues the attestation. The weakness is that cheap SOC 2 "report mills" have damaged trust in the credential (opinion, widely reported).
- **Big Four in-house:** Deloitte Omnia, PwC Aura, EY Canvas/Helix, KPMG Clara. Announced spending: PwC $1bn on AI (2023), KPMG and Microsoft about $2bn (2023), EY.ai about $1.4bn (2023) (all recalled). The incumbents are automating the labour pyramid and keeping the signature.
- **Regulator acceptance moving:** the PCAOB adopted amendments to AS 1105 and AS 2301 for technology-assisted analysis in June 2024, effective for fiscal years starting on or after 15 December 2025 (recalled). That is direct evidence that a standards body will codify machine-performed testing of whole populations. In 2025 the PCAOB was politically weakened: a proposed fold into the SEC, Chair Williams' departure and budget cuts (recalled).
- What AI has not disrupted: the signature, the liability, and the issuer-pays selection. Audit fees have not collapsed. The firms are keeping productivity gains as margin, and partner pyramids are thinning (fewer graduate hires, reported 2024-25, recalled).

## 4. Implications for the verification side of the Approval Factory
1. **The tool-vendor trap.** Selling AI to existing verifiers (Fieldguide or DataSnipper, sold to auditors) is a real business but takes a thin slice, while the signer keeps the margin. In aerospace the equivalent is selling "AI for DERs", which is adjacent rather than the prize.
2. **The vertical-integration path (Thoropass/Vanta pattern).** Own or partner with the licensed signer. In aerospace that means employing consultant DERs or a DAS/ODA in house, so the house both produces the evidence and signs within delegated authority. HEICO already does this through its own DER and repair-station network; the AI-native version cuts the cost per package.
3. **The regulator-side play (Kroll pattern).** Become a new designated verifier in a slot where incumbents are slow. DLA source approval is the clearest case: engineering support activities (AF/Navy/Army ESAs) review source approval requests, the backlog is the bottleneck, and approval takes 1-3 years (recalled from a prior review). An AI-native verification service sold to the government (an "independent technical evaluator" for SAR packages) would be investor-pays and avoid the issuer-pays conflict.
4. **Full-population evidence instead of sampling.** Audit moved from sampling to testing 100% of transactions with AI. The aerospace equivalent is replacing first-article inspection plus small-sample qualification with per-serial digital records (CT scan, as-built metrology, process telemetry), machine-checked against the technical data package. Physics tests still have to run, but the reviewer hours spent checking that the evidence is complete and consistent can go to near zero.
5. **Evidence anyone can re-run.** Package the approval evidence so the regulator, or a second independent AI, can re-run the verification deterministically. That makes the verifier's independence less critical and attacks the conflict at its root. Today nobody issues SAR, PMA or DER packages in a machine-checkable form.

## 5. What would need to be done (assuming abundant intelligence and compute)
- A machine-readable standard for approval packages (SAR/TDP/PMA) that a regulator-run checker can test. This is the counterpart of XBRL plus PCAOB AS 1105: it would let DLA ESAs and the FAA accept automated completeness and consistency checks.
- A regulator-recognised status for "AI-performed verification" through FAA policy, such as an AC or order on tools that generate compliance findings (like the AS 1105 change). The path is an FAA Section 2/ACSAA rulemaking push or a DLA pilot under DFARS. The binding constraint is regulator acceptance. The change would come from a pilot that shows lower defect rates than human review, much as the PCAOB accepted technology-assisted analysis.
- Investor-pays or regulator-pays verification: a contract with DLA or the services to pre-screen SARs as an independent evaluator, paid per package.
- Liability: verifiers survive by having a signer who carries liability insurance plus an LLP structure. An AI verification house needs a captive DER/ODA with errors and omissions insurance, plus statistics from running in parallel with human review that underwriters can price.
- Ownership: the aerospace side has no 51%-CPA-style rule. A company can hold an ODA, and DERs can be employees, so the vertical-integration path is legally easier than in audit. That is an advantage.

## 6. Surprises
- PCAOB inspectors found about 31% of Big Four audits deficient, and only about 6.6% of those led to action. The verification signature carries value even when its quality is poor.
- Rating agencies turned the Dodd-Frank liability threat into nothing by withholding consent, which froze the ABS market. Verifiers in a statutorily required slot can resist accountability.
- The move to issuer-pays was caused by a copying technology (the photocopier). An AI "copy" of verification could likewise shift who pays.
- Deloitte, the largest firm, gets only about 21% of revenue from audit. The signature is the door-opener for consulting.
