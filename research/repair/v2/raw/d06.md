# d06: Insurers and lenders as payers for verification (aviation insurers, engineering insurers, lessors' residual value): what would they pay for verified evidence?

Method note: the shared WebSearch quota was already used up when this agent started (0 of 3 searches ran). Everything below comes from directly fetched pages (cited with URLs) or from memory, marked **(recalled)**. Recalled figures need checking before they go in a YC application.

## 1. Short answer

- The military first wedge (DLA SARs on C-130/KC-135) has **no insurer or lender payer**. The US government self-insures, and contractors who build to government specs are largely shielded by the government-contractor defense (*Boyle v. United Technologies*, 1988) **(recalled)**. On that side, verification is paid for by the program office or DLA, or by the Approval Factory itself.
- On the **commercial side (FAA PMA/DER), lessors and their lenders are the gatekeepers, and today they are mostly the blocker, not the payer.** Lessors own roughly 40%+ of the world jet fleet (Wikipedia, citing 2017 data: 40%; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_lessor). Lease return conditions commonly bar PMA parts and DER repairs, or require lessor consent, because appraisers and secondary buyers discount "non-OEM content" **(recalled; this is industry practice, and the clause wording varies by lease)**. A PMA approval that a lessor will not accept is worth much less than it looks, because every leased aircraft or engine operator is barred from using it.
- So the verification product insurers and lenders would pay for is **not "help us get FAA approval."** It is **"prove this non-OEM part or repair does not impair value or raise risk, and stand behind that claim with capital."** That is a rating plus a warranty: a value warranty or residual-value wrap on PMA/DER content, priced from evidence.
- There is strong historical precedent that **insurers create verification bodies when they need non-OEM or uncertain hardware made legible**:
  - Lloyd's Register was founded in 1760 by underwriters to grade the ships they insured. Class status still gates whether a ship can be insured (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd%27s_Register, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_society).
  - Hartford Steam Boiler was founded in 1866 to sell inspection and insurance together. It imposed design rules on insured boilers (the 1919 "Hartford Loop"). It has 1,200+ engineers and inspectors, about half its staff, and was bought by Munich Re in April 2009 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartford_Steam_Boiler).
  - Other examples **(recalled)**: Underwriters Laboratories (1894, with fire-insurer backing); FM Approvals (owned by the FM Global mutual insurer); German TÜVs (from 1860s boiler-inspection associations); and **CAPA**, the Certified Automotive Parts Association. CAPA was founded in 1987 with auto-insurer backing to certify non-OEM collision parts so insurers could specify them instead of OEM parts. Its history includes the *Avery v. State Farm* verdict over non-OEM parts: about $1.2B in 1999, reversed by the Illinois Supreme Court in 2005 **(recalled)**.
- **No one does the "CAPA/HSB for aviation PMA/DER" job today: an independent rating of non-OEM parts and repairs that lessors, appraisers and insurers accept, backed by a carrier.** This is the strongest verification-side play I found. It complements the Approval Factory and can feed it: the rating turns each approval into value lessors can accept.

## 3. Evidence on insurers already underwriting "verified performance"

- Munich Re **aiSure**: it says it pioneered AI insurance in 2018. It backs vendor *performance warranties* and *value warranties*, for example **Barkr (AI luxury-asset valuations, with Munich Re covering Barkr's value warranty)**, MKIII (AI credit model), SeismicAI, and Deep Instinct. Mosaic uses a "parametric-like structure" (https://www.munichre.com/en/solutions/for-industry-clients/insure-ai.html). This is the exact template for "AI-verified part or repair plus a carrier-backed value or performance warranty."
- **Armilla**: a Lloyd's coverholder backed by Chaucer, AXIS and Convex. Limits go up to $25M aggregate and $5M per model. It prices from "a technical review of each system" and offers a performance warranty (https://www.armilla.ai/). This shows carriers will price based on third-party technical verification.
- **HSB** today also sells IoT sensors (Meshify) plus inspection services (https://www.munichre.com/hsb/en.html). It is an insurer that owns the verification workforce.

## 4. Existing players on records and provenance (the adjacent verification layer)

- **flydocs**: digital records management back to an aircraft's "birth"; Asset Trading Solution for back-to-birth gaps on life-limited parts; AI-powered component inspection integrated with AMOS (Swiss AviationSoftware). It claims 75+ partners (easyJet, Qantas, Qatar, Cathay, Airbus, Northrop Grumman) (https://www.flydocs.aero/). It is adjacent, not a direct threat: it digitizes records but does not rate PMA/DER equivalence or take risk.
- Others **(recalled, not verified this run)**: Veryon, Traxxall, Ramco, AMOS, Bluetail (business aviation records), ProvenAir / SkyThread / Aerotrax (blockchain-style parts provenance), Boeing AerData, Cirium (values data). None that I know of issues insurer-backed value-neutrality ratings for non-OEM content.
- **AOG Technics (2023)** **(recalled)**: a London broker supplied CFM56 parts with forged EASA Form 1 release certificates. Initial reporting put the number of affected engines at 100+, across airlines including TAP, Southwest, United, American, Delta and Virgin Australia, and the UK Serious Fraud Office prosecuted the founder. The SFO page returned 503 on fetch, so the figures are unverified. The scandal showed that the industry's verification of release paperwork is visual and trust-based. Lessors and insurers absorbed the cost of grounding, inspection and record reconstruction.

## 5. The verification pathway: what is done, what nobody does

| Step | Who does it today | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Design substantiation for PMA/DER (tests, analysis) | PMA holder's engineers + FAA ACO/DER | The Approval Factory itself |
| FAA approval | FAA | Regulator acceptance; physics clocks on tests |
| Part provenance (8130-3 / Form 1 authenticity) | Visual checks by receiving inspectors | **Not machine-verified**: no cryptographic or registry check against the issuer. AOG Technics shows the gap. |
| Fleet reliability of PMA vs OEM | Anecdote; HEICO and MARPA (trade association) claims | **No public, neutral, actuarial-grade comparison**, even though the FAA Service Difficulty Reporting System (SDRS) data is public **(recalled)** and could seed one |
| Lessor acceptance | Bilateral lease negotiation, case by case | **No standard rating or clause.** No Aviation Working Group (AWG) or ISTAT standard that says "rated content X is value-neutral." |
| Appraisal haircut | Appraiser judgment | No data-driven adjustment |
| Risk transfer | None for value impact; products liability only | **No residual-value or value warranty specific to PMA/DER content** |

## 6. Binding constraints and what it would take to change each

- **Physics clocks**: fleet reliability evidence accrues in flight hours. Mitigations: start with parts that already have long PMA service histories (HEICO adds about 400-550 PMAs a year; Delta, United and American have flown PMA for decades **(recalled)**); mine SDRS and airline reliability data; run accelerated testing for new parts.
- **Data access**: airline and MRO shop findings, teardown reports, and lessor records sit in private AMOS, Ramco and flydocs silos. To change this: a data-sharing pool where contributors get cheaper insurance or ratings (the CAPA and insurer-pool model). The insurer is the natural pool convener.
- **Law and liability**: a value warranty is an insurance product and needs a carrier or a Lloyd's coverholder (Armilla is the model). The startup holds the evidence and model; the carrier holds the capital.
- **Regulator acceptance**: not needed for value ratings, since this is a commercial norm and not airworthiness. That makes it faster than the FAA path. The FAA would only matter for a suspect-parts registry; a voluntary industry registry first is plausible.
- **Norm acceptance (the real constraint)**: the lessor community (AWG, ISTAT) and two or three big lessors must agree to reference the rating in return conditions. The OEMs (GE/CFM, Pratt & Whitney, RR) have a strong incentive to oppose. Some OEMs offer lessor and airline programs tied to OEM parts **(recalled)**.

## 7. What lenders and insurers would plausibly pay (opinion, built from the figures above)

- Per-asset provenance and records verification at lease transition: $20-100k per aircraft, versus today's manual audits and weeks of downtime. Even 1,000 transitions a year would be $20-100M of revenue. **(opinion)**
- A value warranty on PMA/DER-content engines: a premium of a few percent of the protected value gap. If PMA/DER saves about $1-2M per shop visit and the warranty costs $50-200k, the airline or lessor pays it happily. The startup takes a fee or a share of the coverholder's commission. **(opinion)**
- An insurer-funded independent test lab or "approvals" mark (FM Approvals/UL model) for orphaned parts in energy and industry: paid by manufacturers seeking the mark, required by insurers. **(opinion)**

## 8. AI plays on the verification side

1. **"CAPA/HSB for aviation non-OEM content"**: an AI-built reliability and value model for PMA/DER parts and repairs, trained on SDRS, teardown, shop-finding and appraisal data. It issues ratings that lessors and appraisers accept, wrapped in a Lloyd's or Munich Re value warranty. The moat is the data pool plus carrier capacity, not the model.
2. **Machine-verifiable provenance**: AI forensic checks of 8130-3/Form 1 certificates and back-to-birth trails, matched against issuer registries, sold to lessors, insurers and MROs after AOG Technics. This is adjacent to flydocs, but positioned as *assurance with liability*, not storage.
3. **Insurer-side claim avoidance**: rapid AI-generated DER repair substantiation that turns a constructive total loss, or a long business-interruption outage, into a repair. Sold to hull and machinery-breakdown insurers, who capture the savings directly. This shares an engine with the Approval Factory.
4. **Evidence once, used by many**: the Approval Factory's substantiation package becomes, with no extra test work, the input for the FAA/DLA approval, the lessor rating, the appraiser haircut, and the insurer's underwriting. The same verification dossier yields several revenue streams.
5. **Energy and industrial orphan-spares assurance** with HSB, FM or Allianz: the insurer requires, or discounts premiums for, plants that hold verified reverse-engineered spares, and pays to shorten business-interruption outages.

## 9. Surprises

- On the commercial side, lessors and lenders are the de facto second regulator for PMA. An FAA approval does not equal market access.
- Insurers have a 260-year track record of creating the verification institutions they need (LR 1760, HSB 1866, UL 1894, CAPA 1987). Aviation non-OEM parts never got one.
- Munich Re already backs AI *value* warranties (Barkr), which is structurally what a PMA value-neutrality warranty would be.
- The military first wedge has no insurance payer at all, so this play mainly strengthens the commercial (PMA/DER) phase. It could be the reason a lessor-heavy airline adopts the Approval Factory's parts.
