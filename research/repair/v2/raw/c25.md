# c25: Automotive and heavy equipment aftermarket parts validation (CAPA certification, NSF automotive, OE-equivalence testing, Dorman, LKQ engineering): how equivalence is proven and any AI.

Search budget: 3 WebSearch used. Figures marked (recalled) come from memory and were not fetched this session.

## 1. The key structural fact
For most automotive aftermarket parts, no regulator approves anything. Equivalence is proven privately, for three audiences:
1. **Insurers / collision estimating.** CAPA certification lets an insurer specify a non-OEM part on an estimate and defend that choice against "like kind and quality" (LKQ) statutes and diminished-value suits.
2. **Retail and distribution buyers.** AutoZone, O'Reilly and Advance want low return rates. Dorman's two largest customers together are ~40% of net sales (Dorman FY2025 10-K).
3. **Federal self-certification.** This covers safety-regulated items: FMVSS 108 lighting, FMVSS 111 mirrors, brake hose FMVSS 106, glazing FMVSS 205. The manufacturer self-certifies and NHTSA only enforces after the fact (recalled). The EU is the exception: ECE R90 for replacement brake pads and discs requires type approval with comparative testing against OE (recalled).

So the "permission" here is mostly **insurer and buyer trust plus OEM IP clearance** (design patents, tooling), not regulator approval. That is a very different shape from FAA PMA or DLA SAR.

## 2. CAPA (Certified Automotive Parts Association)
- Founded 1987 by insurers (recalled). It is operated and administered by **Intertek**: RDN 2019 names Intertek as "CAPA manager" (https://www.repairerdrivennews.com/2019/09/26/companys-3d-printed-replacement-headlight-tab-kits-certified-by-capa/).
- **Method: comparative testing against the OEM service part.** CAPA does a full measurement and characterization of the OEM part as the benchmark, then runs the aftermarket part through the same tests (bodyshopbusiness).
  - Steps: material composition (FTIR/DSC-type ID, recalled), mechanical properties, coating/corrosion, dimensional checks, and **Vehicle Test Fit (VTF)**, i.e. physical gap/flush/fit on a real vehicle. CAPA reports >10,000 VTFs since 1999 (https://www.bodyshopbusiness.com/?p=126114).
  - Manufacturing-plant audits, then continuing lot/random sampling and marketplace monitoring. Material is retested at random through the life of the part.
  - Certified parts must follow any OEM material change. This is the Ford 2011 polystyrene-vs-polypropylene isolator dispute (https://www.bodyshopbusiness.com/capa-says-ford-abpa-debate-demonstrates-importance-of-part-certification/).
- **CAPA 501 bumper standard** (published 3 Nov 2010) requires full-part dynamic or quasi-static stress testing for every part number. The validation study IIHS ran: two 2008 Ram 1500s, one with OEM and one with CAPA-501 parts, in a 40 mph offset frontal test (both rated "Good") and a 5 mph bumper test (repair cost $1,120 OEM vs $1,053 aftermarket). Source: https://www.bodyshopbusiness.com/capa-releases-new-bumper-standard-enlists-iihs-for-crash-tests/
- Other programs:
  - Wheels standard (Dec 2020): https://www.repairerdrivennews.com/2020/12/04/capa-creates-standard-for-certified-aftermarket-wheels/
  - **"Verification" (not certification)** of novel non-OE-copy products, starting with the CRS 3D-printed headlight tab kits in 2019. It uses material plus vibration testing, VTF and process audit, and is design validation rather than comparison to OE.
  - **Tier 1 Verification Program** for OE suppliers selling their own-brand version. Hella was first (June 2022). Eligibility requires the supplier to be "contractually allowed to produce an in-house branded version." Stellantis/FCA (2019) and DENSO said OEM tooling and IP can't be used. Source: https://www.repairerdrivennews.com/2022/06/10/hella-becomes-first-supplier-to-join-capas-tier-1-verification-program/
- Scale: CAPA has announced a "100 Million Certified Parts" milestone (link title only). The certified share of aftermarket crash parts is low; most aftermarket sheet metal and plastic sold is uncertified (opinion/recalled).
- Cost/time per part number: not found. Recalled order of magnitude is low tens of $k, weeks to months, dominated by sourcing OE samples, the VTF vehicle, and lab queue. Treat it as unverified.

## 3. NSF automotive (exited)
NSF International ended its Automotive Parts Certification program, distributor/recycler certification, and mirror and camera registration effective **30 Sep 2019**. The reason given was strategic focus. Parts made before that date stay certified for life. Sources: https://www.fenderbender.com/news/article/55045855/nsf-international-to-discontinue-automotive-parts-certification-programs and https://nsf.org/news/nsf-international-discontinues-automotive-parts-certification-programs
**Implication:** a third-party OE-equivalence certifier could not make the economics work even with insurer demand. CAPA is effectively the only certifier left in the US. Evidence that the verification market is thin, low-margin, and lab-labor-bound.

## 4. Dorman (the closest automotive analog to an "Approval Factory", minus the approval)
FY2025 10-K: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/868780/000086878026000014/dorm-20251231.htm
- ~144,000 distinct SKUs (up from ~138k in 2024).
- 5,560 parts introduced in 2025: **1,608 "new-to-the-aftermarket"** (1,659 in 2024, 1,791 in 2023) and 3,952 line extensions.
- 256 Quality & Engineering staff (161 US, 95 non-US) and 233 Product Development staff.
- **Throughput:** roughly 1,600 new-to-aftermarket designs per ~490 engineering/PD people per year, about 3+ new designs per head-year. Compare HEICO's ~400-550 PMAs/yr (FAA). Dorman's per-part cost is far lower because there is no regulator review. Equivalence is proven internally (bench test, fit, durability) and backed by a limited lifetime warranty plus returns accruals.
- Product types: "alternative," "upgraded / OE FIX" (redesign that fixes the OE failure mode), and new parts.
- Manufacturing: mostly third-party, on Dorman-owned tooling.
- AI: being incorporated into systems/ops. The 10-K says failing to adopt AI in product development could hurt competitiveness, i.e. Dorman sees AI-for-design as a risk/opportunity but discloses no product.
- Named risks: OEM IP claims; OEM restrictions on "testing equipment, software, telematics, and repair data". 132 patents.
- Competitors: SMP, Tenneco, TrakMotive, Bosch, Gates, Continental, MevoTech, ACDelco, Motorcraft, Cummins, WARN (LKQ).

## 5. LKQ / Keystone (recalled, not fetched)
- LKQ has ~$14B revenue (recalled). Keystone Automotive is the largest US distributor of aftermarket collision parts, and LKQ buys a large share of CAPA-certified parts. It also sells recycled OEM parts, which need no equivalence proof because they are genuine OEM.
- **LKQ v. GM** (Fed. Cir. en banc, May 2024) overturned the Rosen-Durling test for design-patent obviousness. This makes it easier to invalidate OEM design patents on body panels, lamps and grilles. LKQ is the main litigant against OEM design patents. Design patents (14-15 yr term) and OEM repair-clause bills (PARTS Act, SAFE Repair Act) are the real gate on collision parts, not testing.
- In heavy equipment, the analogs are "will-fit" undercarriage, GET and hydraulics (ITR, Berco, Hensley/Komatsu, Blumaq, etc.). Validation is by the vendor alone; there is no third-party certifier. OEMs (Cat, Deere) compete with their own "reman" and second lines, and dealers pair warranty/telematics to OEM parts (recalled).

## 6. The equivalence pathway (automotive)
| Step | Who | Time/cost | Undone or broken |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opportunity ID (VIO × failure rate × OE price) | Dorman/SMP analysts, catalog data (ACES/PIES, Auto Care) | cheap | Done reasonably; mainly done by humans |
| Acquire OE sample + fitment data | engineer buys from dealer | $10s-$1000s | OE spec, tolerance and material data are not available; every company reverse-engineers the same part separately |
| Reverse engineer (scan, CMM, material ID) | in-house / Asian suppliers | days-weeks | Duplicated across 10+ suppliers per SKU |
| Design & tooling | supplier | $10k-$500k tooling (recalled) | Tooling is the dominant cost, not engineering |
| Bench/durability/vibration/fit validation | in-house; CAPA/Intertek lab for collision | weeks-months | No shared test standard outside CAPA's crash parts. Mechanical/electrical parts have none |
| Third-party certification | CAPA only (NSF exited 2019) | per part number | Covers only collision parts, and only a minority of the parts sold |
| Production conformity (lot sampling) | CAPA/Intertek, buyer QA | ongoing | Sparse; material swaps go undetected (the Ford Fusion energy absorber case) |
| Field monitoring | warranty returns, insurer complaints | lagging | No shared field-failure database. Returns data is private to each distributor |
| Electronics/software pairing | OEM | blocked | Increasingly the binding constraint (ADAS sensors, keyed modules); no equivalence path at all |

## 7. AI and players on the verification side
- **CCC Intelligent Solutions / Mitchell (Enlyte) / Solera (Audatex):** AI estimating and part-type selection (OEM vs aftermarket vs recycled) on insurer estimates. They decide when a part is used but don't verify it.
- **Tractable:** photo-based damage appraisal, ~$185M raised (recalled).
- **Partly** (NZ, parts catalog/fitment data platform, raised ~ $50M+ recalled) and **PartsTech:** fitment data and ordering, not equivalence.
- **OEM side:** OEM position statements (Honda, Toyota, FCA) say non-OE parts are not to be used in ADAS zones. OEM calibration repair procedures (I-CAR RTS) act as a de facto anti-equivalence gate.
- **CAPA/Intertek:** no AI disclosed.
- **3D scanning / metrology** (Creaform, Zeiss, Hexagon) and **simulation** (Ansys, Altair) are general-purpose tools, not equivalence services.
- No company found doing **AI-generated equivalence evidence packages** or **automated OE-equivalence certification** for auto or heavy equipment. (Search was limited, so absence is weak evidence.)

## 8. What would need to be done (assuming compute is plentiful)
1. **A shared "digital OE twin" registry.** Scan, CT, material-fingerprint and fit-test every OE service part once, then sell equivalence-reference access to all aftermarket makers and certifiers. Today the same part is reverse-engineered N times.
2. **AI-generated certification dossiers plus automated comparative testing cells.** Robot CMM/CT, FTIR/DSC/XRF material ID, and VTF via a digital vehicle-body scan library instead of a physical vehicle. Goal: cut CAPA-style per-part cost about 10x and extend certification beyond collision parts to the mechanical parts nobody certifies.
3. **Production conformity by spectroscopy and vision at distribution centers.** Spot-check every inbound lot for material swaps; insurers and distributors pay for it. This addresses the failure mode CAPA can only sample.
4. **A pooled field-failure / warranty-return database** across distributors and insurers. That turns post-market surveillance into a data product and creates the "evidence" that insurers would accept.
5. **Physics-based equivalence for crash/ADAS-zone parts by validated simulation.** Calibrate it against a small number of IIHS-style physical tests so that one physical test covers many part numbers. This goes after the OEM "not for ADAS zones" positions, which are the real blocker as vehicles get sensors.
6. **Design-patent clearance automation:** prior-art and obviousness analysis after LKQ v. GM. The gate for collision parts is legal, not technical.
7. **Electronics pairing:** the binding constraint is law (Section 1201 DMCA exemptions, state right-to-repair, the EU Data Act / Block Exemption), not engineering.

## 9. Relevance to the Approval Factory
- **Opinion:** automotive shows what happens when no regulator sits in the middle. Equivalence proof becomes cheap and in-house (Dorman: ~3 new designs per engineer-year), certification is a thin market (NSF quit, CAPA is a near-monopoly), and the moat moves to tooling, distribution, catalog data and OEM IP.
- So an AI verification play in automotive is a low-price, insurer-paid service. The value of AI verification is higher where a regulator forces it (FAA/DLA).
- **Transferable pieces** to aerospace: the reference-twin registry, automated comparative test cells, and lot-level conformity checks. Dorman's throughput benchmark is the ceiling an AI-native PMA house should aim at once reviewer hours are taken out.
