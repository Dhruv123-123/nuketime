# s10: Return-to-service sign-off and installation records (8130-3, EASA Form 1, Form 337, logbooks). Where verification fails (AOG Technics) and what would fix it.

Notes from s10 (Oct 20 2026). Marked as EVIDENCE (I fetched it), RECALLED (from memory, not re-checked this run), or OPINION.

## 1. The step itself (what the paper does)

- **Authorized Release Certificate (ARC).** FAA Form 8130-3 in the US, EASA Form 1 in the EU. It travels with a new, overhauled or repaired part and states that the part conforms to approved data and is airworthy. Only three kinds of organisation may issue one: a Part 145 repair station, a Part 121/135 carrier with an approved continuous airworthiness program, or a production approval holder (PAH). Once a part has an ARC it "can be traded freely on the marketplace" (ASCIC report p.13 and p.27, EVIDENCE). So the ARC is the bearer instrument of airworthiness. Whoever holds the paper holds the permission.
- **Installation records.** 14 CFR 43.9 covers maintenance entries in logbooks (work done, date, name, signature, certificate number). 43.11 covers inspection entries. 91.417 says what the owner must keep. FAA Form 337 records a major repair or alteration. It cites approved data, for example a DER-approved repair or an STC, and a copy goes to the FAA Aircraft Registry (RECALLED: within 48 hours, 4 copies historically). The Registry stores it as scanned paper, so it cannot be cross-checked by machine.
- **Military parallel (RECALLED).** DD Form 1574 and 1577 condition tags, plus AFTO/IMDS/REMIS/GCSS records. Counterfeit electronic parts fall under DFARS 252.246-7007/7008, with GIDEP for alerts. Since about 2012 DLA has required DNA marking (Applied DNA Sciences) on FSC 5962 microcircuits. That is the one place where a regulator forced a physical-to-document binding.
- **The receiving inspector** is described as "the last line of defense" (ASCIC Rec #10, EVIDENCE). In practice the check is a person comparing a paper certificate with a box.

## 2. AOG Technics: how verification failed (EVIDENCE)

Sources: ASCIC Final Report, Oct 2024 (aviationsuppliers.org PDF), and SFO sentencing coverage (securitymattersmagazine.com, Feb 23 2026).

- AOG Technics was founded in 2015 by Jose Alejandro Zamora Yrala in London. It claimed warehouses in the UK, Miami, Singapore and Frankfurt.
- **It was accredited.** Transonic Aviation Consultants certified it as meeting the voluntary distributor standard (FAA AC 00-56B). The FAA removed Transonic from the accepted accreditor list in April 2024, after the fact. Accreditation checked that procedures existed. It did not check that individual documents were real.
- **Method.** Zamora altered genuine ARCs on a home computer. He forged shipment memos to suggest parts came direct from OEMs such as Safran, and he forged signatures of real Safran employees and of former employees. He also invented staff, with fake LinkedIn profiles and stock photos. Forged records date back to 2018.
- **Sales spiked in late 2019**, just after the forgery began. That is a detectable signal: an anomaly in a broker's volume and mix.
- **Scale.** Over 60,000 parts sold between Jan 2019 and Jul 2023, worth £6.9M, mostly CFM56. About 90% of £7.7M revenue was fraudulent. The SFO put the fraud at £39.3M. Fewer than 1% of in-service CFM engines were affected, but aircraft were grounded worldwide (American and Ethiopian are named). He pleaded guilty to fraudulent trading in Dec 2025 and was sentenced to 4 years 8 months at Southwark Crown Court in Feb 2026.
- **Detection was by chance.** In June 2023 a technician at TAP Air Portugal noticed a CFM56 damper that looked worn but was papered as new. Safran checked its own records and found the certificate was fake. EASA and the UK CAA issued SUP notices on Aug 4 2023, and the FAA followed on Sep 21 2023 (Notification 2023-AAE-EHL-20230801-7133). Bushings for GE CF6 engines and turbine blades were also involved.
- **The tell-tales were machine-checkable.** "Fairly accurate depictions of an authorized signature, while other falsified features of the document were disparate — a purchase order number that did not conform to the OEM's practice, or a part number that conflicted with the part" (ASCIC p.27). None of this was cross-checked against the issuer's own records.
- **The response was also slow because of paper.** Regulators could not see the full scope of AOG's sales, so GE and Safran had to go to a UK court to get AOG's records. Respondents said reliance on paper made the response "more difficult and onerous". Many firms only started OCR-scanning their records after AOG.
- **Non-serialized parts** (dampers, bushings) have no back-to-birth trail at all. Back-to-birth tracing is in practice limited to life-limited parts.

## 3. What exists today (players)

- **Digital 8130-3.** The FAA has allowed electronic 8130-3 since 2009 under Order 8130.21 (EVIDENCE, ASCIC p.13), but most ARCs are still paper. Where they are digital they are mostly PDFs, which forge as easily as paper.
  - Boeing, Southwest and Aeroxchange (eARC) shipped the first digitally signed 8130-3 in Oct 2025: a battery repaired in Davie FL and verified in Dallas. It used X.509 and public-key signatures and is "blockchain-ready" (EVIDENCE, Boeing press release Oct 13 2025).
  - **Bottleneck:** each of Boeing's 9 repair centers needs its own FAA authorization for electronic recordkeeping, signatures and manuals (EVIDENCE). Approval is per site, not per platform.
  - The industry standard for signed digital ARCs is ATA Spec 2000 Chapter 16 (RECALLED).
- **GE Aerospace** began digitizing over 18M MRO records back to 2015 in 2023. It now uses AI to check that ARC fields are valid and match other records, and planned a digital-signature pilot from late 2024. It also keeps a "source-of-truth" database of new-make GE/CFM material, so its shops can check new-make claims before buying (EVIDENCE, ASCIC case study p.30). This is the closest thing to the answer, but it covers only GE's own material and is not available to the rest of the industry.
- **ASCIC** (co-chaired by Sumwalt and Porcari; members include Boeing, Airbus, Delta, United, American, GE, Safran and StandardAero) made 13 unanimous voluntary recommendations in Oct 2024 (EVIDENCE):
  1. Use AC 00-56B / EASA-accredited distributors.
  2. Feedback loop from installers to accreditors.
  3. Oversight body over the accreditors.
  4. Public database of accredited vendors.
  5. Expand digital ARCs, digital signatures, hashing and encryption.
  6. Standard for the information documents must carry.
  7. OCR-digitize past documents.
  8. **Industry-wide software and APIs that query OEM, carrier and PAH databases to validate each ARC field in real time (part number, serial, tracking number, issue date, "the signatory is an authorized individual"), with an audit trail, possibly third-party hosted.**
  9. Voluntary back-to-birth database of digitized ARCs, including non-serialized parts.
  10. Training.
  11. Audit of scrap and recycling vendors (inferred from the findings list).
  12. Faster SUP reporting coordination between industry and regulators, using ICAO Annex 13 as a model.
  13. New technology for non-serialized parts: digital unique identifiers, digital twins, blockchain.
  - The regulator section is one paragraph: "study alternatives for additional supplier accreditation standards." Recs 8, 9 and 13 have no named owner.
- **Logbook and records software:**
  - Bluetail: AI digital logbooks with plain-English Q&A over source documents. It claims 30M records and $10B in asset value, cites AC 120-78B and SOC 2 Type 1, and states no FAA approval (EVIDENCE, site).
  - Others (RECALLED): CAMP Systems (Hearst, dominant in bizjet tracking), Veryon, Traxxall, flydocs, TrustFlight (UK eTechLog), and the large MRO systems AMOS, TRAX, Ramco, IFS/Ultramain and Lufthansa Technik AVIATAR.
- **Parts marketplaces and provenance (RECALLED):**
  - Honeywell GoDirect Trade (blockchain-tracked marketplace).
  - Moog VeriPart (blockchain, additive parts).
  - Aerotrax (blockchain for Spec 2000 records; its site would not resolve today).
  - Rotabull and ePlaneAI (AI quoting and document extraction for parts sales).
  - PwC and airline blockchain pilots, and EASA's VIRTUA project (named in ASCIC p.35, EVIDENCE).
- **Physical authentication (RECALLED):**
  - Applied DNA Sciences (DLA microcircuits).
  - Alitheon FeaturePrint (optical surface fingerprinting).
  - Laser or dot-peen 2D marking per AS9132 / Spec 2000 Ch 9.
- **What nobody does (OPINION, drawing on the evidence above):**
  - No one offers a neutral, cross-OEM verifier for "is this ARC real, was this signer authorized on this date, and does this physical part match its paper".
  - No public registry exists of individual authorized signatories per Part 145 station. Rosters sit inside each station's Repair Station Manual.
  - No one checks every Form 337 against the approved data it cites.
  - No one checks images for wear-state against the claimed condition (new, overhauled, as-removed).
  - No one does graph or anomaly analytics on broker flows. AOG's late-2019 spike went unseen.

## 4. Binding constraints and what it would take to change each

- **Law and regulator acceptance.**
  - Electronic signatures are permitted (AC 120-78B, Order 8130.21), but each certificate holder needs its own acceptance, as Boeing did for each of 9 centers.
  - Fix: a platform-level FAA/EASA acceptance. The platform would hold the process approval once, and subscribing repair stations would inherit it by reference, in the way a software vendor gets an approved process referenced in many operators' manuals.
  - Path: Rec 6 standard, then an FAA policy statement, then an AC revision. The verifier never signs a release, so it avoids the certificate-holder burden entirely.
- **Liability.**
  - The installer (A&P/IA or Part 145) stays responsible under 43.13 and 91.403. A verifier that says "verified" takes on implied liability.
  - Fix: sell a risk score plus evidence pack, not a guarantee. Later, underwrite a "verified provenance" warranty with aviation insurers, priced on actual SUP loss data. The SFO's £39.3M figure and the cost of fleet-wide groundings set the size of the risk.
- **Data access** is the real moat.
  - Issuer-side truth sits in OEM and PAH ERPs (GE has it; most others have not built it). Signer rosters sit in each station's manual. 337s sit at the FAA Registry as image scans. Defense records sit in REMIS/GCSS.
  - Fix:
    - Join in two ways: as a neutral, antitrust-safe custodian (which ASCIC asks for explicitly), and as the default verifier on the Approval Factory's own parts. Parts you make yourself are born digital and signed.
    - OCR and AI over the FAA Registry's 337 archive (obtainable through FOIA or bulk request) and the public Air Agency certificate data.
    - Ask the FAA to publish individual signatory authorization as a machine-readable registry.
- **Physics clock.** There are no tests to run. Verification here is about information, plus a fast image or 3D scan at receiving inspection. This is the least physics-bound step in the whole pathway (OPINION).

## 5. AI play on the verification side (OPINION)

**"Provenance verifier of record."** An independent verifier that, for every ARC, 337 or logbook entry:
- (a) extracts every field, OCR included, and checks it against the issuer's API or reconstructed issuer conventions: PO formats, tracking-number sequences, signature biometrics, form revision;
- (b) confirms the signer existed, was employed by the issuer and held authority on that date, using public certificate data, OSINT, and the fake-LinkedIn pattern seen in AOG;
- (c) checks the physical part from receiving-inspection photos or a 3D scan, comparing wear state with the claimed condition and part-number geometry with the drawing (the TAP technician's catch, automated);
- (d) runs graph anomaly detection over trade flows and broker volumes;
- (e) for 337s, confirms the cited approved data (DER 8110-3, STC, PMA) exists and actually covers that aircraft and serial number.

**Fit with the Approval Factory:**
- The reverse-engineering house already builds the deepest geometry and material models for each part. That same model becomes the physical verifier for that part.
- Each new PMA, DLA-approved or DER part ships with a born-digital, signed ARC (Spec 2000 Ch 16) plus a surface fingerprint. "Verifiable by default" becomes a reason for buyers to choose the factory's parts over the OEM's.
- On the defense side, DLA already mandates physical marking for microcircuits. Extending that to the reverse-engineered mechanical parts the factory supplies to DLA, and offering it as a counterfeit-prevention service under DFARS 7007/7008, is a natural wedge.

**Business shape:**
- SaaS per verification at receiving inspection, sold to MROs, airlines, lessors, and DLA / DoD depots.
- Later, data-network effects: each verification adds to the back-to-birth graph (ASCIC Rec 9).
- Later still, insurance-backed provenance.

**Competitors:**
- GE does this in-house for its own material only.
- Aeroxchange provides transport and signatures, not verification of content.
- Bluetail and CAMP organise records rather than authenticating them.
- None found does cross-issuer validation of signatories and physical parts.
