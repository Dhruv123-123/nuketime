The players with real traction (Ketryx, Basil, RegDesk) sell system-of-record and data infrastructure, not "write the 510(k)".

## 2. Cost and time structure (where the hours actually go)

- From first disclosed funding round to first 510(k) for AI software devices: median 25.7 months (n=37), mean 32.0 months. Median funding consumed: $16.0M market-comparable, or $11.0M capital-efficient. Each follow-on clearance: about $11.6M (Innolitics, May 2026). https://innolitics.com/articles/ai-samd-fundraising/
- FY26 user fees: 510(k) is $26,067 ($6,517 for small businesses). De Novo is $173,782. Fees are a small share of total cost.
- 2025 AI/ML 510(k) cohort: 295 clearances, median about 142 days from submission to clearance. 24% cleared in under 90 days, 22% took over 200 days (IntuitionLabs, citing Innolitics).
- MDUFA V goal: 95% of 510(k) decisions within 90 FDA days. Total calendar time is typically 130-160 days because the clock stops during additional-information (AI) requests (recalled).
- Key point: the paperwork is a minor share of the 25-month, $11-16M path. The rest is evidence generation (bench and verification testing, clinical and standalone performance datasets, cybersecurity, biocompatibility, electrical safety per IEC 60601), the QMS, and FDA's deficiency loop. Drafting AI attacks the cheapest slice.

## 3. FDA attitude

- On sponsor AI use: no FDA rule requires disclosing AI use in drafting a 510(k). FDA "does not regulate general-purpose drafting tools", and the sponsor is fully accountable for content (IntuitionLabs' reading of FDA and EMA statements). FDA encourages structured formats and clean data. eSTAR, mandatory for 510(k)s since Oct 1, 2023 (recalled), is the structured template.
- On its own review: FDA completed an AI-assisted scientific review pilot in May 2025 and launched Elsa agency-wide on June 2, 2025 (FDA press release). Elsa is an LLM tool in GovCloud that does not train on industry submissions. Uses: summarising adverse events, label comparison, code generation for nonclinical databases, choosing inspection targets, and "accelerat[ing] clinical protocol reviews". https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-launches-agency-wide-ai-tool-optimize-performance-american-people
- Commissioner Makary's anecdote: some tasks went from "two to three days" (or "three days") to "six minutes" or "minutes". FDA published no metrics, baselines or task definitions.
- Critical reporting (recalled): CNN (July 2025) reported FDA staff saying Elsa fabricated or misrepresented studies and needed checking. FDA said use was optional. Later in 2025 FDA announced "agentic AI" capabilities for staff (recalled).
- Morgan Lewis (June 2025): sponsors should expect AI-generated flags, contest them with traceable data, and submit well-structured files, since inconsistencies are easier for AI to spot. It flags an open question about how AI fits user-fee performance goals. https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/asprescribed/2025/06/fdas-ai-assisted-review-the-next-stage-of-regulated-product-evaluation

## 4. Does it compress review time?

Evidence: no published causal data. Applicant-side AI vendors claim weeks instead of months for preparation. That is plausible, but preparation is not the bottleneck. FDA-side Elsa claims are anecdotal. Innolitics explicitly speculated whether AI-assisted review explains the 2025 timing and called causation unproven. The 142-day median for 2025 AI device clearances is close to the historical norm, so there is no visible step change (opinion, from the numbers).

Mechanisms that DO compress the clock (structural, not AI):
- eSTAR replaced the refuse-to-accept checklist with a technical screen and cut early rejections (recalled).
- Q-Submission pre-subs settle the test plan before testing, which avoids deficiency loops.
- The 510(k) Third Party Review Program (3P510k): accredited private reviewers do the review and FDA issues a decision within about 30 days of their recommendation. It is used for only a small share of 510(k)s; the program was revamped in 2020 with 3P510k re-reviews (recalled). This is the FAA DER/ODA analog: delegated verification.
- PCCP (Predetermined Change Control Plan), with final guidance in Dec 2024 (recalled). FDA pre-approves a change protocol so future modifications need no new 510(k). This means verifying the PROCESS once, then executing changes under it. It is the most important analog for an "approval factory".

## 5. Lessons for the Approval Factory (military SAR / FAA PMA)

1. Drafting-AI alone is a commodity: many startups, fixed fees around $11k, a "C-minus" output that experts must finish. The aviation and DLA equivalent (AI that writes the SAR or PMA package) will commoditise the same way. The moat is not the document.
2. Time-to-approval is set by evidence generation plus the reviewer's deficiency loop plus queueing at the regulator. AI on the applicant side does not change the regulator's clock unless the regulator changes the procedure.
3. The levers that moved FDA time were procedural: structured submissions (eSTAR), delegated review (3P510k), and pre-agreed change protocols (PCCP). Aviation already has delegation (DER, ODA). DLA's equivalent is the engineering support activity (ESA) review of SARs, which is notoriously backlogged.
4. Verification-side gap: no one sells the regulator or delegated reviewer a tool that checks a package against its basis (requirements traceability, test-evidence sufficiency, cross-document consistency). FDA built Elsa in-house, which shows the regulator will build its own tooling. The opportunity is with delegated reviewers (3P510k orgs, FAA DERs and ODA holders, DLA ESAs and contractors) and with "pre-verified" packages. Machine-checkable evidence (test data tied to requirements, provenance, reproducible analysis) would let a reviewer sign in hours.
5. Liability stays with the sponsor and signer. AI can lower hours per approval but not take on the sign-off. So the business must own or employ the signers (DER-equivalent, ODA, or an accredited 3P org) to capture the time compression.
