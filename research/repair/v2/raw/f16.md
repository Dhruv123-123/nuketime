# f16: Physics clocks that cannot be compressed (fatigue cycles, creep, thermal aging, radiation): which qualification tests are fundamentally time-bound, and which accelerated testing methods have regulator acceptance

## 1. Bottom line

- For the Approval Factory's own work (reverse-engineering replacement parts for C-130/KC-135, then PMA), physics clocks are usually **weeks to a few months per package, not years**. The slow tests are high-cycle fatigue on coupons and parts, salt-fog corrosion, moisture conditioning of composites and elastomers, and endurance runs on engine or gearbox parts. Each specimen's clock is fixed, but a program of specimens can run **in parallel**. So the limit is how many test frames you have, how long the lab queue is, and how fast the reviewer works. Physics is not the main limit.
- Clocks that truly cannot be compressed show up when **extrapolating to long life**: creep and stress-rupture over 10^5 hours, low-dose-rate radiation (ELDRS), polymer aging where oxidation is limited by diffusion, real-time calendar or shelf aging, and full-scale airframe fatigue at 2x-3x design life. These matter for new materials, new type certificates, nuclear work and space electronics. Replacement-part approvals rarely need them because **the OEM's service history already covers the long-life part of the claim**. A copy that shows it is equivalent to that part inherits that history.
- So the verification lever is to stop proving absolute life and instead **prove equivalence to a part with a known history, using comparative accelerated tests**. Errors in the acceleration model then cancel to first order. Add Bayesian adaptive test design and in-situ damage monitoring, and the specimen count and duration drop sharply. The regulator still sees physical test data. That is the AI play on the verification side.

## 2. What the rules say (fetched)

- **14 CFR 33.87 engine endurance test** (Cornell LII, fetched): at least **150 hours**, made of a 6-hour sequence repeated 25 times. The sequence covers takeoff/idle alternation, max continuous, 2.5 h of incremental cruise, and 30 min of accelerations and decelerations, plus **100 starts** (25 after at least 2 h shutdown, 10 false starts, 10 restarts). The only flexibility: the FAA sets the run order, accessory loading can be shown on a separate rig with approved analysis, and bleed can reduce power if validity holds. This is a fixed clock of about 1-2 calendar months with teardowns. A PMA for a single engine part does not normally re-run it. Instead, the applicant supports a component life claim by test and analysis (recalled).
- **14 CFR 25.571 / LOV** (fetched): the limit of validity must be shown by "full-scale fatigue test evidence". A type certificate may be issued before the test is done, but **aircraft may not fly beyond half the cycles accumulated on the test article** until the test finishes. This is the regulator's own precedent for a "lead-the-fleet" or progressive approval: operational life is unlocked in step with test progress. That mechanism is the template for a part-level version.
- **14 CFR 33.70 engine life-limited parts** (fetched): life must be backed by "validated analysis, test, or service experience". The rule names no method. Analysis and service experience are allowed in principle. Validation is the gate.
- **14 CFR 21.303 PMA** (fetched): the applicant submits "test reports and computations", unless the design is identical to a TC design (under licence). There is no fixed test list. The FAA and DER decide what is enough, case by case.

## 3. Time-bound qualification tests, by physics

| Physics | Typical test | Calendar clock (recalled) | Accelerated method | Regulator acceptance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High-cycle fatigue (HCF) | Axial/bending S-N coupons to 10^7 cycles (ASTM E466) | 10^7 cycles at 20-30 Hz = 4-6 days per specimen; a 15-30 specimen S-N curve at several stress levels takes 2-4 months on one frame, ~1-2 weeks on 10 frames | Higher frequency (resonant frames at 100-300 Hz; ultrasonic at 20 kHz gives 10^9 cycles in ~14 h) | Servo-hydraulic and resonant frames within frequency limits that avoid heating are routine. **Ultrasonic (20 kHz) fatigue is generally not accepted for certification allowables** because of frequency and strain-rate effects in some alloys. It is used for research and gigacycle work (recalled) |
| Low-cycle fatigue (LCF), rotors | Spin-pit cyclic tests on disks; strain-controlled LCF coupons (ASTM E606) | Disk spin-pit at ~1-3 cycles/min: 20k-50k cycles takes weeks to months; coupons take days | Higher strain range (then predict with a model), truncating dwell periods | Accepted when the model is validated (33.70 "validated analysis"). Dwell-fatigue alloys (Ti-6242) cannot drop the dwell: the time-dependent mechanism is the point (recalled) |
| Spectrum/structural fatigue | Component or full-scale test to 2x-3x design service goal | Full-scale airframe tests take 2-5 years (e.g., F-35 durability tests ran several lifetimes over many years) (recalled). Component tests take weeks to months | **Omit non-damaging cycles**, truncate high loads, **load enhancement factors (LEF)** that trade extra load for fewer repeats (AC 20-107B composites, LEF ~1.15 with a life factor) (recalled) | Widely accepted: spectrum editing, LEF/life-factor (FAA AC 20-107B; Navy/USAF practice), and the 25.571 half-cycles rule |
| Creep / stress-rupture | Constant-load creep (ASTM E139) | Allowables for 10^5 h design life need tests of 10^4 h or more (14 months each) to extrapolate about 3x-10x. ASME Sec III Div 5 high-temperature nuclear allowables took decades (Alloy 617 code case approved ~2019 after a >10-year program) (recalled) | Larson-Miller / Manson-Haferd / Wilshire time-temperature parameters; raised temperature or stress | Accepted with **limits on the extrapolation ratio** (ECCC/ASME practice: about 3x the longest test) (recalled). This is the hardest physics clock. Mechanism changes, such as precipitate coarsening, are not captured by acceleration |
| Thermal aging (polymers, insulation, seals) | Oven aging, then function test | Real-time aging over years of life | **Arrhenius** acceleration (IEEE 323 / IEEE 1205; NRC 10 CFR 50.49 EQ via Reg Guide 1.89) (recalled) | Accepted by NRC, the military and the FAA. The weak point is **choice of activation energy** and diffusion-limited oxidation at high temperature, which under-predicts degradation (Sandia work, Gillen and Clough) (recalled) |
| Radiation (polymers, cable, electronics) | Total ionizing dose (MIL-STD-883 TM 1019; ASTM F1892), SEE at cyclotrons, neutron displacement | **ELDRS** in bipolar parts needs ~10 mrad(Si)/s, which takes weeks to months to reach 100 krad. Cyclotron beam time is a **capacity clock** (TAMU, LBNL 88-inch, MSU FRIB): months-long queues (recalled) | High dose rate with room-temperature and elevated-temperature anneal for CMOS (accepted in TM 1019). Bipolar parts need low rate or an accepted enhanced-rate method | CMOS acceleration accepted. Bipolar ELDRS is accepted only through specific low-rate or high-temperature-irradiation protocols. **NRC does not accept ion irradiation as a substitute for neutron irradiation of reactor materials.** Neutron dpa clocks in ATR/HFIR take years (recalled) |
| Moisture diffusion (composites, adhesives) | Conditioning to equilibrium (ASTM D5229), then hot/wet testing | Thick laminates take weeks to many months to saturate | Higher temperature/RH within the limits of the material's damage threshold, thinner travellers | Accepted with limits (CMH-17 / NCAMP) (recalled) |
| Corrosion | Salt fog ASTM B117 at 500-3,000 h (3 weeks to 4 months); cyclic SAE J2334 / GMW14872 | Weeks to months | Cyclic and electrochemical tests (EIS, potentiodynamic) | B117 is still specified in many MIL/DLA drawings despite **poor correlation to the field**. Changing the spec is a paperwork problem, not physics (recalled) |
| Electronics wear-out | HTOL 1,000 h (JESD22-A108), temperature cycling (JESD22-A104), HAST | ~6 weeks per HTOL lot | Arrhenius, Peck (humidity), Coffin-Manson / Norris-Landzberg (solder), HALT | Accepted under JEDEC/AEC-Q100 and MIL-PRF-38535 (recalled) |
| Rotorcraft drive and dynamics | Gearbox 200-h endurance bench (29.923); blade and hub fatigue | Weeks to months | Overtorque spectra | Accepted, with fixed minimums (recalled) |
| Calendar / shelf life (elastomers, energetics, batteries) | Real-time surveillance; MIL-HDBK-695 / SAE ARP5316 storage | Years | Arrhenius, with a real-time confirmation program alongside | Accepted provisionally. Shelf-life extension programs rely on real-time sampling (DoD SLEP) (recalled) |

## 4. Which clocks bind the Approval Factory

- **DLA SAR / C-130, KC-135 parts:** DLA requires a SAR to show equivalence to the OEM part, using a technical data package plus first article test (FAT) and qualification test where needed. Critical safety items (CSIs) go through engineering support activity review (e.g., Robins AFB, Tinker). Typical extra physics: dimensional and material checks (days), fatigue tests on parts or coupons where needed (weeks), and environmental, salt-fog or seal aging (weeks to months) (recalled). Reported SAR review queues of **6-18 months or more** (recalled) are much longer than the physics clocks. **The reviewer, not physics, is the bottleneck.**
- **FAA PMA via test and computation:** for non-critical parts, physics clocks take days to weeks. For critical engine LLPs (disks, shafts), PMA is rare and costly. The HEICO/Pratt and Wood precedent required extensive LCF and spin tests plus FAA scrutiny, which took years (recalled). Avoid LLPs early on.
- **Where life must be extrapolated** (creep in hot sections, polymer seals, radiation-exposed parts in space or nuclear), the copy can **lean on the OEM part's service history** by showing equivalence in microstructure, chemistry, process and properties. Equivalence is cheaper to prove than absolute life.

## 5. Accelerated methods regulators already accept (precedents to build on)

1. Arrhenius thermal aging for EQ (NRC 10 CFR 50.49; IEEE 323-2003 and its 1974 version as endorsed by RG 1.89) (recalled)
2. Load enhancement and life factors for composites (FAA AC 20-107B, 2009, building-block approach) (recalled)
3. Omitting non-damaging cycles from spectra (ASIP practice, MIL-STD-1530D) (recalled)
4. **Progressive / lead-the-fleet approval**: 25.571 "half the test cycles" (fetched); USAF ASIP individual aircraft tracking (recalled)
5. JEDEC acceleration models (Arrhenius, Peck, Coffin-Manson) for electronics (recalled)
6. CMOS TID high-dose-rate with anneal (MIL-STD-883 TM 1019) (recalled)
7. **Equivalence sampling against shared databases**: NCAMP/CMH-17 lets a new user of a qualified material run reduced equivalency testing (about 1 batch instead of 3-5) (recalled). This is the closest existing analogue to "inherit the qualification, prove equivalence".
8. Analysis in lieu of test by similarity and service experience (33.70 wording; 21.303 "computations") (fetched)
9. Larson-Miller creep extrapolation within about 3x the longest test duration (recalled)

**Not accepted or contested:** ultrasonic (20 kHz) fatigue allowables; ion-irradiation surrogates for neutron damage; ML-only fatigue life predictions; pure simulation for critical LLPs; high-dose-rate shortcuts for bipolar ELDRS (recalled).

## 6. What it would take to change each constraint

- **Physics clock per specimen:** this cannot change. You can change (a) how many specimens run in parallel, (b) how many specimens are needed, and (c) when a test can stop.
  - (a) A robotic, 24/7 test farm with hundreds of cheap electrodynamic or resonant frames. The marginal frame costs about $20-150k (recalled), so the capital for 200 frames is about $10-30M.
  - (b) Bayesian adaptive design (staircase, D-optimal stress-level choice, censored run-out handling) that hits the B-basis / A-basis tolerance bound with fewer specimens. Statistically rigorous; the regulator has to accept the method rather than the count.
  - (c) In-situ monitoring (acoustic emission, DIC, potential drop, thermography) that detects crack initiation, so tests can stop early, which only helps if the agreed criterion is crack initiation rather than run-out.
- **Acceleration-model risk:** use **comparative accelerated testing**. Run the OEM part and the copy side by side in the same rig at the same elevated stress, temperature or dose. The claim becomes "copy ≥ OEM under identical acceleration", so errors in the activation energy or frequency effect largely cancel. Regulators already accept equivalence logic (SAR, NCAMP equivalency). Formalising it per failure mode is new.
- **Regulator acceptance of new acceleration (ultrasonic fatigue, ion irradiation):** build a published cross-validation dossier for each alloy family (the same material tested at conventional and accelerated rates, mechanism comparison by fractography), then take it to ASTM/MMPDS/CMH-17 committees. This takes years and works through consensus-standards bodies rather than the FAA directly.
- **Long-life extrapolation (creep, calendar):** progressive approval. Grant life in blocks as fleet-leader parts or a parallel real-time test accumulate, mirroring 25.571. Life unlocks over time while parts ship. This needs a written FAA issue paper / DLA policy, but the precedent exists.
- **Liability:** a test-backed equivalence claim with full raw data is more defensible than analysis alone. Insurers and DERs prefer it.
- **Data access:** OEM service-history data is held by the OEM and the services (e.g., REMIS/IMDS for USAF, recalled). The DLA/USAF ASIP office holds fatigue test data for the C-130 and KC-135 (SwRI and Lockheed did much of it, recalled). FOIA, CRADAs or a contract with the ASIP office are the paths in.

## 8. AI plays on the verification side (opinion)

1. **Equivalence-first test planner**: generate the test plan and protocol that proves the copy is equivalent to the OEM part for each failure mode. Comparative and accelerated, with the statistical basis pre-argued in the regulator's own vocabulary (MIL-STD-1530, AC 20-107B, MMPDS, CMH-17 equivalency).
2. **Autonomous parallel test farm**: robotic specimen prep and loading, many cheap frames, 24/7 operation, auto-generated test reports in the DLA/DER format. Sell capacity, or use it internally to shrink the Approval Factory's cycle time.
3. **Adaptive Bayesian test design plus early stopping with in-situ monitoring**: fewer specimens and shorter runs for the same tolerance bound.
4. **Acceleration-validity dossiers**: a public evidence library on when frequency, temperature or dose-rate acceleration preserves mechanism, by alloy or polymer family. Feed it into ASTM/MMPDS/CMH-17 to move what regulators accept.
5. **Progressive-approval packages**: life-in-blocks approvals backed by fleet-leader tracking and parallel real-time tests, plus the monitoring software to run them.
6. **Reviewer-side tooling**: the long queue is in the SAR/DER review. Pre-checking packages against ESA/DLA criteria and producing a traceability matrix from requirement to test to evidence cuts reviewer hours, the KPI the earlier review named.
