# a02: UL's history (1894-today): how a private lab became the de facto gate for electrical safety, its business model, and lessons for a private hardware verification authority

## 1. History: how a private lab became the gate

### The mechanism, step by step (my analysis)
1. **The party bearing the risk wanted a gate.** Fire insurers were paying for electrical fires and needed a cheap way to price risk. The customer for the gate (insurers) was not the party being gated (manufacturers).
2. **The gatekeeper wrote the rulebook.** UL wrote its own standards, which became ANSI standards, and those were incorporated by reference into the NEC and other model codes. State and local adoption then turned them into law. The 10-K says demand is "driven primarily by state and local governments as enforced through the use of model codes".
3. **Spread-out enforcers took the shortcut.** Thousands of AHJs (local electrical inspectors) cannot evaluate products, so they accept a listing mark. The mark saves the reviewer work. This is the same economics as "reviewer hours per approval".
4. **Retailers made it a private mandate.** "Many large retailers currently require that the products they sell be third-party certified" (10-K).
5. **Federal recognition came last and was opened to competitors** (NRTL, 1988). Even so, UL's brand and installed base kept its share. Recognition by name is fragile. Recognition by category plus a brand lasts.
6. **About 118 years as a nonprofit** gave it the neutrality and legitimacy to sit on code panels. It turned for-profit only after the gate was locked in. Even now the nonprofit controls 94% of the votes, which keeps the "independent" look.
7. **Liability is kept off the gatekeeper.** Courts have mostly refused to make certifiers insurers of the products they certify (recalled; Hempstead v. General Fire Extinguisher, D. Del. 1967, is an early case that allowed a negligence claim; as I recall, most later cases favored certifiers). The certifier sells confidence without carrying product liability. The 10-K still lists "Misunderstandings regarding our role in our customers' compliance processes" as a risk.

## 2. Business model (FY2025 10-K, evidence)
- Revenue **$3,053M** (2024: $2,870M; 2023: $2,68xM). Net income $345M (11.3%). **Adjusted EBITDA $792M, a 25.9% margin** (22.9% in 2024, 21.0% in 2023). Operating cash flow $600M.
- Revenue by service type: **Certification Testing about 28%**, **Ongoing Certification Services about 33%**, Non-certification Testing and Other about 30%, Software about 9%.
- Ongoing Certification is the core of the model. "To maintain the right to use our certification marks... customers must meet certain certification program requirements, including mandatory inspection and monitoring by us", including "factory inspection and testing to confirm products that are being produced match the configuration of products that were tested and certified." Physical UL Mark labels are sold as a separate performance obligation, and "the sale of labels bearing the UL Mark is material to our certification business" (one US label supplier).
- Segments 2025: Industrial $1,341M (28.8% segment operating margin); Consumer $1,319M (about 9.2% margin, $122M); Software and Advisory $393M (3.6% margin). The gate business (Industrial: codes, AHJs, wire, cable, energy) earns about 3x the margin of the consumer and retail testing business. **Code-mandated gates earn the margin.**
- Scale: more than 80,000 customers, over 110 countries, 14,587 full-time employees, about 9,900 technical staff, more than 650 accreditations, active on more than 1,200 standards panels, certifies against more than 4,000 standards. Average technical tenure is 11 years. About 65% of global and strategic accounts also buy software or advisory.
- Majority fixed-price contracts, which carries cost-overrun risk.
- Organic growth about 7% (Industrial +7.1% organic). This is a slow-growth franchise valued for durability.

### Risks UL itself names (10-K, evidence), which also map where it could be disrupted
- **AI:** "If any of our customers, competitors or new market entrants develop algorithms or other AI tools capable of replicating or better competing against our services, our services and solutions could, over time, become obsolete or unnecessary... particularly if any such AI alternative proved to be more accurate, more efficient or more cost-[effective]." It also says automation "enable[s] our customers to reduce or bypass the use of our services."
- **Accepting third-party data:** "any requirement that the Company accept third-party test results or certifications in lieu of collecting its own data and conducting its own tests ... could lead to the reduction in demand for, or commoditization of" its services. UL's moat partly depends on doing the tests itself, in its own labs.
- **Self-declaration:** risk if regulators or large retailers accept supplier self-declaration of conformity. The 10-K cites the end of the Chevron doctrine as a possible regulatory change.
- **AI data-center energy demand** is named as a growth driver: new grid and energy storage products that "must be evaluated".

## 3. Lessons for a private hardware verification authority (the "Approval Factory" verification side)

Opinion and analysis below, grounded in the facts above.

1. **Find the party that bears the risk and lacks reviewers.** For UL that was fire insurers and AHJs. For legacy military parts it is DLA and the service Engineering Support Activities (ESAs), which are backed up on Source Approval Requests (SARs), plus fleet operators facing obsolescence. For FAA PMA it is FAA ACO engineers, with DER/ODA designees already acting as delegated private reviewers. The gate pays when it saves the official reviewer hours. Today a reviewer has to re-derive equivalence from scratch for every package.
2. **Write the standard before you sell the test.** UL's lasting asset is ULSE's standards inside the codes, not its labs. The aerospace and defense analogue would be a published, consensus method (SAE G-/AS/ARP-type document, or a DoD MIL-STD/handbook annex) for proving that a reverse-engineered part is equivalent: what scan, material, and test evidence is enough for each criticality class. Today the evidence rules for SARs are case-by-case in each ESA's guidance, and DER judgment for PMA "test and computation" is case-by-case. A method that regulators reference becomes the gate. UL did this for new fields too: UL 9540A (battery thermal runaway) and UL 4600 (autonomy safety case, 2020) (recalled).
3. **Recurring revenue comes from surveillance, not first approval.** 33% of UL's revenue is follow-up on parts already certified. The Approval Factory analogue is continuous conformity: production-lot verification, field-return monitoring, and re-validation when a supplier changes a process. This is the part nobody does well in alternate-parts approval today. Once a SAR is approved, DLA largely relies on first-article tests plus quality escapes found later.
4. **Recognition by name fails; recognition by category plus a brand lasts.** OSHA was forced to open UL's named status into 21 NRTLs, and UL still leads. For a startup, aim for a recognized category the regulator already has: an FAA ODA (Organization Designation Authorization) for PMA and major repairs, an EASA Part 21J/21G-style approval, or an ISO/IEC 17065 / 17025-accredited conformity body that DoD can lean on. Do not aim for a bespoke exemption.
5. **Neutrality is the product.** UL spent a century as a nonprofit and still has nonprofit voting control. A company that both makes parts (Approval Factory) and verifies them has a conflict regulators will not accept as a third-party gate. Options: (a) build the verification engine as a separately governed entity (UL's 2022 three-entity split is a template), or (b) stay a supplier and sell verification tooling to the reviewers (ESAs, ACOs, DERs) so their hours per review drop. The two strategies conflict, so pick one.
6. **Physics clocks stay.** Factory follow-up and lab tests are UL's cost base (employee compensation is the largest line in each segment). The AI-native version replaces the parts that are not physics: evidence assembly, cross-referencing standards, comparing against prior approvals, and building the safety and equivalence case. It keeps the physical test only where it carries real uncertainty, using simulation and digital-twin results to prune the test matrix. UL itself flags "accept third-party test results in lieu of our own" as the commoditizing threat. That is exactly what a verification-data layer would push for.
7. **Liability stays manageable when the certifier certifies conformance to a written standard, not fitness of the product.** A private aerospace verifier should certify conformance to a published method and keep design authority with the PMA holder or DLA. That is how UL keeps product liability off itself.
8. **The gate grew with the hazard technology.** UL rose with electrification. The equivalent new waves today are battery and energy storage, grid equipment for AI data centers, robots and autonomy, and drones. Legacy airframes are a smaller, shrinking wave. A new verification authority might start where no incumbent standard exists yet (humanoid and industrial mobile robots, eVTOL components, grid-scale BESS retrofits, defense drones and loitering munitions under rapid procurement) and bring the same AI evidence engine back to legacy parts.

### What it would take to change each binding constraint
- **Physics clocks:** regulators accept validated simulation plus reduced physical tests (precedents: FAA's "analysis supported by test" and certification by analysis in DER practice; NRC/ASME code cases). That needs a published standard on simulation credibility (for example ASME V&V 40 for medical devices as the model) for alternate-part equivalence.
- **Law:** none needs changing for PMA or SAR. Both already allow "test and computation" or a technical-data-package-based SAR. What has to change is reviewer guidance, which is softer than law.
- **Regulator acceptance:** get recognized as an ODA, or get an ESA to adopt the method as its SAR evidence standard. DoD has an incentive: obsolescence plus the FY2024-26 right-to-repair and sustainment push in NDAA language (recalled).
- **Liability:** certify conformance to a written method (UL model). Carry E&O insurance. Insurers once funded UL, and aviation insurers or fleet operators could fund this.
- **Data access:** the OEM technical data package is withheld. Reverse engineering plus field-failure data (from operators and the DLA quality database) replaces it. UL's analogue is its own installed base of approvals: 80,000 customers' certified configurations is a data asset no new entrant has.

## 4. Who is already doing pieces of this (evidence vs recalled)
- **Incumbent TIC:** UL Solutions (ULTRUS software with AI "to help customers accelerate product launches, improve regulatory compliance"), Intertek, SGS, Bureau Veritas, TUV SUD, TUV Rheinland, DEKRA, CSA, Element, Eurofins, Nemko (all NRTLs per OSHA). None are known for alternate-part equivalence approval for DLA or FAA PMA. That niche is DERs, small consultancies, and the in-house engineering of PMA houses (HEICO, Wencor, now part of HEICO? (recalled: Wencor was acquired by HEICO in 2023)).
- **No "UL for alternate aircraft and military parts" exists** as far as I know (opinion). The closest are FAA ODA holders (OEMs and some PMA houses) and DER firms.

## 5. AI plays on the verification side (opinion)
1. **A reviewer copilot sold to the gatekeepers** (DLA and service ESAs, FAA ACOs, DER firms). It ingests a SAR or PMA package, checks it against requirements, cites precedent approvals, and flags gaps. KPI: reviewer hours per approval. It sells to the side with the bottleneck and keeps the company neutral.
2. **A private equivalence verification body built AI-first.** Write the "evidence standard for reverse-engineered part equivalence" with SAE or an ESA, become an accredited ISO/IEC 17065 body and/or ODA, and run certification plus ongoing lot surveillance as recurring revenue (UL's 33% model).
3. **Continuous conformity via production telemetry,** replacing UL-style periodic factory visits with in-process sensor, CT and metrology data streams and AI anomaly detection. This attacks the most recurring part of TIC.
4. **A test-matrix minimizer** that uses simulation and digital twins to cut physical tests to the minimum the evidence standard requires, so the physics clock is spent only where it reduces uncertainty.
5. **Certification for new-wave hardware where no gate exists yet** (robots, BESS retrofits, eVTOL and drone parts, data-center power gear), UL-1894 style: write the standard, then sell the mark.
