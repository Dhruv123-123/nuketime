# c29: Robotics/drone inspection-to-signoff players (ANYbotics, Square Robot, Flyability, Skydio, Percepto, Energy Robotics): do any hold signing authority or accredited inspection status?

## Short answer
No. None of the six holds signing authority in its own name. What they hold is (a) **product certifications** for the robot (ATEX/IECEx Zone 1 for ANYmal X; ISO 27001), (b) **aviation operating permission** (Percepto's nationwide US waiver plus BVLOS approvals from EASA, CASA, Transport Canada, Israel and others; Skydio's BVLOS waivers and Blue UAS listing, recalled), and (c) **data acceptance by a signer** (ABS "certified" Elios 3 UT data for use in class surveys, June 2024). The sign-off still sits with a human credential: an API 653 authorized inspector, an ASME/NBIC Authorized Inspector working for an Authorized Inspection Agency, or a classification-society surveyor. The one partial exception is **Square Robot**, which brings that signer in-house by employing or contracting certified API 653 inspectors and NDT Level IIIs who review its reports. Even there, the authority belongs to the individual's API ICP certification, not to the company or the robot.

## Structural facts (recalled, not fetched this run)
- **API 653** (aboveground tanks) and **API 510/570** (pressure vessels and piping): the signer is an individual holding API Individual Certification Program (ICP) certification, employed by the owner-user or a contractor. No accredited-company route exists for robots.
- **ASME / NBIC boilers and pressure vessels**: the signer is a National Board commissioned Inspector working for an Authorized Inspection Agency, such as HSB/Munich Re, Allianz or Zurich, or for an owner-user inspection organization. NBIC has slowly been adding remote and alternative inspection provisions.
- **Class societies**: ABS, DNV, LR and BV approve **Service Suppliers** under IACS UR Z17 for thickness measurement and remote inspection techniques (RIT). IACS Recommendation 42 and the ABS Guidance Notes on Using Remote Inspection Technologies (first published 2016, since revised) govern drone/RIT use. Approval goes to the *service supplier firm*, but the class surveyor still credits the survey. This is the nearest existing "accredited company" slot a robotics firm can occupy, and several drone service firms hold it. I found no evidence that Flyability itself holds it; it sells to such suppliers.
- **ISO/IEC 17020**: accreditation for inspection bodies. Type A/B/C bodies (TUV, Bureau Veritas, SGS, Intertek, Mistras) hold it. None of the six robotics companies appears to.
- **NRC (nuclear)**: inspections run under the licensee's 10 CFR 50 App B QA program. A robot vendor is at most a qualified supplier, and acceptance comes through EPRI/PDI performance demonstration for UT.
- Funding (recalled, approximate): Skydio about $740M total, valued around $2.2B in 2023; ANYbotics Series C about $50M+ in 2023, extended later; Gecko about $220M+ before its 2025 Series D at roughly $1.25B; Percepto about $100M+; Energy Robotics around a €13.5M Series A in 2023; Flyability around $40M+; Square Robot tens of millions.

## Interpretation (opinion)
The robotics wave automated *data capture*. It did not take over *qualification of the method* or *signature*. Every one of these companies hands the result to a human credential-holder, and the business is mostly hardware or RaaS. Margin and lock-in stay with the TICs (BV, TUV, SGS, Mistras) and with the AIAs and class societies that sign. Accreditation is not a technical barrier. ISO 17020 or Service Supplier approval costs months and an audited QMS, yet the robotics firms have not pursued it, probably because a hardware company's brand depends on not taking signature liability.

That leaves a gap: an "AI-native inspection body" that holds the accreditation itself (ISO 17020 Type A or C, class Service Supplier, API ICP-certified staff augmented by AI review). It would treat the robot fleets as interchangeable sensors and sell *signed determinations*, with the KPI being signer-hours per signed report.

What it would take to shift the binding constraint:
- **Method qualification**: probability-of-detection (POD) studies per robot and sensor, under MIL-HDBK-1823A and ASME V. These could be generated largely in simulation plus physical specimens.
- **Code cases** to API, ASME or NBIC recognizing algorithmic first-pass evaluation with human countersign. A code-committee process takes about 2-4 years.
- **Liability**: professional indemnity insurance priced on a track record of verified data.
- **Data**: inspection histories and failures, which are mostly owner-held. Partnering with an AIA or insurer such as HSB gives access to both the data and the signature.

The bridge to the Approval Factory: the same "signed determination engine" serves aviation, where the equivalents are DER/ODA and Part 145 inspection sign-off. It also serves DLA SAR verification through first-article inspection and dimensional and NDT reports. The verification evidence package, meaning the POD-qualified inspection, traceable data and a pre-drafted finding, is common across these domains.
