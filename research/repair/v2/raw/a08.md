## 1. Carfax: the pattern
Evidence (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carfax_(company)):
- Founded 1984 in Columbia, MO, by Ewin Barnett III and Robert Daniel Clark. In 1986 it sold a dealer report by fax. Website launched Dec 1996.
- Claims more than 151,000 data sources and over 35B records (30B in Dec 2022).
- Ownership: R.L. Polk 1999, then IHS 2013, IHS Markit 2016, S&P Global 2022 (part of the ~$44B IHS Markit deal), then S&P Global Mobility. Mobility Global Inc became a public company on Jul 1, 2026.
- It sells reports to consumers and data to dealers. It also does marketing and data brokerage.

How it captures value (some recalled):
- **Seeded from public records.** The first records were state DMV title and odometer data, then auctions, police and insurers. No one had to agree to join, because the state already held the data. (recalled)
- **Give-to-get from fragmented contributors.** Independent service shops and dealers send service records, usually through integrations with shop-management or DMS software. In return they get free marketing (a "Carfax-verified service shop" listing, service-reminder apps). The marginal contributor pays nothing and gets traffic. (recalled)
- **Charges at the moment of decision.** A consumer pays per report, about $45 for one report (recalled). Dealers pay subscriptions so they can show free reports as a sales tool. "Show me the Carfax" made the report an expected part of the transaction. Sellers pay so that buyers will trust them.
- **Asymmetric value.** A clean history raises a car's price and a branded title lowers it. So the report takes a share of the price gap it reveals.
- **Moves up the stack.** It added "History-Based Value" pricing and listings, going from records to valuation to marketplace. (recalled)
- **Rival and regulator context.** Experian AutoCheck is the main competitor and adds auction scoring (recalled). The federal NMVTIS title database (Anti Car Theft Act 1992, live ~2009) became the government floor. Carfax kept its premium because of service-history breadth that NMVTIS lacks. (recalled)
- **Fraud is the reason it exists.** Odometer rollback and title washing created demand. Carfax never had to make the data legally binding, only expected by buyers.

## 2. Credit bureaus: the pattern
Evidence (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_bureau):
- Commercial credit reporting began after the Panic of 1837. By the 1930s there were more than 1,000 bureaus. A computerized bureau in 1965 started automation and consolidation.
- FCRA (1970) defines "consumer reporting agencies". Furnishers (creditors, utilities, collectors, courts) report payment data, and the agencies aggregate it and sell it for credit, employment and rental decisions.
Recalled:
- **Reciprocity.** Historically only lenders that furnished data got access, and that made reporting self-reinforcing. Furnishing is still voluntary under FCRA, but a lender that does not furnish loses access and standing.
- **Standard format.** CDIA's Metro 2 format made furnishing cheap and uniform. Whoever sets the schema sets who can join.
- **Law as legitimizer, not mandate.** FCRA imposed duties (accuracy, disputes, permissible purpose). That raised the cost of entry and so locked in the incumbents. Consolidation from thousands of bureaus to 3 national ones (Equifax 1899, TransUnion 1968, Experian/TRW) followed computerization.
- **The scoring layer earns more than the data layer.** FICO (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Isaac_Corporation) was founded in 1956. 2025 revenue was ~$1.99B, operating income ~$925M (about 46% margin), and in 2013 lenders bought more than 10B scores. FICO owns no consumer data. It owns the number that regulators and the GSEs (Fannie/Freddie) name in their rules. Antitrust suits (2020-23) allege inflated prices. That is a sign of pricing power from being named in regulation (recalled: GSE mortgage-score rule; FHFA allowed VantageScore 4.0 in Jul 2025; FICO launched direct licensing at $4.95/score in 2025).
- **Equifax Work Number.** Equifax had 2024 revenue of $5.68B (Wikipedia). Employers and payroll processors send payroll data because it offloads their verification work. Lenders pay per verification. Workforce Solutions is roughly 40% of Equifax revenue at about 50% margins (recalled). A May 2024 class action alleges a monopoly in income verification (Wikipedia). **This is the closest analogue for parts:** contributors give data to get rid of a compliance chore, and the verifier pays per check.

## 3. Mapping to parts provenance
Aviation airworthiness already consists of paperwork. A part without its FAA 8130-3 / EASA Form 1 and back-to-birth trace (required for life-limited parts) is worth a fraction of its value or is scrap. (recalled: records gaps on LLPs cut value sharply, and lease-return records reconciliation is a known multi-month cost.)

The fraud event that creates demand:
- AOG Technics (London), 2023 (recalled). It forged EASA Form 1 / release certificates for CFM56 parts. CFM/Safran found the parts on more than 100 engines; reports said 96 at first. TAP, Southwest, United, Delta and Virgin Australia were affected. A UK SFO criminal case followed against director Jose Zamora Yrala. The fraud surfaced because a TAP technician queried the paperwork and the OEM's serial records did not match. **Lesson: the OEM held the authoritative serial/certificate registry but nobody queried it at purchase.** It is the parts equivalent of the DMV title database.
- Defense electronics counterfeits: NDAA FY2012 Sec. 818, DFARS 252.246-7007/7008, GIDEP reporting, SAE AS5553/AS6081 (recalled). DLA has required DNA marking of FSC 5962 microcircuits (Applied DNA Sciences) since about 2012 (recalled).

Existing players (recalled unless noted; threat to the Approval Factory in brackets):
- Records/maintenance tracking: CAMP Systems (Hearst; business-aviation maintenance tracking), Traxxall, Flydocs (Lufthansa Technik), Bluetail, AMOS (Swiss AviationSoftware), Ramco, IFS/Aerostrat, TRAX. [adjacent]
- Parts marketplaces and locators: ILS (Inventory Locator Service), PartsBase, ePlaneAI, Rotabull. [adjacent]
- Blockchain/digital trace: SkyThread (homepage fetched: "SkyThread for Parts, Planes and Engines, MRO..., Blockchain"; no customers or funding listed), Aerotrax (site unreachable), Moog VeriPart (additive manufacturing), GE/Aviation blockchain pilots, Honeywell GoDirect Trade marketplace, SITA. Most stalled on the same thing that killed IBM/Maersk TradeLens (shut 2023): no reason to contribute. [adjacent]
- Standard: ATA Spec 2000 Chapter 16 (electronic signatures/records, electronic 8130-3/Form 1) and Ch. 9 (part marking/RFID). [schema layer, nobody owns]
- Defense: GIDEP (government-run, only as good as voluntary reports), ERAI (counterfeit electronics database, private), SiliconExpert/Z2Data (component lifecycle and DMSMS risk). [adjacent]
- None of these compute a per-serial provenance confidence score that a buyer, insurer or regulator relies on at the moment of purchase or install. That FICO-style slot looks empty (opinion).

## 4. Lessons (opinion, based on the patterns above)
1. **Start from government and OEM public records, not from asking people to join.** For parts: FAA SDRs, ADs, the PMA database, the aircraft registry, DLA FLIS/WebFLIS, DIBBS award history, FPDS, GIDEP alerts, OEM service bulletins. The AI cost to read scans and PDFs is near zero.
2. **Give-to-get where you remove a chore.** The Work Number model: MROs, brokers and distributors upload trace packages because the platform then answers buyer, lessor and auditor queries for them. Records reconciliation at lease return is the chore to take over.
3. **Charge the verifier per pull at the decision moment:** a purchase order, a receiving inspection, a lease return, a DLA source-approval submission.
4. **The score earns more than the database.** Own the confidence number and get it written into a buyer's procedure (an AS9120 distributor QMS, an airline receiving-inspection procedure, a DLA requirement for a provenance check), and later into regulation (FAA AC 00-56 accreditation, DFARS).
5. **Law creates the moat after the market exists.** FCRA and NMVTIS came after adoption. An FAA/EASA push to electronic release certificates (Spec 2000 Ch.16) is the opening. Whoever runs the lookup for e-8130-3 validity becomes the NMVTIS of parts.
6. **Fraud events create urgency.** AOG Technics is the parts equivalent of odometer rollback.
7. **The fragmentation has to be real.** It is: tens of thousands of brokers, MROs and operators, and no single party holds a serial's whole life.

## 5. Link to the Approval Factory (verification side)
- The same evidence engine works for two products: (a) a provenance score for existing parts (the bureau), and (b) a conformity and equivalence evidence package for new reverse-engineered parts (the approval). Both rest on serial-level evidence such as material certs, CMM/CT scans, test results and field history.
- Field reliability history of OEM parts (removal and failure data from operators and SDRs) is evidence that supports PMA test-and-computation and DLA SAR similarity arguments. The bureau's data then feeds the factory's approvals.
- Once reverse-engineered parts are in service with full digital provenance and failure tracking, their accumulated service record lowers the bar for regulator and insurer acceptance of the next approval. This is the "credit history" for a new source: a thin-file supplier builds credit.

## 6. What would need to be done (assume abundant compute)
- Ingest and OCR every public and obtainable parts record, then resolve P/N + S/N entities across scans, 8130-3s, teardown reports and broker listings. The result is a per-serial life graph (a back-to-birth reconstruction engine).
- Forgery detection on release certificates: check signatories, repair-station certificate numbers, form versions, dates against the FAA repair-station registry, and PDF and image tampering. Also cross-check against the OEM serial registry through API deals with CFM, GE, P&W and Honeywell (the data-access constraint).
- A Spec 2000 Ch.16 e-certificate validation service: become the lookup the issuing station signs into.
- Get the score named in procedures: AS9120 distributors, airline receiving inspection, insurers' underwriting, DLA/DCMA source approval, and the FAA SUP program (AC 21-29).
- For defense: a GIDEP-plus that is proactive (scan DIBBS awardees and the broker market) rather than waiting for reports.
- Liability: take an FCRA-like stance with a dispute process, accuracy duties and E&O insurance, so buyers can rely on the score.
