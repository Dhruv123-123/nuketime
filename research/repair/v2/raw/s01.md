# s01: Step 0 of the pathway: picking which parts to approve (demand and price-spike data from DIBBS, FedMall, FLIS, ILS and airline usage). How targets are chosen today, and what data-driven targeting would look like.

Search budget used: 3 WebSearch calls. dla.mil and dodig.mil returned 403 to WebFetch, so DLA-internal pages could not be read directly; the GAO report and an NDIA/DIBBS workshop deck stood in for them. Anything marked (recalled) comes from memory and was not verified in this session.

## 1. How targets are chosen today (evidence)

### Military (DLA)
Source: GAO-19-586 (July 2019), https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-19-586 (PDF text extracted).

DLA buys and distributes about 5M distinct items and spends more than $35B a year (FY24 figure from the DIBBS workshop: over $52B, $20B of it to small businesses).

Parts become reverse-engineering (RE) candidates "generally because the government does not have necessary legal rights to the drawings or lacks data". DLA guidance treats RE as the "least attractive alternative" and expects staff to try buying data from the OEM first.

Entry criteria:
- a documented business case;
- procurements above $10,000 in each of the past 2 years;
- engineering support activity (ESA) approval of the project before work starts, and again for the resulting technical data package (TDP) or source approval request (SAR).

There are two programs:
- **RPPOB (Replenishment Parts Purchase or Borrow), contractor-funded.** Contractors pick parts "from a candidate list on a DLA website or through their own research". In practice these are mostly small hardware and electrical items such as antennas and cables. Critical and classified parts are excluded. A prior note (c-series, from a DLA deck) adds that AMSC G/T, spec-controlled and AMSC B source-controlled items are barred.
- **DLA-funded efforts.** These cover sole-source, obsolete and limited-rights parts, worked by DLA engineers, by DoD partners, or under contract.

Outcomes, FY2015-FY2018:

| | Contractor-funded | DLA-funded | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Projects | 600 | 1,033 | 1,650 |
| Completed | 55 | 86 | 141 (under 10%) |
| In process | 487 | 422 | 909 |
| Canceled | 75 | 525 | 600 |

Reported savings were at least $22M, gross: the figure excludes RE cost, and more than half of it came from RPPOB. One example is a retaining ring that came in about $70 a unit cheaper, saving about $11k in total.

Why projects failed, from the GAO report:
- the ESA was slow to respond;
- contractors underestimated the labor;
- DLA never heard back from contractors after they bought the part;
- DLA staff were pulled to other priorities;
- **"the need no longer exists for some parts that were identified as reverse engineering candidates."**

That last reason is a targeting failure: demand decayed before approval arrived.

About two-thirds of projects fell in five categories: hardware and abrasives (screws, nuts), vehicular components, electrical components (switches, antennas), wire and power distribution (harnesses, cables), and aerospace craft components (insulation blankets, filters, door handles). These are low-value, low-complexity items. Targeting today is opportunistic, not value-ranked.

Small-business tooling (DIBBS workshop deck, "First DIBBS", hosted on swapcard: https://static.swapcard.com/public/files/5f8e9cbcacb245e289ae6aef07201b83.pdf):
- The recommended process is a manual 7-step funnel: USASpending, then DLA Product and Service Search, then DIBBS RFQs by FSC, then by NSN, then RFPs, then award history by NSN, then the DLA Demand Forecast.
- **SRVA** (Supplier Requirements Visibility Application) gives up to 24 months of projected purchase-order demand by NSN. It needs a DIBBS account.
- **WebFLIS** maps each NSN to manufacturer part numbers and approved-source CAGE codes. It needs a CAC, ECA or PIV credential.
- **PUB LOG** is publicly releasable FLIS data: a monthly download, free, with no login.
- **DLA FOIA Purchase Transaction Logs** are monthly logs of actual DLA purchases, with NSN, item name, manufacturer, supplier, quantity and **price paid**. They are public, in the FLIS Electronic Reading Room.
- "15% of NSNs are actively purchased 1G items, fully open for competition." By implication, most actively bought NSNs have restricted sourcing (AMSC C/D/P/B/H and similar). That restricted set is the Approval Factory's universe.
- The manufacturer's decision tree is "access to technical documents and capability match". It does not consider value, approval probability or platform life.

Commercial DIBBS intel tools:
- **HigherGov** (https://www.highergov.com/alt/dibbs/) offers historical pricing, recurring-NSN tracking, supplier research, an API and MCP. Pricing is $500/yr individual and $2,500/yr team. It shows no sole-source or AMSC flag and no RE-candidate scoring.
- **SAMSearch** (https://docs.samsearch.co/features/contract-overview/dibbs) shows NSN award history and average and weighted unit cost.
- **Apify DIBBS RFQ scraper** (https://apify.com/automation-lab/dibbs-rfq-scraper) does RFQ monitoring.

All of these serve distributors bidding on open RFQs. None ranks orphaned NSNs for approval.

Price-spike evidence (recalled):
- DoD IG DODIG-2019-060 (TransDigm) found excess profit on 46 of 47 parts reviewed, about $16M on about $26M in purchases.
- DODIG-2021/22 found excess profit on about 105 of 107 parts, about $20.8M.

These show that sole-source price outliers are detectable from purchase history plus should-cost, and that the IG does this by hand, a few dozen parts at a time.

### Commercial (FAA PMA)
- **ADPma** (InPractise interview, https://www.inpractise.com/articles/adpma-and-pma-aerospace-parts) says you must be "mindful about which parts you choose". It ranks candidates on on-wing reliability gains (redesigning 30-50-year-old parts, e.g. from billet on 5-axis machines), lead-time pain, service difficulty reports (SDRs), airworthiness directives (ADs) and removal rates. The airline partner must approve the part, so the first sale is to the operator. PMAs are priced near OEM levels when reliability is better, so selection is not mainly about discount.
- **AeroVal** (https://aeroval.com/key-themes-actively-shaping-civil-aircraft-parts-mro-aftermarket) reports that OEM part and ICA prices are rising faster than aftermarket inflation and that some ICA licenses require OEM-only parts. It describes no targeting method.
- **HEICO** (recalled) picks targets largely from requests by airline partners, which have invested in it (Lufthansa Technik held 20% of HEICO Aerospace; American, United and Delta have had partnership arrangements). Its engineers then screen on volume and price. Selection knowledge is held by people.
- Data assets, all recalled:
  - **ILS** (Inventory Locator Service, Boeing-owned via Aviall) holds part-number availability and quote traffic.
  - **PartsBase** and **Locatory** are marketplaces.
  - **Airbus Skywise** holds fleet reliability and removal data from a large share of Airbus operators, but it is OEM-owned, a conflict for PMA targeting.
  - **IATA MRO SmartHub** handles part-history digitization.
  - Airline reliability programs (MTBUR, removal rates) are private to each airline.
  - The FAA PMA approval list is public through the DRS and PMA database.
- **ePlaneAI** (https://www.eplaneai.com) does AI for parts distributors, MROs and airlines: quote automation, inventory optimization, and an "AeroGenie" decision engine. It makes no claim to identify PMA candidates.

## 2. Gaps: what nobody does, or does badly

1. **No ranked universe of orphaned, high-value NSNs.** The pieces are public, but nobody fuses them:
   - PUB LOG gives AMSC/AMC codes, CAGE and P/N cross-references, and criticality indicators.
   - FOIA purchase logs give price paid over time.
   - DIBBS gives award history and solicitations.
   - USASpending gives contract-level spend.
   - The FAA PMA list says whether a commercial-equivalent PMA already exists.

   The DLA RPPOB candidate list is limited to small hardware. Contractors use their own ad hoc research.
2. **No model of approval probability or approval time per NSN or ESA.** DLA does not track whether cancellation happened before or after RE work (GAO). Historical SAR outcomes are not public. Nobody knows, for example, which ESA at Warner Robins or Tinker approves which part families in how many days.
3. **No decay-adjusted demand.** "Need no longer exists" kills projects. Remaining platform life (such as the C-130H and KC-135 retirement schedules), fleet drawdowns and engineering changes are not joined to NSN demand. SRVA covers only 24 months.
4. **No readiness weighting.** Backorders, MICAP (Air Force mission-capable parts), NMCS and AWP are restricted data. Ranking by dollars alone misses parts that ground aircraft.
5. **No clustering across parts.** Nobody groups NSNs by shared geometry, material, process and ESA so that one test campaign or similarity argument can qualify a whole family. That clustering is the lever for engineering-hours-per-approval, the Approval Factory's KPI.
6. **No should-cost at scale.** IG audits are manual and cover a few dozen parts. An automated should-cost from geometry, material and process (estimated from item name, FSC and P/N) would flag price-spike targets across millions of NSNs.
7. **Commercial-side data is fragmented and OEM-held.** Removal rates sit with airlines and OEM platforms (Skywise), and price lists are OEM-controlled. Except through partner airlines, PMA houses lack cross-fleet demand.

## 3. What data-driven targeting would look like (opinion, built on the evidence above)

Score each NSN or P/N:

> value = (annual buy $ x (price paid / should-cost - 1) + readiness weight x backorder or MICAP days) x expected remaining demand years x P(approval) / (expected engineering + reviewer + test cost and time)

Filters:
- AMSC/AMC code: target restricted codes that are not barred, and treat B/G/T separately;
- critical safety item (CSI) flag;
- data-rights status (DFARS 252.227-7013/7015 markings);
- existing PMA or commercial-equivalent cross-reference;
- export-control and classification.

Steps:
1. **Ingest.** Pull PUB LOG monthly, FOIA purchase logs (multi-year price series), DIBBS award and RFQ history, USASpending, SRVA (with a DIBBS account), the FAA PMA and DRS lists, GIDEP and DMSMS notices, and platform retirement schedules from budget documents (P-1/R-1, SAR/President's Budget force structure).
2. **Model.**
   - Learn P(approval) and time-to-approval from whatever history can be obtained: FOIA requests for SAR outcomes by ESA, DLA competition advocate data, and the company's own submissions over time. This becomes a proprietary flywheel.
   - Use LLMs to read item names, characteristics (FLIS MRC data) and drawings in cFolders/JCP to estimate geometry class, material, process, inspection burden and CSI risk.
   - Cluster similar parts into families.
3. **Output.** A ranked pipeline of approval portfolios ("approve 40 C-130 bracket and fitting NSNs at one ESA with one test campaign"), not single parts.
4. **Sell the same engine to the buyer.** DLA competition advocates, the Air Force Rapid Sustainment Office (RSO) and service ESAs could use it to prioritize their limited reviewer hours. That makes the regulator's queue part of the product.

Binding constraints, and what it would take to change each:
- **Data access.** WebFLIS (CAC) and MICAP/backorder data are restricted. Fix: a CRADA, an SBIR or OTA with DLA R&D or the RSO, or FOIA. The public data (PUB LOG and FOIA purchase logs) is already enough for a v0.
- **Law.** Data rights and patents are a real constraint. GAO notes that Troop Support skips patent checks on old parts, while Aviation and Land and Maritime check. Fix: automated rights screening on the DFARS markings.
- **Regulator acceptance.** ESA approval of the project is required up front. Fix: approval-probability scoring that tells the ESA "you approve 90% of this family", and portfolio submissions.
- **Physics clock.** Not binding at this step. Targeting is pure data and compute, the one step with no lab-time floor.
- **Liability.** CSI items carry it. Fix: start outside CSI, as RPPOB already does.

- **DLA itself.** The RPPOB candidate list and the competition advocate offices.

## Surprises
- Under 10% of DLA RE projects (141 of 1,650, FY15-18) completed. Some failed because the demand vanished before approval came, so targeting quality is itself a large lever.
- The $10k-per-year threshold is tiny. DLA's screen is "is it bought at all", not "is it worth it".
- Price-paid transaction logs are public (FOIA reading room), so a price-spike screen across all of DLA needs no special access.
- Only about 15% of actively bought NSNs are fully open (1G), which implies a very large restricted universe.
- Commercial PMA houses say selection is about reliability and lead time, not discount.
