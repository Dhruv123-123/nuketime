## 1. How verification became the most valuable layer in chips

### Evidence
- Synopsys closed its acquisition of Ansys on 2025-07-17 (announced 2024-01-16). Synopsys says this gives it an "expanded $31 billion" TAM (a 2023 management estimate). It also plans integrated multiphysics capabilities across the EDA stack in 1H 2026, aimed at semiconductors, automotive and aerospace. Source: https://news.synopsys.com/2025-07-17-Synopsys-Completes-Acquisition-of-Ansys
  - Deal value about $35B in cash and stock (recalled).
- The Pentium FDIV bug led to a $475M pretax charge (1994 annual report), about $891M in 2024 dollars. Afterwards the industry sharply increased its use of formal verification for floating point: word-level model checking in 1996, STE plus theorem proving on Pentium 4, and Nehalem (2008) as the first Intel design with formal as its primary validation method. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentium_FDIV_bug
- ChipAgents (agentic AI for chip design and verification) lists B Capital, Bessemer, Micron, MediaTek, Samsung and Ericsson as backers. It claims a 50% cut in design cycle time, and its blog covers autonomous root-cause analysis for regression triage. Source: https://www.chipagents.ai/

### Recalled figures (verify)
- Siemens EDA / Wilson Research Group functional verification studies (2022 and 2024): verification takes about 50 to 60%+ of ASIC project time. Peak verification headcount is at or above design headcount. First-silicon success fell to about 14% in 2024, from about 30% a decade earlier. Most respins are caused by logic or functional flaws. (recalled)
- Revenue: Synopsys FY2024 about $6.1B, Cadence 2024 about $4.6B, Siemens EDA about $2B+ (recalled).
- Simulation roll-ups into EDA: Cadence bought BETA CAE (about $1.24B, 2024). Siemens bought Altair (about $10.6B, closed March 2025). Cadence also bought Future Facilities and NUMECA (recalled).
- Advanced-node mask sets cost about $10M to $30M+ at 5/3nm, and a respin costs months of schedule (recalled).

### The mechanism (analysis, not evidence)
Verification became the largest, stickiest and most valuable layer because four things lined up.
1. **Escapes cost far more than checking.** A mask set costs $10M+, a respin costs months, and a field recall can cost $475M. Compute is cheap by comparison, so customers rationally spend more and more on proving the design is correct before committing.
2. **The spec is fully digital and executable.** RTL, netlists, GDSII and the foundry PDK are all machine-readable. Design rules ship as executable rule decks (Calibre DRC/LVS decks), and timing and power models ship as characterized Liberty (.lib) files. The rules can be checked exhaustively by a machine.
3. **The gatekeeper hands acceptance to certified tools.** TSMC, Samsung and Intel Foundry certify specific tool versions for each node, and their output counts as "golden sign-off": Calibre for DRC/LVS, PrimeTime and Tempus for STA, RedHawk (Ansys, now Synopsys) for IR/EM. The foundry accepts a clean report from a certified tool as permission to tape out. That certification is the moat. Customers buy the tool the gatekeeper already trusts. Permission flows through the tool, not through a person's signature.
4. **A correlation loop.** Tool models get correlated against measured silicon, node after node (foundry plus tool vendor). New entrants cannot copy that data asset. Better correlation lets designers cut margin, which makes the tool worth more.

Other enabling pieces include equivalence checking (Formality, Conformal: proves the netlist is logically the same as the RTL), coverage closure metrics (functional and code coverage give a number that says how much has been checked), standard methodology (UVM, SVA assertions), and regression farms (verification as continuous integration).

## 2. Mapping to physical parts (PMA / DLA SAR)

| Chip sign-off element | Physical-part analog today | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Executable PDK / DRC rule deck | Certification basis: 14 CFR Part 25/33 plus ACs, MIL-specs, OEM drawings and process specs. Prose PDFs | MISSING: nobody compiles a cert basis into executable checks |
| Liberty files (characterized cells) | Material allowables: MMPDS (metals), CMH-17 and NCAMP shared composite databases (recalled) | PARTIAL: NCAMP is the closest thing to a shared PDK. AM process allowables are thin |
| Equivalence checker (netlist = RTL) | PMA "identicality" or "test and computation" showing a replica matches the OEM part | MISSING as a product: done by hand by DERs and test labs |
| Golden sign-off tool certified by gatekeeper | FAA delegates to PEOPLE (DERs, ODA unit members), not tools. DO-330 tool qualification covers only software and airborne-electronics dev tools. NASA-STD-7009A and ASME V&V 10/20 cover model credibility but give no regulatory "pass" | MISSING: no qualified analysis tool whose output an ACO or DLA ESA accepts as a finding |
| Coverage closure | Compliance matrix and traceability (8110-3 data approvals) | MANUAL: no number says how much of the cert basis is evidenced |
| Silicon correlation loop | Coupon, component and engine test data plus service data (SDRs, teardown) | FRAGMENTED: each applicant's own data, siloed. OEMs hold the rest |
| Regression farm | Re-verification after a material or vendor change (major/minor change) | MANUAL |
| Formal proof | Nothing comparable. Physics is continuous and stochastic, so the best on offer is probabilistic bounds (UQ) | Research only |

Precedent in another regulator: FDA finalized "Assessing the Credibility of Computational Modeling and Simulation in Medical Device Submissions" (Nov 2023, recalled). It is built on ASME V&V 40-2018, a risk-informed credibility framework. So a safety regulator HAS written down how a model's output can stand in for physical testing. FAA and DLA have no equivalent for compliance-by-analysis of replacement parts. NASA CR-20210015404 (2021, Boeing, Pratt & Whitney, Stanford, U. Wyoming) sets out a vision and research roadmap for Certification by Analysis of airplanes and engines (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210015404). It is aimed at flight and engine test, not spare parts.

## 3. Pathway (PMA via test and computation / DLA SAR) and what is broken

1. Acquire the OEM part and reverse-engineer it (CT, CMM, chemistry, microstructure, hardness, coatings). Physics clock: destructive sectioning, several samples needed. Gap: no standard "complete characterization" recipe and no statistical equivalence output.
2. Classify criticality (Critical Safety Item, life-limited part). Gap: manual judgement, conservative, drives everything after it.
3. Build the TDP and compliance matrix against the cert basis (14 CFR 21.303; FAA Order 8110.42 (recalled rev D)). Gap: prose rules, no machine check, no coverage metric.
4. Analysis (FEA, fatigue, thermal) plus a test plan. The DER approves data on Form 8110-3. Gap: analysis is accepted on the DER's judgement, not tool qualification. Models are not credibility-graded.
5. Conformity inspection and tests (fatigue, endurance, engine tests). Physics clock: real hours on rigs. Gap: test rigs are scarce, and results are not pooled.
6. Quality system and fabrication inspection (Part 21 Subpart K / AS9100). Gap: production equivalence is checked by sampling, not by in-process signatures.
7. Regulator review. For FAA PMA this is the ACO or an ODA. For DLA, the SAR goes to the military service Engineering Support Activity (ESA), with backlogs of months to years (recalled; DLA has publicly cited SAR backlogs). Gap: THE BOTTLENECK is reviewer hours, and reviewers get no tools.
8. First article test, then production and continued airworthiness. Gap: no closed loop from service data back into the models.

## 4. Who is near this (verification side)
- **Simulation incumbents:** Synopsys/Ansys, Siemens (Simcenter plus Altair), Dassault SIMULIA, Hexagon MSC, Cadence (BETA CAE). They sell analysis, not regulator-accepted sign-off. They are adjacent, and they are the likely acquirer if someone builds the sign-off layer.
- **AI-physics surrogates:** PhysicsX (about $135M Series B, 2025, recalled), Neural Concept (about $100M Series C, 2025, recalled), Luminary Cloud, Navier, Monolith (recalled). Speed, not acceptance. Adjacent.
- **Inspection/CT:** Lumafield (industrial CT, about $75M+ raised, recalled), Glimpse, Instrumental. These cover characterization for step 1. Adjacent.
- **Test-data platforms:** Nominal (test and evaluation data, about $75M Series B 2025, recalled), Sift. Step 5 data plumbing. Adjacent.
- **Digital certification:** Istari Digital (Will Roper, DoD digital engineering and "digital certification", recalled). Closest in spirit to a sign-off layer for DoD. Potentially direct.
- **AM qualification:** America Makes, Sigma Additive (in-process monitoring), Phase3D, Additive Assurance. These cover process-signature equivalence for step 6. Adjacent.
- **Materials data:** Citrine Informatics, NCAMP (Wichita State). The allowables layer.
- **Chip AI verification (the template):** ChipAgents, Cognichip, Bronco, Axiomise. None of them works on physical parts.
- **The applicants doing it by hand:** HEICO, Wencor, Jamco and others hire in-house DERs. They would be customers for a tool.

## 5. What would need to be done (assuming plentiful compute and intelligence)
1. **Compile the "physical PDK."** Turn the cert basis (14 CFR parts, ACs, Order 8110.42, MIL-specs, OEM process specs where legally obtainable) into an executable rule deck per part class, with automatic compliance-matrix generation and a coverage-closure number.
2. **Build a "part equivalence checker."** Fuse CT, CMM, chemistry, EBSD microstructure, residual stress, surface and coating data into a probabilistic equivalence certificate: the replica sits inside the OEM population distribution with stated confidence for each critical characteristic. This is the Formality/Conformal analog. Formal proof isn't possible here, but quantified bounds are.
3. **Build the correlation moat.** A robotic coupon and component test lab running continuously to build test-correlated allowables for legacy alloys and processes ("Liberty files for metals"). The physics clock is real but parallelizable, and the data compounds.
4. **Get the gatekeeper to delegate to the tool.** Pursue an FAA policy or Issue Paper and a DLA ESA pilot that accepts credibility-graded analysis (a V&V 40-style framework adapted from the FDA precedent) for defined part classes. Alternatively, become an ODA so the company's own delegated unit uses the tool. That reproduces "foundry-certified golden sign-off."
5. **Build reviewer-side tooling.** Give DLA ESAs and FAA ACOs an AI pre-review that checks a SAR or PMA package against the rule deck and flags gaps. Today's bottleneck is reviewer hours. Whoever cuts them gets to set the format.
6. **Close the service-data loop.** Ingest SDRs, teardown findings and fleet usage to re-verify parts continuously (a regression farm).
7. **Liability.** Pair the sign-off with insurance or warranty capacity. Chips never needed this because the foundry and designer carry the risk, but for flight parts the underwriter is a second gatekeeper.

## 6. Answer on the AI play
The play is to become the "golden sign-off" for replacement parts: a verification engine (rule deck, equivalence certificate, credibility-graded analysis, coverage number) whose output a gatekeeper (DLA ESA first, then FAA ACO/ODA) accepts. It is worth more than any single approval because every applicant must pass through it. There is a strategic tension with the Approval Factory. EDA won by staying neutral (Synopsys does not make chips). A neutral sign-off tool could sell to HEICO and Wencor, and to the regulators. The suggested path: run the Approval Factory first to earn correlation data and regulator trust on the bottleneck review (C-130/KC-135 SARs), then spin the verification layer out as the neutral standard.
