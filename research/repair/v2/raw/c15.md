# c15: New defence manufacturing startups that touch qualification (Hadrian, Divergent, Machina Labs, Firestorm, Rangeview, Freeform, Isembard, Cuby, Re:Build): do any own approvals or sell qualified parts for sustainment?

## Short answer
None of them was found to own approvals as a durable asset: no source approvals in its own name (DLA SAR approved-source status), no government-accepted technical data packages, no FAA PMA, no engineering/design authority. They all sell **capacity** (build-to-print, manufacturing-as-a-service, deployable cells, foundry time). The customer keeps the approval: the OEM or prime, or a government engineering support activity (ESA) such as the depot or the program office. The two closest to sustainment are Machina Labs, with RoboCraftsman cells inside an Air Force depot and a qualification effort run by the Rapid Sustainment Office (RSO) and the University of Dayton Research Institute (UDRI), and Rangeview, with a Navy contract (Aug 2026) to "operate foundry capacity producing MRO replacement parts". Even in these two cases, the government's engineers decide whether a part is qualified. The company makes the part. The approval step, which is the "Approval Factory" idea, is still unclaimed. These firms are better treated as manufacturing partners or acquirers than as competitors. The main exception: Hadrian's "Opus" software claims to "interpret legacy designs", a step toward the data-package side.

## Evidence (fetched 2026-10-20)

### Machina Labs (robotic sheet forming, "RoboCraftsman")
- 3 Apr 2024: Warner Robins Air Logistics Complex (WR-ALC, Robins AFB) begins using a portable Machina Deployable System for "repair and sustainment parts" on aging aircraft. WR-ALC's automation expert (Shane Groves) says it makes parts "up to six months faster". No contract value, platform, qualification or airworthiness status is given. https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240403536354/en/ ; https://www.potomacofficersclub.com/news/machina-labs-supports-aircraft-sustainment-needs-at-robins-air-force-base/
- 7 Apr 2025: second RoboCraftsman for the **Air Force Rapid Sustainment Office** (under AFLCMC). It goes to UDRI, which will work with Machina and the RSO to "train, identify and qualify replacement parts for multiple aircraft". Machina cites $14M in total defense awards. **No qualification is claimed as done**, and no part numbers are named. https://machinalabs.ai/resources/machina-labs-strengthens-partnership-with-u.s.-department-of-defense-to-advance-aircraft-sustainment
- Defense page: "aging platforms and hard-to-source components", depot lead times "up to 56 weeks" vs days, a claimed saving of more than $1M per design from no part-specific tooling, AS9100D/ISO 9001 badges, AI process control and closed-loop inspection. No approved parts or platforms are named. https://machinalabs.ai/applications/defense
- Interpretation: Machina sells the machine and the process to the depot. Qualification is being built **at the process level, by the government (RSO/UDRI)**. If it succeeds, a qualified process shortens every later part approval, and that benefit goes to the depot, not to Machina's IP. Funding (recalled): Series B ~$32M, 2024.

### Rangeview (digital investment casting / foundry)
- 4 Aug 2026 press release: "awarded a contract to operate foundry capacity producing MRO replacement parts for U.S. Navy programs". No value or Navy command is named, and no qualification is described. 30 Jul 2026: Kratos picked Rangeview for cast components for advanced turbine engines. Site shows DFARS/ITAR/NIST/SAM/CAGE badges. https://www.rangeview.com/ ; https://www.rangeview.com/news/navy-mro-contract
- Interpretation: it is the castings counterpart of Machina. It addresses the castings bottleneck (no tooling, short lead time), but the Navy's engineering authority keeps the approval. Castings are a well-known source of obsolescence for sustainment parts, so this is the most natural manufacturing partner for an approval house working on castings.

### Hadrian (automated precision machining, Torrance/Mesa)
- Homepage products: Precision Components (build to customer spec), Manufacturing-as-a-Service (dedicated cells, including at customer sites), Factories-as-a-Service, and **Opus**, "AI-powered software platform... interprets legacy designs, automates manufacturing and inspection, trains factory technicians". No mention of sustainment, DLA, spares or qualification. https://www.hadrian.co/
- (recalled) $260M Series C, Jul 2025 (Founders Fund, Lux), Mesa AZ factory; earlier rounds bring the total to ~$360M+; customers are primes and space companies; expanded to naval/shipbuilding parts in 2025.
- Interpretation: build-to-print. "Interprets legacy designs" is the feature to watch. It would let Hadrian take paper or legacy drawings and manufacture them, but making the part and getting the government to accept the data package are separate steps, and nothing shows Hadrian files SARs or owns TDPs.

### Firestorm Labs (expeditionary additive manufacturing, xCell)
- Containerised xCell, said to be operating within 24 hours, makes UAS and "spare parts anywhere". $100M, 5-year Air Force IDIQ for 3D-printed drones. $82M Series B (total $153M). One profile says the Series B pays for "DoD qualification and acceptance testing for 3D-printed airframes". https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/firestorm-secures-82m-to-scale-battlefield-manufacturing-251369/ ; https://www.robotics.press/news/firestorm-labs-company-profile/ ; https://sacra.com/c/firestorm-labs
- Interpretation: it qualifies **its own new designs** (Firestorm is the design authority for its drones). It is not getting approvals for legacy OEM parts. Making legacy spares at the point of need would still require someone to approve the data package. Not a threat to the Approval Factory. A possible distribution channel later (approved digital TDPs printed at the point of need).

### Divergent Technologies (DAPS: generative design + AM + robotic assembly)
- Site returned 503. (recalled) Raised $230M Series E in 2023 at ~$2.3B; further 2025 raise; defense structures and airframes with General Atomics, Lockheed Martin, RTX, Bell and others. Sells to the design authority (new programs, missiles, UAS), not legacy sustainment approvals. No evidence of SAR/PMA.

### Freeform (AI-controlled metal laser powder bed fusion)
- Site returned 403. (recalled) Founded by ex-SpaceX engineers; ~$14M seed in 2024 with NVIDIA's NVentures; later round in 2025. Customers in aerospace and defense. Its pitch is in-situ sensing plus AI that would let a printed part be qualified from its build record. **This is the most relevant one for verification**: if the build data can stand in for destructive coupon testing, the "physics clock" of qualification shrinks. No approvals of its own were found.

### Isembard (UK distributed manufacturing network, Mason OS)
- "Over $50m total funding", 17 factories (8 UK, 1 DE, 1 FR, 7 US; 11 live), 71,290 parts made, 50+ machines, AS/EN 9100, JOSCAR, ITAR, AUKUS logos. Customers include Anduril UK, Rowden, Dexory, Alloyed and HQW Aerospace. No MoD sustainment or qualification offering. https://www.isembard.com/
- Interpretation: a capacity network. Its fit with sustainment is that "find a qualified shop with capacity for this sole-source part" is a matching problem it could solve for UK MoD/DE&S.

### Cuby
- Site returned 503. (recalled) Cuby Technologies builds mobile factories for housing. **No defense qualification relevance found.** The founder may mean a different company. Flag.

### Re:Build Manufacturing (Framingham MA; roll-up of US manufacturers)
- 400+ engineers, 14+ locations, 1M sq ft, "end-to-end engineering and advanced manufacturing" for A&D, sells drone batteries. No sustainment, reverse-engineering or qualified-parts offering is shown. https://rebuildmanufacturing.com/
- Interpretation: the acquired job shops probably hold CAGE codes and some approved-source positions (recalled/unverified), but these are not pursued as a product. Re:Build is a plausible later acquirer or partner for an approval house: it has engineering staff, but no system for producing approvals.

### Context on the approval channel (from search)
- DLA's Jan 2026 Value Engineering flyer: replenishment parts are bought competitively, by approved manufactured part numbers, or from qualified products lists. Non-approved sources must file a SAR. https://www.dla.mil/Portals/104/Documents/SmallBusiness/DB%20Flyers/2025%20Flyers/VALUE%20ENGINEERING%20SB%20-%20V-1-14-2026.pdf
- Solicitations in 2026 still invite SAR packages (DLA Land & Maritime hydraulic pump, 31 Jul 2026; DLA Aviation Parker Hannifin parts, 7 Jul 2026). Demand is ongoing and the channel is open to anyone.

## Pattern / opinion
1. **Approval belongs to whoever owns the design.** The startups that hold approvals (Firestorm, Divergent) hold them for their own new designs. On legacy sustainment, all of them make parts to someone else's approved data. That someone is the ESA, which is overloaded and often lacks the data.
2. **Government is funding process qualification, not part approvals.** The Machina/RSO/UDRI work is about qualifying RoboForming as a process so many part numbers can follow. Process qualification shrinks the cost of each part approval only if someone also produces the part-level evidence package (drawing, material and process spec, inspection plan, first article), and nobody is doing that systematically.
3. **The missing layer** sits between "a machine can make it" and "the government will buy it from you": reverse-engineering the TDP, producing the evidence package (SAR, ECP, first article plan), and taking it through ESA review. HEICO-style PMA houses do this for commercial aircraft. On the military side it is done one package at a time by small reverse-engineering shops and depot engineers.
4. **Competitive risk:** Hadrian, if Opus moves from "interprets legacy designs" to "outputs a TDP the ESA accepts". Machina/RSO, if the depot uses the qualified process to bypass DLA sourcing entirely (organic manufacturing at the depot). Both are adjacent today, not direct.
5. **Partnership upside:** an Approval Factory that owns the approved TDPs/SARs could send the production work to Rangeview (castings), Machina (sheet metal), Hadrian (machining) or Isembard (UK). These companies need sustainment demand they can actually serve, and today approvals block it.

## What would need to be done (verification side; assume plentiful compute)
- A **process-to-part qualification bridge**: once a process (RoboForming, digital casting, AI-monitored powder bed fusion) is qualified, automatically produce the part-level evidence (feature-by-feature comparison to the legacy drawing, simulated margins, a coupon plan reduced to only what physics still requires) in the ESA's required format.
- **Build-record-as-evidence**: in-situ sensor data plus models that the regulator accepts in place of some destructive testing. This needs a precedent agreed with AFRL/RSO/NAVAIR, and it is the only way to shorten the physics clock rather than speed up the paperwork.
- **ESA review capacity**: AI pre-review that maps a SAR package against every requirement in DLA's SAR guidance and the ESA's checklists, so a human reviewer's hours per package fall. This only works if an ESA pilot accepts the tool's output (regulator acceptance is the binding constraint).
- A **DLA source-approval and demand map**: link sole-source NSNs, items with no bids, depot backorders and the capability of qualified processes. Nobody joins DLA demand data to manufacturing capability at the part level.
- **Liability wrapper**: whoever signs the TDP takes on engineering liability. A startup would need a design-authority role (e.g., a CRADA or OTA with the RSO) or insurance to hold that.
