## 1. Demand: who holds old fleets that need obsolete spares

### London Underground (TfL)
- **1972 Stock (Bakerloo line)** [fetched, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Underground_1972_Stock]
  - Built 1972-74, so these are the oldest trains in regular UK passenger service.
  - Refurbished 1991-95 (Rosyth) and 2016-18 (Acton Works), aiming for a "forecast replacement date of 2035".
  - Because of funding, Bakerloo replacement "might not happen until the late 2030s or early 2040s", when the trains would be 60-70 years old, "probably double their design life".
  - A set was "stripped for spares at Ruislip depot in 2022". Cannibalising a set is the clearest public sign that spares have run out.
- **1973 Stock (Piccadilly line)** [fetched]
  - Built 1974-77. The 94 Siemens 2024 Stock trains are due to enter service Dec 2026 to Jun 2027, and the last 1973 Stock retires by 2030.
  - This is a short-tail demand: about 4 years.
- **Central line 1992 Stock** (recalled)
  - Wabtec Doncaster is running the Central Line Improvement Programme (new traction and motors), because the original electronics are obsolete.
  - **Signalling, relays and track equipment** on the older lines have the same problem.
- **Approval route in LU** (recalled)
  - Products must pass LU's own engineering assurance and product acceptance process under LU Category 1 standards. The engineering approval is done by LU itself, as the infrastructure manager and operator.
  - Network Rail runs a parallel scheme: product acceptance (NR/L2/RSE/100/05) plus RISQS supplier qualification.

### Deutsche Bahn
- **3D printing program** (recalled; sibling l02 is also unverified)
  - Started 2015-16. DB co-founded the "Mobility goes Additive" network in 2016.
  - DB has reported more than 100,000 printed parts across several hundred to 1,000+ part types.
  - Parts are mainly obsolete or low-volume: headrest covers, coat hooks, vent grilles, cable housings, and some metal parts for older ICE and regional fleets.
  - DB claims lead times fall from months to days.
- **What made it work:** DB is the operator *and* the Entity in Charge of Maintenance (ECM), under EU Regulation 2019/779 (recalled).
  - For a like-for-like replacement, the ECM owns the decision.
  - If the change is "significant" under the Common Safety Method for risk evaluation (CSM-RA, Regulation 402/2013), an independent Assessment Body (AsBo) must check it.
  - The authorisation authority (EBA / ERA) is touched only if the vehicle authorisation changes.
- **Takeaway:** DB grew printed spares by staying in the non-safety-critical, interior and housing class, where the ECM approves the part itself. Its count of safety-critical printed parts (brakes, bogies, couplers) is small (recalled).

### Amtrak [fetched, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amfleet]
- **Amfleet I and II**
  - Amfleet I was built 1975-77, Amfleet II 1980-83. 642 cars were built; 447 Amfleet I and 136 Amfleet II were in service in October 2025.
  - Siemens Airo deliveries start in 2026 (83 trainsets, Dec 2022 order, $7.3B committed July 2021).
  - Amfleet I retirement was planned for 2027 to end-2030. In October 2025 Amtrak issued an RFI for firms to buy and scrap the Amfleet fleet.
- **Other old fleets** (recalled): Superliner I/II (1979-81 and 1993-96), Viewliner I, and the long-distance fleet whose replacement is still being procured. These keep demand alive into the 2030s.
- **Amtrak OIG** [fetched homepage]: recent featured reports cover maintenance-facility upgrades and long-distance fleet replacement risk. I could not reach the OIG reports on parts or materials management.
- **US approval route** (recalled)
  - FRA rules: 49 CFR 238 (passenger equipment safety, incl. 238.103 fire safety), 49 CFR 231 (safety appliances), 49 CFR 229 (locomotives).
  - FRA does not approve individual parts. The railroad certifies that the part complies, and APTA PRESS standards are the de facto specs.
  - As in the EU, the permission sits with the operator.

### US Navy surface fleet
- **Demand driver** (recalled): GAO has reported for years that most surface ship maintenance availabilities finish late. On-time completion has historically been well below half, and lack of material is a cited cause. Cannibalisation across ships is routine.
  - The fleet is ageing: DDG-51 Flight I/II hulls from the 1990s, LSD-41/49, and the remaining CG-47s being retired.
- **Approval chain**
  - NAVSEA Technical Warrant Holders hold the engineering authority.
  - NAVSUP WSS (Mechanicsburg) and DLA Land and Maritime (Columbus) handle the Source Approval Request (SAR) and alternate-source path for ship parts. A SAR needs a technical data package (TDP) and first article testing.
  - The Level I / SUBSAFE material-control tiers sit at the top.
  - For printed parts, NAVSEA allows only "low criticality" parts to be printed aboard, with commanding-officer approval (sibling note).
  - NAVSEA's first approved shipboard metal AM part was a drain strainer orifice on USS Harry S. Truman (2018-19), installed for a one-year evaluation (sibling, recalled).
- **Money**
  - Maritime/Submarine Industrial Base (MIB/SIB) gets multi-$B per year (recalled), mostly for workforce, supplier capacity and AM capacity.
  - BlueForge Alliance placed a US$1.84M order with AML3D for 5 non-safety-critical obsolete submarine parts (31 Mar 2026; sibling note).
  - The Navy AM Center of Excellence in Danville has delivered more than 300 components (sibling).
  - In the public material siblings found, none of this money goes to AI for reverse engineering or approval.
- **Commercial marine** (recalled)
  - Class societies (ABS, DNV, LR) type-approve parts. DNV-ST-B203 covers AM parts; ABS has an AM guide.
  - DNV has recommended practices on when simulations or AI can serve as evidence: RP-0513 (simulation models) and RP-0671 (AI-enabled systems).
  - Wilhelmsen Ships Service and Ivaldi Group run on-demand printed ship spares, delivered in port.

## 3. The full rail/transit/marine verification pathway, with gaps

1. **Spot obsolescence early.** Done poorly: mostly reactive, found when a stock-out grounds a vehicle (LU stripped a 1972 Stock set for spares). DMSMS tools (SiliconExpert, Z2Data) cover electronics only, not mechanical or interior parts.
2. **Classify safety criticality.** Is it safety-critical, fire-relevant, structural, or a safety appliance? Done by depot engineers by hand, with inconsistent results between operators.
3. **Reverse engineer** (scan, CMM, material ID, legacy drawings). Data access is better than in aviation: BR, DB and Amtrak heritage drawings are often owned by the operator. But drawings are on paper or microfiche, unindexed.
4. **Make the equivalence argument / CSM-RA significance test** (EU), or the FRA 238 compliance self-certification (US), or the NAVSEA/SAR TDP (Navy). **This is the step nobody productises.** Engineers write a bespoke dossier for each part.
5. **Run the physical tests:**
   - EN 45545-2 fire (cone calorimeter, smoke density, toxicity): weeks, a few k€ to 20k€ per material or part (recalled).
   - EN 61373 shock and vibration.
   - EN 50155 for electronics.
   - EN 15085 welding.
   - EN 13749 / EN 13260 for bogies and wheelsets.
   - Navy: first article test, MIL-S-901 shock, MIL-STD-167 vibration.
   - This is the physics clock.
6. **Independent assessment** (AsBo, class surveyor, NAVSEA Technical Warrant Holder). Capacity-limited and billed by the hour.
7. **Qualify the supplier** (IRIS / ISO/TS 22163, RISQS, Navy QPL / Level I).
8. **Accept the part into configuration and the maintenance file** (ECM / operator, or NAVSEA drawing revision and NSN record update).
9. **Monitor it in service.** Rarely closed back into the approval evidence.

### Steps not done, or done badly
- Steps 4 and 6 are hand-written for each operator and each part, with no reuse across DB, ÖBB, SBB, SNCF, LU, Amtrak and others.
- Step 5 fire-test data is not shared or pooled. The same material is re-tested for each part shape.
- Step 1 is reactive.
- Step 9 does not feed back into the evidence.

## 4. What would need to be done (assume abundant compute and intelligence)

- **Operator-side equivalence dossier engine.** It ingests the legacy drawing or scan, maps the part to the applicable EN/FRA/NAVSEA clauses, runs the CSM-RA significance test, and writes an AsBo-ready dossier.
  - In rail the operator is the approver for like-for-like replacements, so the loop is buyer equals approver. That is shorter than FAA PMA.
- **Cross-operator mutual-recognition registry** of approved substitutes, with the evidence attached.
  - It needs ERA/RSSB/APTA buy-in, or a club of ECMs in the way Mobility goes Additive works.
  - Under CSM-RA, an assessment already accepted by another AsBo can be reused for a "reference system" (Art. 8) (recalled). The law already allows reuse; nobody operationalises it.
- **Fire-performance surrogate models.**
  - Train them on pooled cone-calorimeter, smoke and toxicity data, so physical EN 45545 tests become confirmatory and are done once per material family, not once per part.
  - Changing this needs CEN TC 256 / an ERA statement accepting "test by family plus model". DNV RP-0513-style rules on model credibility are a template.
- **Fleet-wide obsolescence radar** for mechanical and interior parts. It would mine maintenance logs (work orders, cannibalisation records) to predict stock-outs years ahead and batch the approvals.
- **Navy:** an automated SAR/TDP generator plus a NAVSEA Technical Warrant Holder reviewer workbench. It would scale beyond today's "low-criticality only" AM limit by building family-qualification evidence (process allowables plus a similarity argument).
- **Liability:** the AI produces the evidence, and a liable signer (AsBo, DER-like engineer, class surveyor) signs. Best case, the verifier *is* an accredited AsBo or notified body that owns the AI. Accreditation runs through UKAS / DAkkS (ISO/IEC 17020 and 17065), which a startup can obtain (recalled: about 12-18 months).

## 5. AI plays on the verification side (opinion)

1. **"AI Assessment Body"**
   - Get accredited as a CSM-RA AsBo / DeBo (UK and EU) and use AI so that independent assessment costs and takes about 10x less.
   - It sells to every ECM and every supplier. The binding constraint, the signature, becomes the moat.
2. **ECM equivalence copilot / dossier factory**, sold to operators (DB, LU, Network Rail, Amtrak, Brightline, US transit agencies through FTA-funded state-of-good-repair work).
   - KPI: engineering hours per accepted substitute.
3. **Shared substitute-approval registry**, a network-effect play: one approval reused across N operators.
4. **Navy SAR/TDP plus warrant-holder workbench.** The highest dollar value per part; it extends the Approval Factory to NAVSUP WSS and DLA Land and Maritime.
5. **EN 45545 fire-test surrogate, plus a lab that owns the remaining physical tests.**

## 6. Surprises
- In rail and transit, like-for-like spare approval belongs to the operator/ECM, not a central regulator like the FAA. The buyer can say yes.
- LU will run 1972 Stock to about double its design life (late 2030s to 2040s), and it already cannibalises sets. That is a long, concrete tail of demand.
- Amfleet I and 1973 Stock retire by about 2030, so their demand is short. Do not build on retiring fleets; build on fleets with extended lives (Bakerloo, Superliner, Navy DDGs, US transit cars).
- The largest public operator program (DB, 100k+ parts, recalled) succeeded *because* it avoided safety-critical parts. The value and the gap are in safety-relevant parts, where assessment is the bottleneck.
