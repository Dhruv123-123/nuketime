# s08: Step: regulator/reviewer review (FAA ACO, DLA ESA, NRC inspectors). Staffing, queue data, rework cycles, what share of elapsed time is review, and how reviewer-side tools could change it.

Search budget: 3 WebSearch used. Several FAA/DLA pages returned 403 to the fetcher. Figures marked (recalled) come from memory and were not re-verified this session.

## 1. DLA source approval (SAR): the best public numbers found

Source: DLA Land and Maritime Value Engineering, "Source Approval Request (SAR)", Eric Wilde, NDIA, 20-21 June 2018. https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2018/dla/SARBriefing_SC.pdf (text pulled from the PDF).

Nominal clock, step by step:
- Competition Advocate (CAO) logs the package and forwards it to Value Engineering (VE): within 5 days.
- VE analyst does a completeness and business-case review: within 15 days. VE had **7 analysts** for all of DLA Land and Maritime.
- If the package is incomplete but fixable, the contractor gets **30 days** to supply missing data. If it does not arrive, the package is rejected.
- The Engineering Support Activity (ESA) does the weapon-system engineering evaluation. This is the military service's engineering office (e.g., the Air Force for C-130), not DLA. Target is a **90-day response**. The ESA can accept, reject, or ask for more data.
- VE concurs or does not concur with the ESA's answer: 15 days.
- VE gathers any further contractor data: 30 days.
- CAO sends the notification letter: 5 days.
- **The nominal total is about 190 days, of which 90 are the ESA step. Even on paper, the ESA review is about half the clock and the only step DLA does not control.**

FY17 throughput for DLA Land and Maritime:
- 282 packages received.
- 111 approved, worth $35.5M in potential annual savings.
- 114 rejected.
- 144 pending, worth $30.9M in potential annual savings.
- 7 items moved to fully competitive government drawings, worth $2.1M.
- So about **49% approval among decided packages**, and the pending backlog equals **about half a year's intake**. About **$320k of annual savings per approved package**, and about $215k of savings sitting in each pending one.

Rejection reasons DLA lists:
- Package incomplete.
- Weapon criticality.
- Item upgraded or discontinued.
- Business case "not in the best interest of the government", for example low demand. DLA Aviation also says SARs may not be processed if demand is too low.
- **"Can't validate part (no government data to compare)"**: the reviewer has no baseline to check against.
- "Good on paper, but need to prove will work in application", which means qualification testing is still needed.

Process changes DLA had already made:
- Lean Six Sigma project, 2011-12, for centralized tracking.
- VE improvements, 2013-15, including a sample SAR and a candidate NSN list.
- DAG 17.7501-6, from 17 June 2014: a newly approved source gets a multi-source award clause for at least one year, and can receive **40% of the award if not low but fair and reasonable**. This is a demand guarantee for newly approved sources.

Historical evidence that the ESA step is outside DLA's control:
- GAO B-202893-type decision (gao.gov/products/458026, 1981): the Army TACOM evaluation took about 4 months, which DLA called "typical". DLA asked for 21 days but "had no authority to control ATAC's timing". DLA awarded to the incumbent while the review was still open.
- GAO B-232702 (1988): GAO sustained a protest over unreasonable delay in processing a source approval. Only the search snippet was seen.
- DLA solicitations still say that if a SAR cannot be evaluated in time, award goes to approved sources.

Opinion: anecdotal industry reports put actual ESA turnaround at 6 to 24 months on aviation items (recalled; not verified). The 144-pending figure is consistent with turnaround well beyond 90 days.

## 2. FAA (ACO / MIDO / ODA) for PMA and repairs

Evidence found:
- GAO-11-14 (Oct 2010), https://gao.gov/products/gao-11-14. Aircraft Certification tracked how long projects were "wait-listed" (project sequencing) in a national database. Flight Standards did not. In Oct 2011 FAA changed the CSOP database to flag wait-listed submissions. So **FAA has internal queue data, but it is not published.**
- GAMA testimony (Pete Bunce), Senate Commerce, 21 Apr 2015:
  - FAA engineers often "retain these activities themselves and not utilize the available FAA-authorized ODA resources".
  - ODA use is inconsistent from region to region and from project to project.
  - Foreign validation took 21 weeks where the original FAA certification took under a year.
  - The source is a Senate Commerce PDF (commerce.senate.gov file 812FE92E...).
- An AVweb (Feb 2017) article citing GAO says that **more than 90% of certification work is delegated** to industry (ODA, DERs). The FAA reviewer's share of hours is small, but it holds the critical path: issue papers, compliance-plan acceptance, conformity, and the final finding.
- Leeham News (Oct 2025) says the FAA certification units are chronically understaffed, and the 2025 cuts and shutdown made that worse. It cites the slow A321XLR certification.
- 2006 AIN: OEMs said FAA needed more certification engineers. Projects needing more than 40 hours of FAA time went into a sequencing hold until staff were free.

Recalled (not verified this session):
- The FAA Aircraft Certification Service (AIR) has roughly 1,300-1,500 engineers and inspectors across ACO branches and MIDOs.
- Under FAA Order 8110.42 (PMA), a "test and computation" PMA needs an ACO engineering finding plus a MIDO production-system evaluation. "Identicality" PMAs need a licence from the TC holder.
- An "Identicality by design" PMA is evaluated by the ACO. Typical elapsed time for a non-delegated test-and-computation PMA is 6-18 months. Of that, applicant engineering is the larger share, and FAA review plus rework rounds are 2-6 months.
- HEICO and Wencor hold ODA or DER capability that lets them self-find many PMAs. That delegation is the competitive moat for PMA houses.
- The FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (P.L. 118-63) includes provisions to expand and streamline ODA, to track certification-project timeliness, and to report on certification workforce gaps.

## 3. NRC (relevant to nuclear spares and commercial-grade dedication)

Evidence:
- NRC NEIMA generic milestone schedules (https://www.nrc.gov/about-nrc/generic-schedules.html), measured from acceptance review to the final safety evaluation:
  - License amendments: 11-12 months.
  - Topical reports: 18 months.
  - Exemptions: 12 months.
  - Emergency amendments: 1 month.
- **For replacement parts, the NRC is usually not a pre-approver.** Licensees and dedicating entities self-certify under 10 CFR 21 and Appendix B (commercial-grade dedication). NRC review comes after the fact, through vendor inspections and resident or regional inspectors. The bottleneck is utility or dedicator engineering acceptance and the Appendix B supplier audit (NUPIC), not an NRC queue. That last point is from knowledge (recalled).
- NRC internal AI (https://www.nrc.gov/ai/internally-focused, updated 28 Jan 2026):
  - "SimplifAI" (deployed) for text retrieval and generation in licensing and oversight.
  - M365 Copilot (deployed).
  - USAi, ChatGPT Enterprise and Gemini for Government (pre-deployment).
  - No time-saved metrics are published.
  - The NRC says "AI is not used as a substitute for professional judgment."
- NRC AI Strategic Plan published 29 Sept 2025 (ML25269A196). Workshop on AI regulatory applications held 15 Sept 2026.
- EO 14300 (May 2025) pushes the NRC toward fixed deadlines (18 months for new reactor licences). The ADVANCE Act (2024) requires efficiency reporting.

## 4. Share of elapsed time that is review

Evidence is thin; the following is estimation.

DLA SAR:
- Applicant engineering takes 3-12 months (reverse engineering, first article, test plan).
- Government review is about 190 days nominal. In practice it is often 6-24 months (recalled).
- **Review plus rework likely makes up 40-70% of elapsed time.** The ESA is the long pole.

FAA PMA, test and computation:
- Applicant engineering and testing dominate the hours.
- Elapsed time is gated by acceptance of the certification plan, FAA test witnessing and conformity scheduling, and rework rounds.
- **Review plus waiting is likely 30-50% of elapsed time without delegation, and under 20% with in-house ODA or DER.**

NRC, for parts:
- Review is mostly internal to the licensee or dedicator.

Rework cycles:
- DLA explicitly builds in two 30-day missing-data loops, and about half of decided packages are rejected.
- **Rejection is the bigger time cost: a resubmission resets the queue.**

## 5. Reviewer-side tools: what exists and what is missing

Existing tools:
- DLA has centralized SAR tracking (2012), the SAR guide and checklist, and candidate lists.
  - Commercial tools sold to suppliers: Haystack (now Lighthouse) and DIBBS.
  - Analytics vendors, from knowledge (recalled): Govini (sole-source and supply-chain analytics for DoD), and Palantir (DLA and Air Force).
- FAA has CSOP/WMS project tracking and DRS. Recalled: the FAA is running a "digital transformation" effort and a model-based certification pathfinder.
- The NRC has SimplifAI.
- Nuclear startups (recalled):
  - Atomic Canyon: "Neutron" search over NRC ADAMS, with ORNL supercomputer work and the PG&E Diablo Canyon deal (2024-25).
  - Everstar: AI for nuclear licensing documents, seed funded around 2024.
  - Nuclearn: AI for utility operations and corrective-action programmes.
- These nuclear tools serve the applicant or licensee side, not reviewer-side acceptance.

**What nobody does today (the gap):**
1. **Submission linting against the reviewer's own checklist** before submission: SAR guide categories, FAA Order 8110.42 data requirements, compliance-plan templates. Half of SARs are rejected, many for incompleteness. A pre-review "would this pass VE's 15-day check and the ESA's data-sufficiency check" tool cuts out a whole queue cycle.
2. **Machine-readable, evidence-linked packages.** Every requirement, whether a drawing feature, material spec or test, is traced to its evidence: CMM scan, material certs, test data, FEA. The reviewer verifies the links and does not reconstruct the argument. This is the equivalent of an FDA eCTD for parts. No such standard exists for SARs or PMAs.
3. **Baseline-reconstruction for "no government data to compare"**: reverse-engineered digital twins plus OEM-equivalence evidence offered to the ESA as the baseline. Today this is an explicit rejection reason.
4. **Precedent and similarity engines** for reviewers: "this NSN or part family was approved before with this evidence set". This lets the ESA approve by analogy and lets the FAA accept a "similar to previously approved" argument.
5. **Queue transparency.** The FAA and DLA hold queue data internally (CSOP, the SAR tracker) but publish none. A FOIA-driven or self-built dataset of turnaround by ESA, office and part family would make it possible to route work to the fastest pathway.
6. **Reviewer-capacity substitution through delegation.** The legal lever is the FAA ODA/DER system. An AI-native firm holding an ODA for PMA, with AI drafting the findings and human unit members signing, compresses the FAA's portion of the clock. DoD has no equivalent of the ODA for SARs: the ESA cannot delegate to a contractor. Changing that needs a DoD policy change, such as a "qualified engineering source" or third-party ESA-equivalent pilot. **That is a regulator-acceptance constraint, not a physics one.**

## 6. Binding constraints and what would change them

- **Law and regulator acceptance.** Findings must be made by the FAA or its designee, and the ESA engineering authority sits with the military service. Changing this requires:
  - Delegation (ODA, which already exists).
  - DoD engineering-authority delegation (does not exist, but airworthiness authorities under MIL-HDBK-516 can rely on third parties).
  - Reviewers adopting AI-assist tools inside government. The NRC already has; the FAA and services are slower.
- **Physics clocks.** Qualification tests and first-article tests still run. Reviewers can accept analysis in lieu of test only with validated models. Getting there needs a regulator-accepted credibility framework such as ASME V&V 40, which is used at the FDA but not yet for parts.
- **Liability.** A reviewer approving a part bears the institutional risk. Reviewer tools that leave an auditable trail lower the perceived risk.
- **Data.** ESA reviewers lack the OEM TDP. AI-reconstructed baselines need acceptance as "government data to compare".
