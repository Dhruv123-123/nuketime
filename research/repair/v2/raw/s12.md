# s12: Liability and insurance for approval holders (product liability for PMA/DER, E&O, recalls). How it is priced; would AI-made packages change it?

Evidence tags: [E] = sourced in this session (URL given); [R] = recalled from prior knowledge, not re-verified here; [O] = opinion/inference.

## 1. Who carries the liability today

- **The PMA holder is the manufacturer of record and the natural defendant.** Under US strict products liability, liability falls on whoever makes and sells the part. The leading analysis is Beard & Smith, "Disassembling Assembler Liability: Are OEMs Strictly Liable for PMA Parts in Aviation Cases?", 82 J. Air L. & Com. (2017) (Wiggin and Dana) [E: https://www.wiggin.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/38726_disassembling-assembler-liability_journal-of-air-law-and-commerce_ksmith-ebeard.pdf]. It finds the majority bright-line rule is that an OEM is not strictly liable for a replacement part it did not make, sell or distribute, even when the part is identical. Making the OEM liable "would effectively transform the OEM into an insurer of the PMA manufacturer". Liability attaches to whoever is "in the best position to ... translate that knowledge into a cost of production against which liability insurance can be obtained."
  - **Exception:** Sikkelee v. Precision Airmotive, 876 F. Supp. 2d 479 (M.D. Pa. 2012). Strict liability survived against the OEM (Lycoming) where a licensing agreement gave the OEM exclusive control of the design of a PMA part made by a third party [E, same PDF].
  - **Contrary trend:** the highest courts of Maryland, New York and California have imposed liability on non-makers in asbestos cases [E].
- **FAA approval is not a shield.** Sikkelee, 822 F.3d 680 (3d Cir. 2016), held that FAA type certification does not field-preempt state-law design-defect standards. Sikkelee, 907 F.3d 701 (3d Cir. 2018), rejected conflict preemption, and certiorari was denied in 2019 [R]. So holding a PMA, STC, DER-approved repair or TC does **not** immunize the holder. Juries apply state law, and FAA compliance is only evidence.
- **The FAA itself is immune for certification errors.** United States v. Varig Airlines, 467 U.S. 797 (1984), holds that the FAA's spot-check certification is a discretionary function under the FTCA [R]. The regulator's sign-off never moves liability onto the regulator.
- **DERs are FAA designees but are not shielded like FAA employees.** Courts have generally treated designees as non-employees for FTCA purposes [R]. Individual and consulting DERs therefore carry personal and firm E&O exposure, and DER/ODA consultancies buy professional liability plus aviation products/completed-operations cover [R]. The ODA holder (the company) usually bears the exposure, not the individual.
- **GARA (1994) and its "rolling" provision.** GARA imposes an 18-year statute of repose for general aviation aircraft (fewer than 20 seats, not in scheduled passenger service). A new replacement part restarts the 18-year clock for claims tied to that part [R]. A PMA maker selling into legacy GA fleets therefore carries a fresh 18-year tail on every part, while the OEM's airframe exposure may have expired. GARA does not apply to airliners or military aircraft.
- **Military-first changes the liability picture favourably [R/O]:**
  - **Feres doctrine:** servicemembers cannot sue the US government for service-related injury. They can sue contractors.
  - **Government contractor defense** (Boyle v. United Technologies, 487 U.S. 500, 1988). It shields a contractor from state design-defect claims when (1) the government approved reasonably precise specifications, (2) the product conformed, and (3) the contractor warned of dangers it knew and the government did not. A reverse-engineered part built to a DLA/engineering-support-activity-approved TDP is a plausible fit for prongs 1 and 2. Whether a government *approval* of a contractor-generated design counts as "approved reasonably precise specifications" is litigated case by case. Courts look for genuine government review, not a rubber stamp.
  - **Unusually hazardous risks:** P.L. 85-804 and FAR 52.250-1 indemnification exist but are rarely granted for spares.
  - **The real big exposure on the DLA side is the False Claims Act, not tort.** Nonconforming or substituted parts bring treble damages plus per-claim penalties, often via qui tam relators. Recent DLA-parts FCA settlements typically run from single-digit $M to tens of $M [R, specific cases not re-verified]. This is a *quality-evidence* liability: it turns on whether the delivered part matches the approved TDP and test records.
- **Recalls in aviation are Airworthiness Directives, not consumer recalls.** The FAA can issue an AD against a PMA part (14 CFR Part 39). The PMA holder must report failures, malfunctions and defects (14 CFR 21.3) and keep a quality system (21.137/21.307) [E: CFR citations in the Beard & Smith PDF]. The PMA holder's costs are replacement parts, labour credits under warranty and contract, and **grounding/loss-of-use claims** from operators. That is why "grounding" is a named cover line.

## 2. How the risk is insured and priced

- **Lines:** aviation products liability (bodily injury and property damage, including passengers, usually with *grounding* cover), aviation completed operations for MRO/repair stations, hangarkeepers, and professional/E&O for engineering and DER firms. Product recall or "product withdrawal" cover is a separate, thinner market [R].
- **HEICO** (about 20,000 PMAs, 400-550 new PMAs a year) [E: FY2025 10-K, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/000004661925000082/hei-20251031.htm]:
  - It is "a named insured under policies ... (i) product liability, including grounding" and believes its coverage is adequate.
  - Risk factor: "We may incur product liability claims that are not fully insured and such insurance may not be available at commercially reasonable rates ... an uninsured or partially insured claim, or a claim for which third-party indemnification is not available, could have a material adverse effect."
  - "Our customers typically require us to maintain substantial insurance coverage." So **insurance is a customer gate**: airlines and MROs require it contractually.
  - Item 3 discloses no material product-liability litigation. Across roughly 20,000 PMAs over 25+ years there is no publicly known catastrophic PMA-caused airliner loss [R/O]. That is the strongest empirical data point that PMA risk is actually low.
- **Market size and conditions:**
  - Aviation gross written premium exceeded US$8B in 2024, a 20-year high (Allianz/Gallagher) [E: https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/au/news/breaking-news/aviation-insurance-market-faces-growing-pressure--gallagher-531249.aspx; https://www.avweb.com/aviation-news/new-report-projects-aviation-insurance-premiums-will-reach-20-year-high].
  - Over 32,000 claims in 2019-2024 (US$15B), collision/crash plus "faulty workmanship or defective products" made up 85% of claim value [E].
  - The aerospace-manufacturer segment saw "stable premium offers" in Q1 2025 [E].
  - Swiss Re exited direct aviation, and social inflation is pushing liability awards up [E].
- **Who writes it [R]:** Global Aerospace (pool), AIG, Allianz (AGCS/Allianz Commercial), Chubb, Starr, Berkley Aviation, USAIG, Tokio Marine HCC, QBE, Sompo/Endurance, AXA XL, and Lloyd's syndicates. Brokers include Marsh, Aon, Gallagher, Willis and BWI. BWI advertises aviation products limits up to US$100M [E: https://bwifly.com/commercial-aviation-insurance/aviation-products-liability-insurance/].
- **Pricing mechanics [R/O]:** premium is set as a rate on annual sales or turnover (or per-unit), adjusted for:
  - part criticality (flight-critical or life-limited versus cabin/non-critical)
  - installation fleet (airliner versus GA versus military; airliner exposure gets the highest per-seat severity)
  - US exposure share
  - limits ($10M-$500M+, with towers for airliner engine parts)
  - loss history
  - QA certification (AS9100, FAA PMA quality system, AS9120 for distributors)
  - contractual risk transfer (hold-harmless agreements, additional insureds)
  - Small PMA and repair shops commonly pay low five to low six figures a year for $10-50M limits. Engine hot-section PMA makers pay much more [R, order of magnitude only].
  - Underwriters rarely price on the *engineering method*. They price on criticality, volume, quality system and track record. A new entrant with no loss history and critical parts gets the worst terms.
- **Hidden demand-side gate: lessors and lease-return conditions [R/O].** Many aircraft and engine leases restrict or ban PMA parts and DER repairs. At return, the lessee must restore OEM parts or take a value haircut. Lessors fear residual-value impairment and buyer-pool shrinkage, not tort. This is an *economic* liability, and it suppresses PMA uptake more than tort risk does.

## 3. Would AI-made packages change it?

Neutral-to-negative today [O]:
- **Discovery risk.** A plaintiff's expert will attack "the design was generated by an AI and checked by X hours of human review" before a jury. Courts do not yet credit AI substantiation, and juries may treat it as a cost-cutting story.
- **Exclusion creep.** Insurers have begun adding generative-AI exclusions and affirmative-AI products. ISO/Verisk filed GenAI-related exclusion endorsements for general liability in 2025-26 [R]. An aviation products wording could add exclusions or require disclosure of AI use in design.
- **Nothing in FAA rules allocates liability differently for AI-assisted substantiation.** The legal duty stays with the PMA holder.

Potentially strongly positive, if the package carries *more physical evidence* [O]:
- The underwriter's real questions are: will this part fail, will we find out early, and can we prove conformance? AI-native packages could come with:
  - full digital-thread conformance (each serial's material certs, CT scans and dimensional data)
  - physics-based margins versus the OEM part across many simulated loads
  - fleet service-difficulty and maintenance-record mining showing the OEM part's own failure modes
  - automated 21.3 failure monitoring after entry into service
- That is better loss-control evidence than a traditional 300-1,000-hour package. It could support lower rates, higher limits and a Boyle-style "government approved precise specs" defense on military parts.
- **Fast, cheap re-substantiation lowers the cost of fixing a problem.** If an AD hits, a fix package in weeks instead of months means less grounding loss, the line insurers fear most.

## 4. Who is doing anything near this

- **AIUC** (Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company, SF): the AIUC-1 standard for AI agents, audits and evaluations, and insurance. It explicitly uses the "insurers funded Underwriters Laboratories" model. Series A of US$55M [E: https://aiuc.com]. Focus is software agents, not physical parts. Adjacent.
- **Armilla AI:** Lloyd's coverholder/MGA selling affirmative AI liability (model errors, agent mistakes, property damage from an underperforming AI), backed by Chaucer, AXIS and Convex. Limits up to US$25M aggregate and US$5M per model [E: https://www.armilla.ai]. Software-model focus. Adjacent.
- **Munich Re aiSure** (AI performance guarantees) [R]. Adjacent.
- **Aviation insurers and pools** (Global Aerospace, AIG, Allianz, etc.) price PMA products liability conventionally. None is known to price on engineering-evidence quality or offer an AI-substantiation product [O].
- **HEICO and Wencor** carry liability internally on large diversified books. Their scale and track record are a moat in insurance terms too: they get capacity and rates a startup cannot [O].
- **No known player** offers: (a) an aviation-specific MGA that underwrites PMA/DER/military reverse-engineered parts on digital-thread and physics evidence; (b) a lessor residual-value guarantee for PMA/DER content; or (c) a "UL for substantiation packages" that insurers, lessors and regulators all accept [O; not exhaustively searched, search quota limited].

## 5. What would need to be done (assume abundant intelligence/compute)

1. **An evidence-priced aviation products MGA (Lloyd's coverholder model, like Armilla but for hardware).** Write PMA/DER/military-spares products and grounding cover where the rate is a function of measured evidence: margins, CT/conformance on every serial, and fleet failure-mode mining. Capacity comes from Lloyd's syndicates. The approval factory is its first and anchor insured.
2. **A "UL for engineering substantiation."** An independent scoring and certification of packages (human or AI-made), plus a standard for AI-generated substantiation (an aerospace analogue of AIUC-1). Cross-reference it to FAA Order 8110.42 and DLA SAR/TDP requirements. It makes AI-made packages *legible* to underwriters, juries and lessors. The FAA does not need to change its rules for insurers and lessors to adopt it.
3. **A lessor residual-value warranty or "PMA-neutral return" guarantee.** Insure the lessor against value loss at lease end from PMA/DER content. This removes the biggest commercial gate, and it is pricing that nobody does with data today.
4. **Continuous post-entry-into-service monitoring as a product.** Automated 21.3 reporting and early warning from operator maintenance data, so the holder catches problems before an AD. That shortens the claim tail and becomes the key underwriting input.
5. **Litigation-grade evidence packaging.** Design every package to survive a Daubert challenge and a jury, with reproducible simulation, physical test anchors and human sign-off logs. For DLA, build the record that satisfies Boyle prongs 1-3 and FCA conformance proof, so the government contractor defense actually holds.
6. **Change attempts on law (long shots):**
   - Seek P.L. 85-804-style indemnity or a statutory safe harbor for DLA-approved reverse-engineered parts on obsolete platforms, framed as fixing a readiness and sole-source cost problem.
   - Seek a GARA-like repose for PMA parts on legacy fleets.
   - Both are congressional asks, multi-year and uncertain.

## 6. AI plays on the verification side (from this step)

- **Underwrite rather than just engineer.** An AI that predicts failure probability and grounding cost per part becomes the pricing engine for an MGA. The data flywheel (every package, every test, every service event) is the moat, and the insurance float and fees are revenue.
- **A verification and certification lab that issues an insurable "evidence grade"** on any PMA/DER/TDP package. It sells to insurers, lessors, airlines, DLA, and incumbents like HEICO.
- **A post-certification surveillance SaaS.** Fleet data mining for PMA holders and operators that feeds 21.3 reporting, ADs and underwriting.
- **Bundle warranty and insurance with the approval factory's parts.** "Every part ships with $X liability, grounding cover and a lessor value guarantee." That neutralizes OEM fear, uncertainty and doubt, and a startup would otherwise lose to HEICO on trust.

## 7. Surprises

- FAA approval gives essentially zero tort immunity (Sikkelee 2016/2018), and the FAA itself is immune (Varig). The "permission" from the regulator does not carry liability with it. The insurer is the hidden co-signer of every approval.
- GARA's rolling provision means a new PMA part on an old GA aircraft creates *new* 18-year exposure.
- HEICO's roughly 20,000-PMA book with no disclosed material product-liability litigation suggests the real risk is low. Insurance pricing reflects trust and track record more than measured risk, which leaves room for evidence-based pricing.
- On the military side, the more dangerous liability is the False Claims Act over conformance evidence, not tort. That is a verification problem by definition.
