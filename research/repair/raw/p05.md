## Findings

### Military (strongest hard data)
1. **[VERIFIED] DLA secondary inventory, FY2006-2008 average (GAO-10-469).** About $13.7B a year in total. About $7.1B (52%) sat beyond the requirements objective. About $5.1B (37%) was not needed even for the requirements objective plus 2 years of forecast demand. Of that $5.1B, about $4.1B was kept as "retention stock", for contingencies or because keeping it was cheaper than disposing of it. GAO named inaccurate demand forecasting as a cause. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-10-469
2. **[VERIFIED] Air Force (GAO-07-232, FY2002-2005).** More than half of on-hand secondary inventory was not needed to support requirements. The Air Force's rebuttal is a useful long-tail statistic in its own right: items with *no recurring demand* were "less than 17% of the total inventory dollar value" and under 20% of assets. Demand fell for reasons including mission changes, reliability improvements and modifications. That is structural demand uncertainty, not a knowledge gap. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-07-232
3. **[VERIFIED] Army (GAO-09-199, FY2004-2007).** About $16.3B a year on hand. About $3.6B a year (22%) exceeded current requirements, while *deficits* of about $3.5B a year existed at the same time. This is the classic spare-parts paradox: too much of the wrong parts and too little of the right ones, at roughly equal dollar values. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-09-199
4. **[VERIFIED] DOD-wide (GAO-12-493).** $8.4B of on-hand excess (Sept 2010) and $940M of on-order excess. Better planning cut on-order excess to 5.5% of on-order by FY2010, against a 2014 target. The on-hand excess target was 10% (FY2012-14), stepping to 8% by FY2016. About $710M of cost avoidance came in FY2010. This shows that *planning discipline and policy* recover hundreds of millions, single-digit to low-double-digit percentages, not orders of magnitude. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-12-493
5. **[VERIFIED] F-35 (GAO-19-321).** Aircraft were unable to fly nearly 30% of the time from May to November 2018 because of spare-parts shortages. There was a backlog of about 4,300 parts awaiting repair, and 44% of the parts bought for a Marine Corps deployment did not fit the aircraft deployed. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-321
6. **[VERIFIED] F-35 (GAO-23-105341).** Fleet mission-capable rate was about 55% in March 2023. More than 10,000 components were waiting for depot repair. Life-cycle cost is over $1.7T, of which $1.3T is operating and sustainment. The services must take over sustainment management from the prime by October 2027. The binding constraint is depot repair capacity and the supply pipeline, and (per other GAO F-35 work, not re-fetched here) government lack of technical data. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
7. **[VERIFIED] DOD additive manufacturing (GAO-16-56, 2015).** The challenges officials cited were "qualifying materials and certifying parts". DOD did not systematically track its additive manufacturing work. The reported savings were small ("thousands of dollars" on a mask prototype). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-56. GAO's 2015 AM forum (GAO-15-505SP) concluded that AM "will complement conventional manufacturing rather than replace it", with quality control as the key challenge. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-15-505sp

### Federal infrastructure (obsolescence: the long tail running out)
8. **[VERIFIED] FAA (cited in the GAO 2025 High-Risk report, GAO-25-107743).** A 2023 FAA assessment found 51 of 138 air traffic control systems (37%) "unsustainable" and another 54 (39%) potentially unsustainable, citing "significant shortages in spare parts". https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107743

### Airlines / aviation
9. **[VERIFIED] IATA (Dec 2024 outlook).** About 5,000 aircraft (14% of the 35,166-aircraft fleet) were parked, about 1,600 more than before the pandemic. About 700 (2% of the fleet) were parked for engine inspections. Narrow-body lease rates were 20-30% above 2019. IATA names supply-chain issues as driving up maintenance and leasing costs. https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2024-releases/2024-12-10-02/
10. **[RECALLED, NOT VERIFIED] IATA/Oliver Wyman supply-chain study (late 2025).** Supply-chain problems were estimated to cost airlines more than $11B in 2025. A component was roughly $4.2B of *additional inventory holding cost*, because airlines hoard spare parts to buffer unreliable lead times. The other buckets were excess fuel burn from older aircraft, extra maintenance and engine leasing. If accurate, it shows long-tail inventory as an *insurance premium against supplier unreliability*, not an information problem. Check before citing.
11. **[VERIFIED] Oliver Wyman MRO forecast 2024-2034.** MRO was $104B in 2024, projected to reach $124B by 2034. The constraints cited are raw-material and labor shortages and OEM production shortfalls. https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2024/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2024-2034.html

### Auto OEMs / general aftermarket
12. **[VERIFIED via secondary; original paywalled] Cohen, Agrawal & Agrawal, "Winning in the Aftermarket" (HBR, May 2006).** As much as 23% of service-parts inventory may become obsolete every year. After-sales is a low share of revenue but a high share of profit. https://hbr.org/2006/05/winning-in-the-aftermarket (figure quoted via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_parts_pricing). [RECALLED] The same article reports that aftermarket goods and services are roughly 8% of US GDP, that companies earn about 25% of revenue but a far larger share of profit from the aftermarket, and that typical OEM parts fill rates are mediocre despite heavy inventory.
13. **[RECALLED, NOT VERIFIED] Auto OEM service-parts SKU structure.** Practitioner literature (Syncron, BCG/Deloitte aftermarket studies) commonly reports that OEM parts catalogs run to hundreds of thousands to millions of SKUs, with a majority (often 50-70%+) slow-moving or intermittent and a small minority (about 20%) of SKUs carrying about 80% of volume. OEMs typically must stock parts for 10-15+ years after end of production (EU/legal and contractual obligations), which forces "final/lifetime buys". Lifetime buys are a major source of write-offs. Deutsche Bahn and Daimler Buses have publicised 3D-printing of hard-to-get or obsolete spares (DB counted thousands of printed parts and tens of thousands of printed units by the early 2020s). These are real cases, but they cover mostly non-safety-critical plastic and metal parts.

### Utilities / process industry
14. **[RECALLED, NOT VERIFIED] MRO stores in utilities, oil & gas and mining.** Practitioner rules of thumb (Life Cycle Engineering, SAP/IBM Maximo consultants, EPRI critical-spares guidance) say 20-60% of MRO SKUs show no movement over 2-5 years. Annual carrying cost is commonly put at 15-25%+ of inventory value. Critical "insurance spares" (transformers, large pump and turbine parts, nuclear-qualified parts) are held deliberately despite near-zero demand, because a stockout costs vastly more than carrying. Nuclear plants face additional obsolescence because replacement parts need commercial-grade dedication or 10 CFR 50 Appendix B QA. This is an inspection and certification bottleneck, not a knowledge one. No primary source was fetched this session.

### Spare-parts planning research (academic)
15. **[RECALLED, NOT VERIFIED] Forecasting literature.**
   - Croston (1972, ORQ) introduced separate smoothing of demand size and interval for intermittent demand.
   - Syntetos & Boylan (2001, 2005) showed Croston is biased and proposed the SBA correction. Syntetos, Boylan & Croston (2005, JORS) gave the ADI/CV² classification (smooth / erratic / intermittent / lumpy) used across industry.
   - Boylan & Syntetos' reviews and Bacchetti & Saccani (2012, Omega, "Spare parts classification and demand forecasting for stock control: investigating the gap between research and practice") report a large gap between research and practice. Companies mostly use simple methods and ABC classification. Better forecasting methods improve service/inventory trade-offs at the margin (typically tens of percent of inventory for the same service level), and gains are bounded because lumpy demand is close to unforecastable from history alone.
   - Research on condition-based and installed-base forecasting (e.g., Dekker, van Jaarsveld et al. at Erasmus; "installed base" information) finds that sensor and installed-base data improve forecasts mainly for wear-out parts. Random-failure parts remain Poisson-like.
   - Sherbrooke's METRIC (1968, RAND/USAF) and its successors (multi-echelon repairable inventory) show that optimising the *system* (where to put the stock, plus repair turnaround) beats per-item optimisation substantially. The USAF, the US Navy and airlines (pooling, e.g. "power-by-the-hour" pools) have used these for decades. Knowledge of *how* to plan spares is old and well developed.

## Reasoning and how this bears on the hypotheses
- **The long tail is real and big.** Across DoD services, 22-52% of secondary inventory by value has been "beyond requirements". Items with no recurring demand alone are about 17% of Air Force inventory value. Practitioner figures of 23% obsolete per year in the aftermarket (Cohen 2006) and 20-60% of MRO SKUs non-moving fit with this.
- **The cause is mostly not a knowledge monopoly.** The excess comes from:
  - (a) intrinsically unforecastable, lumpy, random-failure demand
  - (b) deliberate insurance against catastrophic downtime
  - (c) long, unreliable supplier lead times and single-source parts, which push operators to hoard (IATA's inventory holding cost)
  - (d) lifetime buys forced by obsolescence and legal stocking obligations
  - (e) requirements changes (mission changes, reliability upgrades)
  
  Shortages exist alongside the excess (Army: $3.6B excess vs $3.5B deficit; F-35: 30% of time grounded for parts). This is an allocation and lead-time problem.
- **Where knowledge is a factor, it is about IP and data rights, not know-how.** The F-35 case is government lack of technical data and dependence on the prime. That is a legal and contractual monopoly, which AI does not dissolve.
- **AI and better analytics help at the margin.** Documented planning improvements (DoD's on-order excess falling from 12.6% to 6.2% and then 5.5%) are tens of percent, not 10x. Forecasting research says lumpy demand has an irreducible-uncertainty floor. The plausible big lever is *changing the physics of the tail*: make on demand (AM/CNC), pool inventory across operators, and shorten repair turnaround (METRIC logic). That is not *knowing more*.
- **Bespoke making.** The binding constraint GAO found in AM is qualifying materials and certifying parts. Replacement parts in aviation, nuclear and defense need data rights plus qualification (FAA PMA/DER, NRC commercial-grade dedication, military first-article testing). So C5 is contradicted for regulated parts. It is plausible for non-critical parts (the Deutsche Bahn examples).

## Surprises
- Shortages and excess appear together at roughly equal dollar value (Army, GAO-09-199).
- Much "excess" is held *on purpose* as retention stock because disposing of it costs more than keeping it (DLA: $4.1B of $5.1B).
- Obsolescence of the tail is now itself a readiness crisis: 76% of FAA ATC systems are unsustainable or potentially unsustainable, citing spare-parts shortages.
- Airline inventory carrying cost is rising *because* suppliers are unreliable, so hoarding is a rational response to the supply side.
