## Findings

### F1. History: insurance-plus-inspection was invented to solve a failure and knowledge problem (history)
- Hartford Steam Boiler was founded on 30 June 1866, "conceived at a time when catastrophic boiler explosions were common events, occurring about once every four days" in the US. Its model was "Guaranteed Steam Boiler Inspection": the insurer inspects, and insures only what it inspects. It required the "Hartford Loop" piping configuration on insured boilers (1919). Today it employs 1,200+ engineers, inspectors and technicians, about 50% of its workforce. It has been owned by Munich Re since 2009 and is rated A++ by A.M. Best. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartford_Steam_Boiler_Inspection_and_Insurance_Company
- TÜV started the same way, in the same year. After a brewery boiler explosion in Mannheim in 1865, boiler owners founded the "Society for the Supervision and Insurance of Steam Boilers" in January 1866. From 1871, membership exempted owners from state inspection. Today TÜV SÜD has about €2.7bn in sales and 25,000+ staff (2021), and TÜV Rheinland about €1.97bn (2017). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%9CV
- The Manchester Steam Users' Association (1855) preferred trained inspectors to regulation. Codes followed disasters: the UK Boiler Explosions Act (1882), the Grover Shoe Factory explosion in 1905 (58 dead), Massachusetts boiler rules (1911) and the ASME Boiler Code (1915). 122 of 137 recorded British locomotive boiler explosions (1815-1962) happened in the 19th century. Inspection records show "simple rusting" (corrosion) as the leading cause, at 2-5x the rate of every other cause. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiler_explosion
- Lloyd's Register was formed in 1760 by the underwriters and merchants at Lloyd's coffee house, to grade ship condition for insurers (the "A1" rating dates from 1775-76). A ship "in class" meets the rules, and that decides whether it can be insured. Changes to structure or machinery must be approved before they are made. LR has about 3,600 staff. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd%27s_Register
- FM (Factory Mutual, 1835) was formed by mill owners. In its model, "risk and premiums are determined by engineering analysis" instead of actuarial history, and its premise is that "property losses can be prevented or mitigated". It runs a 1,600-acre research campus. Its self-reported 2024 figures: $26bn surplus, $1.4bn in membership credits returned, and a "$1.1 trillion reduction in total loss expectancy" across clients. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FM_Global (citing the FM annual report)
- Reading: the modern inspection and certification industry was created by insurers to fill a knowledge gap. Owners could not judge their own boilers or ships. The rulebook (codes, class rules) and the inspectors came from insurers. This is the historical analog of the founder's C2 and C6: knowledge was centralised into third-party inspector-insurers, which reduced failures dramatically. It was not just rent-seeking.

### F2. Insurers explicitly prefer OEMs because the OEM holds the design knowledge, which reinforces the "knowledge monopoly" (practitioner_view, IMIA WGP 111 (18) "Ageing Plant and Maintenance", 2018)
- Quote: "OEM's are preferred by insurers due to their inherent design knowledge of the equipment, and their experience of knowledge gained from maintaining equipment for many customers and over many locations."
- "In underwriter's point of view, LTSA provided by OEM is the best option since the equipment in question is best known by OEM. However... for aged equipment the OEM may not be available."
- Policy wording example: cover is issued on the insured's statement that "operating, control and maintenance specifications described on OEM's manuals have been absolutely followed", otherwise "the insurer will have the right to reject the loss".
- After a loss, "the appointed loss adjuster must prove that in-house maintenance has been done properly and professionally in total compliance with OEM's manuals."
- Reading: insurers write OEM manuals into the contract and use them as the test of compliance. That is a structural lock-in mechanism behind C2. The incentive is uncertainty about non-OEM work, not deliberate inefficiency.
- Source: https://www.imia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/MIA-WGP-111-18-Ageing-Plant-and-Maintenance.c.pdf

### F3. The knowledge gap is real, and it is tacit and human: skills, retirements, lost manuals (practitioner_view, IMIA WGP 111)
- "Frequently equipment breakdowns and ensuing business interruption losses can be attributed to human error due to the lack of skill".
- "Many original manufacturer's parts and operating manuals do not survive... much of the practical knowledge and skills resides with the operators and technicians... when they retire, there is a real risk that their skills, experience and memories leave with them."
- Operators develop an "ear" for abnormal sound or vibration, and this "can help detect and prevent larger problems".
- A Morgan Stanley survey, cited in the paper, found most North American industrial equipment is over 10 years old on average: "Not since 1938 have North American factories worked with such outdated equipment."
- Reading: this supports C2, but the gap is often undocumented knowledge (lost manuals, retired staff), not knowledge locked behind an OEM. AI trained on public text cannot recover knowledge that was never written down. This partly contradicts C3. Capturing tacit operator knowledge with sensors and AI is a plausible wedge.

### F4. Bespoke and reverse-engineered parts: insurers see high risk and say it is slow and costly, the opposite of "solved" (practitioner_view, IMIA WGP 111 and WGP 64)
- WGP 111 says bespoke manufacture "can be an expensive and time-consuming option... it may be the only option". It lists four problems: "Lack of original drawings. Lack of detailed specifications covering things like material qualities, tolerances, etc. Lack of availability of original materials or manufacturing processes. Time required to produce manufacturing drawings and find an engineering company capable of manufacture."
- "There can be very high risks in using non-original parts, largely due to the lack of original design information. The potential for repeat failures or cascading damage may be significantly increased."
- On obsolete equipment: parts "may not be readily available... This can increase Business Interruption costs substantially." Faced with an obsolete critical machine, the underwriter's "first and easiest option is to decline the risk".
- WGP 64 (09) "Combustion Turbines: Critical Losses and Trends" says reverse-engineered model quality "is highly dependent on the measurement techniques, data approximation errors, numerical methods, wear and tear of the object and 'frozen-in' errors". It adds that "technical expertise and experience in the field of combustion turbine development and testing is absolutely essential."
- The counterpoint, which supports C5 in part: a reverse-engineered combustion transition piece with changed material, sealing and cooling achieved "a much higher lifetime expectation"; "non-OEM spare parts can, under certain circumstances, be better than the original ones."
- Also: "reverse engineered parts of dubious quality have found their way into machines only to fail... Such failures invariably result in catastrophic events and very high insurance claims."
- Reading: the bottleneck for bespoke parts is not making the shape. It is recovering the material spec and tolerances, and validating and qualifying the part, plus insurer acceptance. C5 is contradicted for safety-critical parts. It is plausible for non-critical or low-stress parts.
- Sources: WGP 111 as above; https://www.imia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/IMIA-WGP-64-Combustion-Turbines-FINALa-4.pdf

### F5. OEM contracts deliberately control parts, failure evidence and knowledge (practitioner_view, IMIA WGP 64)
- "Where Owners use non-OEM spare parts, the manufacturers can cancel warranties and service agreements." "The intended usage of ISPs [independent service providers] may provoke the OEM to threaten the Owner with cancellation of warranties and service agreements."
- On parts and evidence: "With a service agreement in place the parts may automatically belong to the OEM. Insurers might not be able to inspect the damaged part and recover any salvage value." "These agreements can restrict the accessibility of the offending hardware and limit the usefulness of any analysis."
- "A RCA team comprised only of the OEM's personnel and its contractors should not be accepted by insurers or their adjusters - the OEM has commercial issues that cannot be overcome."
- "Most of the agreements between the Owner and OEM are kept confidential and underwriters might not be aware of the full scope".
- "Owners are mostly responsible on their own for verification and assessment of the quality of non-OEM service providers and non-OEM spare parts suppliers. There is no independent organisation to assist the Owner. Operators of smaller fleets are currently mainly dependent on the support of the OEMs".
- "Insurers are increasingly in the hands of the manufacturers for assurance that operation and maintenance is being carried out correctly." "The level of understanding required to make adjustments and interpret data is often beyond the capabilities of the site engineer or a third party maintenance provider." Remote OEM monitoring centres often identify faults "before the site becomes aware of a problem."
- Reading: this is strong insurer-side evidence for C2 and C7. Control of the hardware, the data and the root-cause analysis sits with OEMs, and insurers themselves complain about it. Note the explicit gap: "no independent organisation" to qualify non-OEM parts and service providers. That is a company-shaped hole.

### F6. Failure registers are missing: insurers lack shared failure data (practitioner_view, IMIA WGP 64)
- "Currently there is no comprehensive turbine loss register (despite efforts on the part of certain brokers to maintain statistics) and Insurers & reinsurers may feel that such a register should be set up and made publicly available."
- Root-cause assignment is contested: "insurers' representatives, manufacturers and operators may have divergent views". Failure analysis covers different levels of effort ("damage mechanism", "cause", "root cause") with "different levels of effort and cost".
- EPRI data cited in the paper: F-class gas turbine failures by section are compressor 35%, combustor 29% and turbine blading 28%. Newer models: turbine rotating blades 42%, stationary parts 21%, bearings 14%.
- Prototype risk: insurers "want to avoid subsidising the OEM's R&D programs by paying for the consequences of failures"; "the first 2000 hours of operation seems critical".
- Reading: the diagnosis and understanding steps are contested for commercial reasons, not only for lack of knowledge. A neutral failure-data layer would help, but data access is the hard part (C3 is weakened).

### F7. Detection is often not the bottleneck: alarms were ignored or deferred (practitioner_view, IMIA WGP 64 loss examples)
- Case 7.2: a large-frame gas turbine at a refinery ran with a high exhaust temperature spread. "The exhaust spreads were in alarm, but were ignored until a better time could be planned for a shutdown to make the inspection." The result was blade high-cycle fatigue failure, a loss of over $12m with no compressor damage. The root cause was blocked fuel nozzles from poor-quality fuel oil.
- Case 7.1: a history of high combustion dynamics led to a fuel nozzle tip release and cascading turbine damage. Property damage was over $15m.
- Reading: the "notice" step was done (the alarm was there). The failure was in the decision, an operational and economic trade-off. This challenges a clean linear C1 and suggests that notice-to-act decision support with economic framing matters.

### F8. Electrical failures: mostly mundane, human-caused, and detectable with existing tools (practitioner_view and data, IMIA WGP 110 (18) "Electrical Failures")
- "75% of all electrical system failures can be traced to human error—from carelessness and improperly trained personnel to inadequate maintenance and delayed service."
- "A major insurance carrier estimates that 25% of all electrical failures originate from improper connections... a loose connection can be detected using infrared thermography, whilst the system is in service."
- Transformers that failed from insulation deterioration averaged 17.8 years old against an expected life of 35-40 years (20 years in 1983). In the 1998 HSB transformer study, only a small share of claims came from poor workmanship or manufacturer defects.
- IEEE statistics: preventive maintenance can cut the risk of unscheduled outage "by as much as 66%".
- HSB (2015) maxim: keep electrical systems "clean, cool, dry and tight."
- Loss example: a generator step-up transformer explosion in Asia. Replacement cost was $12,000,000 plus $3,000,000 for low-voltage connection inspection and repair. "The cause of GSUT bushing failure has not been able to be identified from the above data so a deep OEM bushing and transformer investigation was required."
- Reading: the knowledge of what to do (thermography, cleaning, tightening) is widely published. The gap is execution and discipline, not knowledge. That contradicts C3's premise that knowledge is the binding constraint. Where diagnosis is hard (the bushing failure), it still routes back to the OEM.
- Source: https://www.imia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/IMIA-WGP-110-18-Electrical-Failures-renumbered.c.pdf

### F9. Business interruption, not repair parts, dominates large-loss cost; spares strategy is the main lever (data and practitioner_view)
- Allianz Risk Barometer 2026: business interruption (including supply chain) is ranked #3, down from #2. It has been #1 or #2 for 15 years. Only 3% of respondents say their supply chains are "very resilient". Allianz analysed 7,888 BI claims worth about €6.74bn over five years (about €0.85m average, my calculation). Source: https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/reports/allianz-risk-barometer.html
- Allianz Global Claims Review: fire and explosion is 21% of claim value (>€18bn, >12,000 claims, average about €1.5m). Faulty workmanship and maintenance is 9% of value and 7% of count, the third-largest cause by value and the second most frequent. The CCO says "It is now much more expensive to repair or rebuild damaged property" and that materials are often "simply unavailable due to logistics, shipping and supply-chain bottlenecks." Source: https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/reports/global-claims-review.html
- IMIA WGP 64: "it does not matter where the spare parts originate OEM or non-OEM parts might reduce a BI loss... [but] Insurers/Reinsurers are not keen to pay for repairs and parts which might cause future severe damage". Utilities that hold spare parts or whole spare GT/generator sets would see insurance premiums for BI "significantly reduced"; "smaller fleets of identical combustion turbines could share a pool of spare parts"; "For insurers/reinsurers the expense of buying, holding and managing spare parts on their own would tie up too much capital."
- IMIA WGP 111: obsolete-part delays "increase Business Interruption costs substantially." In one case study, a plant was shut for "over 4 months".
- Reading: for heavy equipment, the money is in downtime, and downtime is driven by lead times on large, long-lead items (rotors, transformers, switchgear) and by qualifying alternatives. A shared spares pool financed or recognised by insurers is a named but unbuilt idea.

### F10. Moral hazard and the "soft market" weaken maintenance incentives (practitioner_view, IMIA WGP 111)
- "Some equipment owners demonstrate a total disregard for maintenance, looking to their insurance policies to cover the risk of failure. In soft markets the price of a Machinery Breakdown (MB) policy may be cheaper than the cost of a maintenance agreement".
- For obsolete equipment, "a total loss claim might deliberately arise".
- Reading: some inefficiency is incentive-driven (insurance pricing cycles, deferral of capex), which supports a version of C7. Insurance can sometimes subsidise poor maintenance.

### F11. Insurers are moving to sensors and IoT, but the reported evidence is thin (news and oem_view)
- HSB's IoT programme: Meshify Slim water and freeze sensors on Amazon Sidewalk (May 2025), Flume flow-based leak detection (March 2025), and Samsung SmartThings "Smart Home Savings" (January 2026), where connected appliances earn premium credits. The Florida pilot reported only "meaningful premium reductions for many", with no numbers published.
- Sources: https://www.munichre.com/hsb/en/press-and-publications/press-releases/2025/2025-05-08-iot-slim-sensors-amazon-sidewalk.html ; https://www.munichre.com/hsb/en/press-and-publications/press-releases/2026/2026-01-05-samsung-electronics-collaborates-with-hartford-steam-boiler-hsb-to-introduce-smart-home-savings.html
- Gas turbine OEM remote monitoring catches faults before the site does (WGP 64). The insurer view: the "notice" step is already being automated, but the data belongs to OEMs.
- Reading: insurers are a natural buyer and distribution channel for detection tech (premium credits). Published proof of loss reduction is mostly missing, so evidence is a gap.

### F12. Inspection body for boilers (data, National Board)
- The National Board (NBBI) publishes engineering background. One example: a 60°F rise in tube-metal temperature can cut creep life by about 90%, and steamside scale can raise tube-metal temperatures by 25-75°F. The ASME Code uses 1% creep over 100,000 hours as an allowable-stress criterion. Source: https://www.nationalboard.org/index.aspx?pageID=181
- Reading: the physics knowledge has long been codified and public. The work is in measuring the actual condition, which is an inspection problem.

## Synthesis against the hypotheses
- C1 (one chain): mixed. Insurers' own frame is lifecycle risk management, not a linear chain: maintenance strategy, obsolescence management, spares holding, root-cause analysis and indemnity basis. Notice is often done but not acted on (F7). Root-cause analysis is a contested negotiation (F5, F6). Spares positioning happens before the failure (F9). The chain is a fair description of corrective repair, but much of the value sits outside it (prevention, pre-positioned spares).
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): supports, with nuance. Insurers say outright that expertise "emanates from the OEMs" and they prefer OEMs. OEM contracts control parts, data and root-cause analysis, and there is "no independent organisation" to qualify alternatives. But much of the knowledge gap is lost tacit knowledge (retirements, lost manuals) and poor execution (75% of electrical failures are human error), not monopoly.
- C3 (AI erases the gap): contradicts, mostly. The missing knowledge is (a) proprietary design data (materials, tolerances), (b) undocumented tacit knowledge, and (c) contested failure evidence the OEM may physically own. None of these is in AI training data. Also, the public knowledge (thermography, "clean, cool, dry, tight") already exists and failures persist anyway. AI can help with triage and documentation, but liability and acceptance still require a qualified party.
- C4 (10x cost drop in the four middle steps): mixed, leaning contradicts for heavy equipment. The insurer view puts the dominant cost in business interruption downtime and the physical repair or replacement of long-lead items. Diagnosis or "understanding" is rarely named as the cost driver, except where root-cause analysis is slowed by OEM control. Sourcing and delivery delays are named as major (obsolescence, supply chain), but they are physical-capacity limits, not information limits.
- C5 (bespoke parts solved): contradicts for critical parts. Insurers list missing drawings, specs, materials and processes, plus the risk of repeat and cascading failure. Reverse engineering is possible and sometimes better than OEM, but it needs deep testing expertise.
- C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports. Insurers built the inspection industry (HSB, TÜV, LR, FM) and still underwrite on inspection and compliance with OEM manuals. They are moving to IoT, but published outcome data is thin. Evolution is underway but slow.
- C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed. Some structures entrench inefficiency (OEM warranty-voiding, confidential LTSAs, parts ownership, soft-market moral hazard, no loss register). But the inspection-insurance system was deliberately built for safety and did cut explosion rates dramatically. "Inefficiency" is partly the price of safety assurance and liability allocation.

## Company-shape ideas the evidence points at
1. An independent qualification and certification body or platform for non-OEM parts and service providers (IMIA: "There is no independent organisation to assist the Owner"). Insurer acceptance is the moat.
2. An insurer-backed shared critical-spares pool or registry (GT rotors, transformers, switchgear), priced against BI premium reductions (IMIA WGP 64 names this idea explicitly).
3. A neutral failure and loss register with root-cause data, cross-OEM, sold to insurers and reinsurers (IMIA: no comprehensive turbine loss register).
4. Tacit-knowledge capture for ageing plants: sensors plus AI capturing what retiring operators "hear", plus digitising lost manuals, sold through engineering insurers.
5. Reverse-engineering plus qualification for obsolete parts, with the validation dossier being the product, not the printing. Start with low-criticality parts.
