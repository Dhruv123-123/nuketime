## Findings

### F1. FTC: "scant evidence" for manufacturers' repair-restriction justifications (regulator, verified)
- Source: FTC, *Nixing the Fix: An FTC Report to Congress on Repair Restrictions*, May 2021. https://www.ftc.gov/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions (PDF: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf)
- Quote: "repair restrictions have diluted the effectiveness of Section 102(c) and steered consumers into manufacturers' repair networks or to replace products before the end of their useful lives ... there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions."
- The fixes repair advocates ask for ("access to information, manuals, spare parts, and tools") are "well supported" by the record. The report says the auto industry has expanded consumer choice and "other industries ... have not followed suit."
- The FTC quotes U.S. PIRG: repair could cut household spending on electronics and appliances by 22%, about $330 per family per year. This is a PIRG estimate, not FTC data.
- Relevance: strong support for C2 (gatekeeping of parts, information and tools steers consumers to OEM networks or to replacement). Note that the restriction is on *access*, not on knowledge that no one else has.

### F2. AHAM's counter-claim: appliance service is already mostly local independents (industry view, verified via FTC)
- Same FTC report, p. ~45. AHAM surveyed its members: "Of the 20,000 servicers (repairers), over 87 percent are authorized local independent brand/manufacturer affiliated service providers ... the 'barriers' to repair for independent repair shops are imaginary."
- AHAM and GE Appliances safety arguments: microwave capacitors stay charged after unplugging; technicians work inside homes, so manufacturers require background checks and drug screening; untrained technicians risk "improper diagnosis, use of incorrect or substandard parts or even, unknowingly, counterfeit parts."
- AHAM also says servicing smart appliances means touching chipsets and firmware, which creates cybersecurity exposure.
- Relevance: contradicts a simple "OEM monopoly" reading of C2 for appliances. The labor is largely done by small independent firms, but under OEM authorization: the OEM controls training, service software, warranty-claim reimbursement and parts terms. The "monopoly" is a gate on access and pay rates, not on who holds the wrench. Also relevant to C6: the industry already uses vetting and traceability (background checks, repair tracking) as a form of certification.

### F3. EU ecodesign spare-parts windows (regulation; core facts verified via FTC, details [BG])
- Verified (FTC p. 48): the EU ecodesign rules for home appliances took effect 1 March 2021.
  - Refrigerators: parts for at least 7 years (door gaskets 10).
  - Washing machines and washer-dryers: at least 10 years.
  - Dishwashers: at least 10 years (7 for some professional-only parts).
  - Electronic displays: at least 7 years.
- Some washer parts must be sold to end users: door, hinge and seals, door lock, and plastic peripherals such as detergent dispensers. All others go only to "professional repairers."
- Parts must be replaceable with commonly available tools, without permanent damage. The parts list and ordering procedure must be on a free public website.
- [BG] Reg. (EU) 2019/2023 Annex II details: parts must be delivered within 15 working days of the order. Repair and maintenance information goes to *registered* professional repairers. Manufacturers may delay parts and information availability until 2 years after the first unit is placed on the market. Indicative prices of parts must be published.
- [BG] Reg. 2019/2023: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/2023/oj (fetch returned empty).
- Relevance: regulators treat *parts availability and delivery time* as the binding constraint and set hard windows: 7 to 10 years of availability, 15-day delivery. A 15-working-day ceiling means multi-week waits are normal and legal. That is a "deliver parts" bottleneck. Keeping some parts professional-only keeps a certification-style layer (C6).

### F4. EU Right to Repair Directive 2024/1799 (regulation, verified via Wikipedia summary)
- Adopted 30 May 2024; transposition deadline 31 July 2026. This means it becomes national law in member states around now.
- Manufacturers of covered products (which include washing machines, dishwashers, fridges and others under ecodesign) must offer repair "within a reasonable time" and "for a reasonable price." Contract clauses and hardware or software techniques that hinder repair are banned unless objectively justified.
- Other provisions: a voluntary European Repair Information Form, valid at least 30 days; a 1-year extension of the legal guarantee if the consumer picks repair; a European online repair platform (interface by July 2027, operating by January 2028).
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_repair (summarising Directive (EU) 2024/1799).
- Relevance: the EU's diagnosis is that *price and time* of repair, plus discoverability of repairers, are the problems. The platform is a matchmaking and discovery layer, which is a direct analog to the founder's sourcing thesis.

### F5. Eurobarometer: 77% would rather repair, but cost and service push them to replace (survey, verified via EEB)
- Source: EEB, *Coolproducts don't cost the earth*, Sept 2019. https://eeb.org/library/coolproducts-report/ (PDF https://eeb.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Coolproducts-report.pdf)
- "77% of EU citizens would rather repair their goods than to buy new ones, but ultimately have to replace or discard them because they are discouraged by the cost of repairs and the level of service provided."
- Lifetimes are falling. EEB cites Prakash et al. on the causes: "deficient mechanical and electronic robustness," "software-induced reasons," "high cost of repair," and the desire for new functions (for example, moving house).
- Relevance: the stated barrier is *cost of repair and service quality*, not lack of knowledge. This partly contradicts C2's framing. It supports a cost and labor-economics framing. Desire for new features (replacing working products) is a demand-side factor no repair technology fixes.

### F6. Environmental case for repair: washing machines (study, verified)
- Same EEB report. The typical EU washing machine lasts 11 years. To offset production, distribution and disposal emissions, it should last 25 to 40 years, or 17 to 23 years even with 5% a year efficiency gains in new models.
- Production and other non-use phases are 18 to 31% of a washing machine's lifetime greenhouse-gas impact.
- A 1-year life extension of all EU washing machines saves 0.25 Mt CO2 a year by 2030; 3 years saves 0.66 Mt; 5 years saves about 1 Mt.
- Across washers, notebooks, vacuums and phones, a 1-year extension saves about 4 Mt CO2 a year, which EEB equates to about 2 million cars off the road.
- Cites Öko-Institut 2018: "with very few exceptions, it is always better for the environment to have faulty home appliances repaired."
- Relevance: the social value of repair is larger than the private value, so private repair-vs-replace decisions are biased toward replacement. This is a market-failure argument loosely aligned with C7. The inefficiency is less "built in" by industry than a missing externality price.

### F7. Design for non-repair: sealed drums and bundled spare parts (study and examples, verified via Wikipedia)
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planned_obsolescence, citing Cooper et al. 2014.
- In front-loading washers, the drum bearing is often moulded into the drum, or the outer tub is sealed, so a bearing fix means replacing the whole drum. The repair can then cost more than the machine is worth, and the machine is scrapped.
- Bosch MaxoMixx blender: a plastic latch is designed to break; the latch is not sold separately; the spare is the whole drive, priced near a new device.
- France (2015 law): makers must declare how long spare parts will be available.
- Relevance: much repair-vs-replace failure is *physical design and parts bundling*, not missing knowledge. AI diagnosis does not unseal a tub. This contradicts C3 and C4 as sufficient conditions. It supports the C5 angle only if someone makes the small part, and here the bundling is deliberate, not a supplier gap.

### F8. Whirlpool: warranty is about 2.1% of sales; refocus on the Americas; no service disclosure (company filing, verified)
- Source: Whirlpool 10-K for FY2025, filed 2026-02-11. https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/106640/000010664026000009/whr-20251231.htm
- "warranty expense as a percentage of consolidated net sales approximated 2.1% and 2.4%" (2025 vs 2024).
- Net sales were $15,524M (2025) and $16,607M (2024). That implies about $326M and $399M of warranty expense. This is my calculation.
- The XBRL lists a "spare parts and warranties" revenue category but gives no separate service P&L. Whirlpool does not report repair as a strategic segment.
- Context (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whirlpool_Corporation):
  - 2023: the EMEA business went into the Beko Europe JV (Arçelik 75%).
  - Fiscal 2025 sales fell.
  - Q1 2026 GAAP net loss of $85M.
  - Dividend suspended to prioritise more than $900M of debt reduction.
  - 2026 price rises: 10% in April, 4% planned for July.
  - About $800M of offerings to fund debt paydown and manufacturing automation.
- Relevance: for the largest US appliance OEM, repair is a cost centre (warranty) and not a profit pool it reports. That weakens the idea that OEMs make large monopoly rents on appliance repair. The rent, where it exists, is more likely in parts pricing and in replacement sales. Rising new-appliance prices (tariffs) improve repair-vs-replace economics in 2026.

### F9. Minnesota Digital Fair Repair Act: first US law to cover home appliances (statute, verified)
- Source: Minn. Stat. 325E.72. https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325E.72
- Covers digital electronic equipment sold on or after 1 July 2021 (law effective 1 July 2024). Wikipedia calls it the first US law to address home appliances.
- OEMs must provide documentation (free), parts and tools (including diagnostic software and firmware updates) to independents and owners on "fair and reasonable terms": no higher cost or worse terms than authorised providers get, and no forced authorisation or unnecessary pairing.
- Parts must be available within 60 days of the product's first sale in Minnesota. There is no duty to provide parts the OEM no longer gives its own network.
- Exclusions include work requiring a state contractor licence, which matters for gas and HVAC.
- [BG] California SB 244 (effective 1 July 2024): products priced $50 to $99.99 must have parts, tools and docs for 3 years after manufacture ends; products $100 and up for 7 years. Covers appliances. (leginfo fetch 403.)
- Relevance: US law now codifies the information-and-parts access gap (C2) as real enough to legislate. The "no duty once discontinued" clause leaves the end-of-life parts gap open, which is where a C5-style on-demand part maker would sit.

### F10. Collapse of the in-home national service brand: Sears Home Services (history, partly verified)
- Verified: Sears Home Services was a division doing appliance repair and in-home services. A&E Factory Service was a Whirlpool–Sears joint venture for mobile appliance repair. Sears Holdings filed Chapter 11 in October 2018 and its assets went to Transformco in 2019. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sears_Holdings
- [BG] Sears Home Services was once the largest US appliance repair network, with thousands of technicians. It shrank sharply with Sears' decline, and the OEMs (Whirlpool, GE) moved service to authorised third-party networks and their own in-house fleets.
- Relevance: the biggest national, brand-agnostic repairer did not survive on repair economics alone. That is evidence the appliance repair business is structurally low-margin and fragmented.

### F11. [BG] Unit economics of US appliance repair (practitioner, not verified)
- Typical service-call or diagnostic fee: about $75 to $150, often credited against the repair. Average repair ticket: about $150 to $450. Many machines cost $500 to $1,200 new. (Common in Angi/HomeAdvisor cost guides; the fetch returned 403.)
- Consumer Reports rule of thumb: do not repair if the cost is over 50% of a new product's price and the product is past about half its expected life.
- The call-out fee alone is roughly 10 to 25% of a mid-range washer's price before any part or labor. Parts not on the truck force a second visit.
- Relevance: the fixed cost of a truck roll is the step that kills marginal repairs. AI can cut *diagnosis* cost (remote triage, error-code reading, video diagnosis) and *second visits* (right part on the first trip). But a physical visit is still needed for most repairs, so a 10x cut in total repair cost is unlikely for in-home appliance repair.

### F12. [BG] Technician supply is shrinking (data, not verified)
- BLS Occupational Outlook: about 35,000 to 37,000 US home appliance repairers. Employment is projected flat to slightly declining over 2023 to 2033, with median pay in the mid-$40k range. The page returned 403.
- Trade groups such as United Servicers Association report an ageing workforce and few new entrants.
- Relevance: the scarce input is *trained hands* at a wage the low ticket price can support. That points to the bottleneck being labor cost and density, not knowledge.

### F13. [BG] Samsung and LG service models (practitioner and news, not verified)
- Both run US appliance service mainly through third-party authorised service centres (ASCs) paid flat warranty rates, plus some in-house and dispatched technicians. Independent repairers have long complained that warranty pay rates are low, which limits ASC capacity.
- Consumer complaints and class actions over long waits for parts are recurring, for example Samsung refrigerator ice makers and LG linear-compressor refrigerators.
- Samsung has a self-repair programme with iFixit (from 2022, Galaxy devices first). Coverage of appliances is limited.
- Relevance: Korean OEM appliance service is OEM-gated and capacity-constrained. The wait comes from parts logistics and ASC capacity, not from missing knowledge.

### F14. [BG] German Environment Agency (UBA) / Öko-Institut obsolescence study (study, partly verified)
- Verified: UBA Texte 11/2016 (Prakash et al.) found that first-use lifetimes fell for most product groups. Obsolescence is a "highly complex pattern" of material, functional, psychological and economic causes, with no single main cause. https://www.umweltbundesamt.de/publikationen/einfluss-der-nutzungsdauer-von-produkten-auf-ihre-1
- [BG] The commonly cited figure: the share of large household appliances replaced within 5 years because of a defect rose from about 3.5% (2004) to about 8.3% (2012/13). A large share of replaced appliances still worked.
- Relevance: complex, multi-causal replacement goes against a single-cause story (knowledge monopoly, C2) and against a single fix (AI, C3).

### F15. [BG] Repair subsidies show that price, not knowledge, moves behaviour (policy, not verified)
- Austria's "Reparaturbonus" (2022): covers 50% of repair cost up to €200 per repair. Over a million vouchers were reportedly redeemed.
- France's "Bonus réparation" (end of 2022): a fixed €10 to €60 off at certified QualiRépar repairers. Uptake was initially weak and amounts were raised.
- Relevance: when governments want more repair, they subsidise *labor cost* and certify repairers (C6). Austria's large uptake suggests price is the binding constraint.

## Synthesis

**Where the money and time go in appliance repair:**
- **Truck roll and labor (call-out plus hourly).** This is about 10 to 25% of the device price before any work and is the largest share of a typical ticket.
- **Parts waits and second visits.** The EU legally tolerates 15 working days for parts delivery. US parts pass through OEMs and distributors to authorised servicers. Parts not on the truck mean another visit.
- **Design that sets a high price floor.** Sealed tubs and bundled assemblies raise the cost of the parts themselves.
- **Diagnosis.** It matters, but it is now often guided by error codes and is cheap compared with labor and parts. The stated reasons people don't repair are cost and service quality (Eurobarometer, 77%), not lack of know-how.
- **OEMs don't treat repair as a profit centre.** Whirlpool's warranty cost is about 2.1% of sales. The repair business is fragmented: AHAM counts 20,000 servicers, 87% of them local independents.

**Real bottleneck:**
- Skilled in-home labor and its fixed visit cost, relative to cheap and getting cheaper new appliances.
- Parts logistics and design-driven part bundling.
- OEM gating of service software and parts terms. This is a real but secondary effect, per the FTC.
- Knowledge alone is not the constraint. AI cuts diagnosis and first-time-fix failures, but it does not remove the truck roll or reseal a drum.

**Implications for the founder:**
- In appliances, AI can probably reduce diagnose+understand cost a lot: remote triage, guided DIY for user-serviceable EU-listed parts, and right part on the first visit.
- Sourcing and delivery can improve through aggregation and predictive stocking.
- Combined end-to-end cost dropping 10x looks unlikely, because labor and the visit dominate.
- The more defensible plays are:
  - remote triage and dispatch optimisation that raises first-time fix rate;
  - a parts marketplace or long-tail parts manufacturing for discontinued SKUs (beyond OEM legal windows, which end 7 to 10 years after the last unit);
  - compliance tooling for EU 2024/1799 and US state right-to-repair obligations.
- Appliances are a low-ticket domain. The same thesis likely pays far more in high-downtime-cost assets.
