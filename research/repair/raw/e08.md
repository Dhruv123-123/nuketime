## Findings

### A. Launch vehicle reuse (SpaceX Falcon 9)

1. **Refurbishment cost vs new build (company claims).** In March 2016, Gwynne Shotwell estimated about $1M for refuelling plus about $3M for refurbishing a used first stage, which would allow a launch price near $40M, about 30% below list. In August 2020, Musk said refurbishing a booster costs less than 10% of a new one, that SpaceX breaks even on the second flight, and that it saves money from the third flight on. Reuse costs about 30-40% of payload (propellant margin, legs). Propellant is about 0.3% of vehicle cost. Booster plus fairing are about 80% of launch cost. A fairing is about $6M, roughly 10% of launch cost.
   Source: Wikipedia, "SpaceX reusable launch system development program" (cites SpaceNews, Musk/Shotwell statements). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_reusable_launch_system_development_program. Kind: company statements (practitioner/OEM view), not audited data.

2. **Customers did not share the savings at first.** SES wanted about a $30M price (50% off) before it would take the risk of flying a reused vehicle. A customer's risk premium, meaning trust in the inspection, was the barrier, not the hardware alone. Same source.

3. **Turnaround fell from months to weeks, and cert life from 10 to 40 flights.** The first reflight, B1021 in March 2017, took about 4 months to refurbish and flew a year after its first flight. By 2018 Block 5 was designed for "10 reuses with minimal inspection" and up to 100 with refurbishment. Booster certification rose from 10 to 15, 20 and then 40 flights. The record is B1067 with 37 flights (2026). Landings: 667 of 680 attempts recovered (about 98.1%). 56 boosters have been reflown at least once. Of 58 Block 5 boosters, 34 have been destroyed (25 expended on purpose, 6 failed landings, 3 lost in recovery). One fairing half has flown 40 times. The fairing turnaround record is 9 days 8 hours. SpaceX moved to "wet" fairing recovery after deciding it was cheaper to clean and refurbish fairings than to catch them dry.
   Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_first-stage_boosters ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_reusable_launch_system_development_program. Kind: data (flight records).

4. **What made reuse possible was design for reuse, not better repair knowledge.** The enablers were restartable and throttleable Merlin engines, cold-gas and grid-fin control (titanium grid fins from 2018), landing legs with crush cores, drone ships, and roughly 30% design margin on v1.1 that paid for the recovery experiments. Block 5 was specifically re-engineered to cut refurbishment: reusable heat shielding, better seals and fewer parts to swap. Inspection and test came first. For example, a static fire of the first recovered booster in January 2016 found one engine with thrust fluctuations. Same sources. Kind: history/data.

5. **Counter-example: the Space Shuttle was reusable but uneconomic.** NASA's early estimate was about $9.3M per flight (about $54M in 2011 dollars). Actual averages were about $450M per mission (2011) and about $1.5B per launch over the program's life ($192B over 135 missions). The program had 5,000+ staff and 654 facilities. FY2004-06 non-launch costs were about $13B for 3 launches. Each RS-25 main engine (about $40M) was removed after every flight, inspected, refurbished and requalified on a test stand at Stennis. The planned 24 flights a year never happened. Reuse without design for cheap inspection plus refurbishment produced a standing army. On SLS, NASA now throws the same RS-25 engines away; the RS-25E is designed to be expendable.
   Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_program ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-25. Kind: data/history.

### B. Satellite servicing (life extension, repair, refuelling)

6. **MEV-1 and MEV-2 were the first commercial docking with operational GEO satellites not built to be serviced.** MEV-1 launched in October 2019 and docked with Intelsat 901 on 25 February 2020; IS-901 returned to service on 2 April 2020. MEV-1 undocked on 9 April 2025 after a 5-year mission (about 6.6 years from launch), then went to a new client in May 2025, reported to be Optus D3. MEV-2 docked with Intelsat 10-02 on 12 April 2021. MEV docks mechanically to the liquid apogee engine nozzle and launch adapter ring, which almost every GEO satellite has. It then does stationkeeping for the client with its own thrusters, so the client needs no prepared interface. ViviSat claimed MEV could dock with about 90% of roughly 450 GEO satellites, against about 75% for a refuelling approach that has to open fuel lines (MDA SIS).
   Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Extension_Vehicle ; https://www.northropgrumman.com/space/space-logistics-services. Kind: data/history.
   The insight is that the "repair" here is not a repair. It bypasses the depleted subsystem (propellant) and adds a "jetpack" rather than diagnosing and fixing anything inside the satellite.

7. **MEV economics.** [memory, to verify] Intelsat was widely reported (SpaceNews, around 2019-2020) to pay about $13M a year, about $65M over five years, for MEV-1 service. That is far below the cost of a replacement GEO comsat, roughly $250-400M including launch and insurance. Northrop's page gives no prices. It does claim "over a decade of combined life extension" and describes MEPs as "jetpacks for less expensive life extension" that add about 6 years each. The concept took a long time to reach the market: ViviSat proposed it in 2011, had no customers by 2012, and was dissolved in 2016 before Orbital ATK and then Northrop carried it through. That is about 9 years from concept to first dock, with only 2 MEV units ever built.
   Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Extension_Vehicle ; https://www.northropgrumman.com/space/space-logistics-services. Kind: OEM view plus history.

8. **The robotic repair servicer (MRV plus DARPA RSGS) took about 10 years and the first partner walked away.** DARPA's 2016 RSGS solicitation required the commercial partner to fund the bus. Maxar withdrew in 2019 because it had no orders for its GEO satellites. Northrop SpaceLogistics took over in 2020. Orbital ATK sued in 2017 over government competition with private industry, and the suit was dismissed. MRV (3,000 kg, with three MEPs) launched on a Falcon 9 on 21 July 2026. Service calls are expected from 2027. Optus has a first MEP contract. MRV can install MEPs, relocate, inspect, repair and upgrade satellites, and it can be refuelled through a Passive Refueling Module.
   Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Robotic_Vehicle ; Northrop page above. Kind: data/history.

9. **NASA's OSAM-1 (Restore-L) was cancelled after about $2B.** It was meant to refuel Landsat 7, which was not designed for servicing. The robot would cut insulation and wires, unscrew a bolt and attach a hose. Launch slipped from 2020 to no earlier than 2026. NASA cancelled it on 1 March 2024, citing "continued technical, cost, and schedule challenges" and a shift in the community away from refuelling unprepared spacecraft.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OSAM-1. Kind: regulator/agency decision. This contradicts the idea that capability alone makes legacy repair cheap: repairing unprepared assets was judged not worth it.

10. **The market is moving to prepared interfaces, which means designing for serviceability.** Airbus ordered 100+ second-generation docking plates from Astroscale (March 2025). Astroscale's ELSA-d used a magnetic plate on a cooperative client. ADRAS-J (2024) inspected an uncooperative debris stage from about 15 m. That was inspection first, before any capture: ADRAS-J2 is due in 2027. Orbit Fab sells RAFTI refuelling ports and publicly prices hydrazine delivered in GEO at "$20M for 100 kg" ($200k/kg), with DIU/Space Force and Astroscale LEXI as customers.
    Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astroscale ; https://www.orbitfab.com/. Kind: OEM/company view plus data.

11. **History: crewed servicing worked only where the satellite was designed for it, and it was very expensive.** Solar Max (1984, STS-41-C) was built on the Multimission Modular Spacecraft bus with in-orbit replaceable modules and a grapple fixture. Capture first failed because an undocumented grommet blocked the capture tool, a documentation/knowledge gap. The repair added about 5 years of life. Hubble was designed around Orbital Replacement Units. It had 5 servicing missions (1993-2009). SM1's repair cost about $500M excluding the Shuttle flight. Lifetime cost was about $11.3B through 2015. The mirror flaw that needed the repair came from relying on a single test instrument and ignoring conflicting tests, which was an inspection failure. Intelsat 603 (stranded in LEO in 1990) was rescued by STS-49 in 1992 and flew until 2013. The STS-49 EVA showed that "space work was harder than expected."
    Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Maximum_Mission ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Space_Telescope ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat_603. Kind: history.

12. **Counter-example: most failures in orbit end in write-off, not repair.** Intelsat 33e had a primary thruster failure in 2016 and excess propellant use in 2017 that cut about 3.5 years of life (an insurance claim was estimated near $40M). It broke up in October 2024 into 700+ debris pieces and was declared a total loss, uninsured. There was no servicing option. Even with MEV available, the industry still uses insurance plus replacement as its default "repair".
    Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat_33e. Kind: news/data.

## Reasoning against the hypotheses

- **C1 (universal chain):** Space shows that the chain often collapses or is skipped. MEV does not diagnose or repair anything; it bypasses the depleted function. Falcon 9 refurbishment is inspection-led, scheduled maintenance, not reactive "notice, diagnose" work. Satellite failures mostly end at "notice": with no access, the outcome is write-off plus insurance. The chain holds as a logical skeleton, but in this domain access/reachability is a stage before everything else, and it dominates.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly):** Weak. The barrier in space is physical access (delta-v, rendezvous and docking, robotic manipulation), the lack of designed-in interfaces, and capital. Knowledge does matter in places: the undocumented Solar Max grommet, and OEMs holding the bus design data (Maxar left RSGS for business reasons). But the main gate was never information.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap):** Silent to mildly contradicting. Autonomy in rendezvous and proximity operations helps, but none of the breakthroughs (MEV, Falcon reuse) were knowledge breakthroughs. They were hardware, design-for-reuse and capital breakthroughs.
- **C4 (an order of magnitude cut in diagnose/understand/source/deliver):** Mixed. Falcon 9 got roughly 10x on the refurbish-and-reuse step relative to a new booster (Musk's claim), but it did so by redesigning the asset, and the hardest stage was "deliver" (getting there and back). In satellite servicing, "deliver" (launch plus rendezvous) is the dominant cost: $200k/kg for fuel in GEO, and a dedicated multi-hundred-million-dollar servicer per client.
- **C5 (an AI parts factory solves bespoke parts):** Contradicted for flight hardware. In space the scarce resource is qualification and reachability, not fabrication. MEPs are standardised "jetpacks", not bespoke parts.
- **C6 (inspection remains essential):** Strongly supported. SES's reuse risk premium, the 10, 15, 20 and 40 flight certification steps, the static-fire inspection, the Shuttle's costly between-flight inspection, the Hubble mirror test failure, and ADRAS-J's inspection-first design all point the same way. Inspection plus certification governs whether reuse is cheap: Shuttle versus Falcon is largely a story about the cost of inspection and refurbishment.
- **C7 (structurally inefficient, needs a rebuild):** Mixed. The legacy model (throw away and replace, insure the loss) was rational given that access was impossible. The rebuild did happen in launch, through vertical integration and design for reuse by a new entrant (SpaceX), which supports the idea that incumbents won't rebuild themselves. Satellite servicing is still small: 2 MEVs and 1 MRV in about 15 years, and NASA's $2B OSAM-1 was cancelled.

## Where the money and time go
Getting to the asset and back (launch, rendezvous, docking/capture) plus qualification and certification, not diagnosis. In launch reuse the remaining cost is inspection and refurbishment, and the lever was designing the asset so that inspection is cheap.
