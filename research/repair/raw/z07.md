## Findings

### 1. Sustainment dominates lifecycle cost, so design-stage choices matter most (hard data)
- GAO-23-105341 (F-35 sustainment): the program's life-cycle cost is above $1.7 trillion, and about $1.3 trillion of it (~76%) is operating and sustaining the aircraft. The fleet mission capable rate was ~55% in March 2023. Over 10,000 components were waiting for repair. DOD "has not identified or obtained the technical data it needs" for its intended government/contractor sustainment mix, and missing tech data and training hurt organizational-level maintenance. As of March 2026 the recommendations were still open. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- GAO-21-439: the F-35 sustainment estimate grew from $1.11T to $1.27T (constant 2012 $). The Air Force must cut $3.7M per aircraft per year (47%) by 2036 or overspend $4.4B in that year. GAO: "Cost reductions become increasingly difficult as the program grows and matures." That is the lock-in effect stated directly. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-439
- GAO-03-57: GAO told DOD to prioritize O&S costs "prior to and early in product development, when it is possible to have significant impact on those costs." https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-03-57
- GAO-10-717: for 5 of 7 aviation systems there was no life-cycle O&S estimate from the production milestone. The F-22A O&S estimate rose 47% ($19B) from 2005 to 2009. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-10-717

### 2. The "70-80% of cost is committed at design" folklore (opinion vs data)
- Widely repeated in systems engineering texts (Blanchard & Fabrycky; DAU LCC curves): 70-85% of LCC is "committed" by the end of concept or preliminary design, while only ~5-15% has been spent. [background, unverified this session. The DAU Acquipedia URL redirected to an unrelated domain and was not followed.]
- Counter-evidence: Barton, Love & Taylor (2001), "Design determines 70% of cost? A review of implications for design evaluation," Journal of Engineering Design 12(1). They trace the 70% figure to weak empirical roots, mostly anecdotal and sometimes circular, and argue that "committed" is not "determined". Later supply, process and operating choices still move cost a lot. [background, unverified this session. A DOI fetch returned a different paper.] Implication: the direction (design locks in a large share) is robust. The exact percentage is folklore.

### 3. CFM56 to LEAP: new tech raised maintenance cost and lowered early durability (hard data, mixed)
- CFM56 (Wikipedia, citing industry data): average time on wing to first shop visit is about 30,000 h, with a record of 50,000 h (2016). Life-limited parts: hot section 20,000 cycles, compressor 25,000, fan/booster 30,000. Whole-engine LLPs cost about $3.5-4M, roughly $150/cycle. Incremental redesign did cut maintenance: the CFM56-7 has ~15% lower maintenance cost than the -3, Tech Insertion (2004) about -5%, and Evolution about -4%. Fan blades are dovetailed, so they can be swapped on wing (a maintainability-by-design feature). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_CFM56
- LEAP: CFM lowered operating limits "to improve durability and service life" despite the higher pressure ratio (HPC 22:1, ~2x the CFM56). CMC HPT shroud coating flaked off in 2017 and needed a design change. A 2015 power-by-the-hour deal priced the LEAP-1A at ~$3,039 per engine per day vs $1,852 for the CFM56: about 64% higher maintenance cost per day, in return for ~15% fuel savings. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_LEAP
- [background, unverified this session] 2023-2025: the LEAP-1A/1B HPT blades showed shorter time on wing than the CFM56 in hot, dusty environments (Middle East, India). GE/CFM introduced an HPT blade "durability kit" (certified around 2024-2025) and said it would roughly double time on wing in harsh environments. Shop-visit demand and MRO capacity became a bottleneck. GE's aftermarket earnings rose with it.
- Lesson: "redesign cut maintenance" is not automatic. Efficiency-driven redesign (hotter, higher pressure ratio, new materials) often raises maintenance cost and lowers early durability. Maintainability gains come later, through iterative fixes.

### 4. PW1100G GTF: absence of mature design-for-durability, and its cost (hard data)
- Lufthansa (Sept 2018): A320neos were grounded 254 days since first delivery, 13x worse than the A320ceo, with 78% of it due to engines. In July 2017, 46% of GTF A320neos were out of service for at least a week vs 9% of LEAP aircraft. IndiGo replaced 69 engines from mid-2016 to early 2018. Early start time was ~7 min vs 1-2.5 min for the CFM56/V2500.
- 2023 powder-metal contamination: all ~3,000 PW1100G were recalled, with inspections taking 250-300 days. About 350 aircraft were expected grounded on average through 2026. The cost to P&W and partners is $6-7B, ~80% of it customer compensation. Go First went bankrupt in 2023, citing engine reliability (P&W disputes this). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_%26_Whitney_PW1000G
- Lesson: the dominant repair cost here comes from manufacturing quality and design maturity, plus scarce shop slots and parts. It does not come from a knowledge gap. Diagnosis was known. Inspection takes 250-300 days because of shop capacity.

### 5. A350 and 787: composites and digital design (OEM claims, few numbers)
- Airbus: the A350 is 70% advanced materials, "resulting in lower weight and reduced maintenance". No figures on its page. https://aircraft.airbus.com/en/aircraft/a350/a350-1000
- Wikipedia: Airbus expected 10% lower airframe maintenance than the original A350 design. It moved to carbon-fibre frames in 2007 after criticism of maintenance costs. Li-ion batteries need maintenance every 2 years vs 4-6 months for NiCd. Dispatch reliability rose from 98% (2015) to 99.3% (Feb 2018) and 99.28% recently. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A350
- [background, unverified this session] Airbus marketing: A350 heavy (C-check) intervals of 36 months / 12-year structural checks, vs ~24 months / 6-year for the A330. Airbus claims ~50% fewer structural maintenance tasks thanks to composites (no corrosion or fatigue tasks on the fuselage) and quotes ~25% lower maintenance cost per seat. These are OEM claims.
- Counter-examples of new design creating new repair burdens: Qatar Airways grounded A350s from August 2021 over paint and lightning-protection degradation. It claimed $200,000 per aircraft per day. EASA found no airworthiness issue. The dispute settled in Feb 2023. EASA AD (2017) required power-cycling early A350s before 149 h of continuous power-on. 787: the FAA considered extra inspections on up to ~900 aircraft (2020) over production nonconformities (shimming, flatness). Composite damage assessment and repair also need specialist NDT and repair data, mostly controlled by OEMs. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_787_Dreamliner

### 6. Space Shuttle: the canonical example of design without maintainability (hard data)
- Designed for 100 flights or 10 years each, and pitched at 24 missions per year and ~$9.3M per flight ($54M in 2011 $). Reality: 135 missions in 30 years, an incremental ~$409M per flight in 2010, and ~$1.5B per flight all-in. In FY2009, $1.46B of $2.98B (5 launches) went to flight hardware, including refurbishment between flights. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_program
- Lesson: when reusability or refurbishment costs aren't designed in, no amount of downstream knowledge fixes them. SpaceX's reusable-booster economics are the counterexample: the design set out to make refurbishment fast. [background]

### 7. Regulation forcing design for repair (regulator, hard rules)
- EU Ecodesign Regulation 2023/1670 (smartphones/tablets), applying from 20 June 2025: spare parts must stay available for 7 years after end of placement on the market, delivered within 5 working days (years 1-5) and 10 working days after. Fasteners must be removable or reusable. Repairs must be doable with basic or commercial tools by a generalist (display etc.) or a layman (user-replaceable parts). Repair information (disassembly maps, wiring and board diagrams, diagnostic codes) must be granted within 1 working day of a request. Indicative spare-part prices must be published. Serialised-part tool access must be given within 3 working days. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1670/oj
- Read: regulators treat repairability as a design and information-access property. They target parts lead-time and serialisation (parts pairing) as much as knowledge. That supports C2 (information is gated) but shows the bottleneck spans design, parts and software locks, not knowledge alone.

### 8. Parts provenance is an inspection and certification problem (regulator/news)
- AOG Technics (2023): the UK CAA and EASA found the broker supplied CFM56 parts of unknown origin with falsified release documents, which triggered fleet-wide checks. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_CFM56 (citing Bloomberg)
- Relevance to C5/C6: a made part is worthless without traceable certification. The binding constraint in aviation is the paperwork and approval chain (PMA/DER, EASA Form 1, FAA 8130-3), not the ability to fabricate.

### 9. Design-borne knowledge gating: technical data rights (regulator)
- F-35: DOD did not buy the technical data rights needed for organic sustainment. That design and contract-stage choice now drives depot backlogs and readiness shortfalls (GAO-23-105341). The knowledge monopoly here is a legal artifact of the design and contract phase: AI cannot infer proprietary repair limits and approved data for a stealth coating or a classified subsystem.

## Synthesis
- Design locks in the dominant share of lifecycle repair cost. Sustainment is ~70-76% of LCC for military aircraft (GAO), and GAO says costs get harder to cut as the program matures. The precise "70% committed at design" figure is folklore, though the direction holds.
- Redesign often does not cut maintenance in the first generation. LEAP's maintenance per day was priced ~64% above the CFM56. The GTF was a durability disaster. Gains come from incremental tech-insertion programs (CFM56-7: -15% vs -3), structural material changes (A350/787 composites removing corrosion tasks), and on-wing replaceable modules (dovetailed fan blades).
- Where the money and time go in jet engines: shop visits (LLPs, HPT blades, hot section) and the shop-slot and parts supply queue. The 250-300 day GTF inspection is a capacity problem. Diagnosis is cheap by comparison (borescopes, EGT trend monitoring).
- The knowledge monopoly exists, but it is legal and contractual (data rights, approved data, serialised parts) and is created at design and contract time. AI cannot make an unapproved repair legal.

## Stances
- C1 mixed: the chain applies, but it misses the upstream "design" stage, which sets most of the cost before any failure happens. It also misses the "capacity/queue" stage.
- C2 mixed: information gating is real (F-35 data rights, EU rules), but it is legal and contractual. Much of the cost is physical: durability, shop capacity, LLPs.
- C3 contradicts: AI does not grant data rights, approval, or durability.
- C4 contradicts (mostly): the big cost blocks are physical shop work and parts, and diagnosis is a small share. LEAP/GTF costs were driven by durability and shop capacity.
- C5 contradicts: provenance and approval (AOG Technics) bind. Hot-section parts are among the hardest things to make.
- C6 supports: certification and provenance remain gating.
- C7 mixed: the inefficiency is largely designed in (supports "built to be inefficient"). But it often comes from deliberate trade-offs (fuel efficiency over maintenance) and from business models (aftermarket profits, PBH), not stupidity. The fix point is design and contracts, not just downstream repair.
