## Findings

### F1. UTC (Pratt & Whitney) v. HEICO / Jet Avion: about 10.5 years of litigation, ending in a licence rather than a ruling (court record via SEC filings; history)
- The complaint was filed November 1989 in S.D. Fla. As amended in fiscal 1995 it "alleged infringement of a patent, misappropriation of trade secrets and unfair competition relating to certain jet engine parts and coatings sold by Jet Avion in competition with Pratt & Whitney."
- Damages sought: about $8M for patent infringement and about $30M for trade secrets and unfair competition (overlapping, so about $30M in total), plus pre-judgment interest and treble damages.
- HEICO counterclaimed for malicious prosecution, trade disparagement, tortious interference and unfair competition.
- "All counts of UTC's complaint that were not previously withdrawn by UTC were dismissed by the court and UTC appealed."
- "In March 2000, the Company settled the litigation with UTC. As part of the settlement, the Company received a permanent license to make and sell parts which were the subject of the litigation, and UTC was paid a pre-paid sum for such license by the Company's insurer."
- Defence cost: Travelers sued HEICO in May 1998 seeking "reimbursement of legal fees and costs totaling in excess of $15 million" paid to defend HEICO against UTC.
- Sources: HEICO 10-K FY1997 (filed 1998-01-27), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/0000950170-98-000125.txt ; HEICO 10-K FY2000 (filed 2001-01-25), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/0000950170-01-000098.txt
- Reading: the legal attack on a PMA second-source took more than a decade and over $15M in defence costs, then ended in a paid licence. Even when the reverse engineer largely wins, the legal and time cost of second sourcing is large.

### F2. The PMA business is real at scale: about 20,000 parts approved, about 400-550 new PMAs a year at HEICO, which has "no material patents" and relies on trade secrets (data; company filing)
- HEICO FY2025 10-K: "we are now adding new products to our line at a rate of approximately 400 to 550 Parts Manufacturer Approvals ... per year. We have developed for our customers approximately 20,000 parts for which PMAs have been received from the FAA."
- FSG (Flight Support Group) net sales were a record $3,117.3M in FY2025 (+18%, organic +14%). FSG R&D was $43.7M in FY2025, $36.7M in FY2024 and $26.4M in FY2023.
- "We have no material patents for the proprietary techniques ... we primarily rely on trade secret protection."
- Derived estimate (mine, rough): $43.7M R&D / about 475 PMAs is about $90k of R&D per new PMA on average. This figure also covers DER repairs, other R&D and acquisitions' PMAs, so it is only an order-of-magnitude indicator of the engineering cost per reverse-engineered part at the most experienced PMA house.
- Back in 1998: R&D was $3.1M in FY1997; "since 1991, we have added approximately 200 new PMA parts through internal development and 160 through acquisitions."
- Source: HEICO 10-K FY2025, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/000004661925000082/hei-20251031.htm ; FY1998 10-K, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/0000950144-99-000335.txt

### F3. HEICO itself says the binding constraint is regulator throughput and trust, not knowledge (company view)
- "We believe that an applicant's ability to successfully complete the PMA process is limited by several factors, including (i) the agency's confidence level in the applicant; (ii) the complexity of the part; (iii) the volume of PMAs being filed; and (iv) the resources available to the FAA. ... companies such as HEICO that have demonstrated ... an established favorable track record with the FAA, generally receive a faster turnaround time ... the PMA process creates a significant barrier to entry in this market niche through both its technical demands and its limits on the rate at which competitors can bring products to market."
- The process: "submission of sample parts, drawings and testing data to one of the FAA's Aircraft Certification Offices where the submitted data are analyzed."
- Source: HEICO FY2025 10-K (as above).
- Reading: the incumbent second-sourcer names certification bandwidth and track record as the moat. This weighs directly against C3/C4 (AI removes the knowledge gap) and supports C6 (inspection and certification as the bottleneck).

### F4. Market share achievable by a reverse-engineered second source: over 50% on targeted parts (data, 1998-2000)
- "We believe that we supply over 50% of the market for certain JT8D engine parts for which we hold a PMA from the FAA, with Pratt & Whitney controlling the balance." Competition is "principally based on price and service inasmuch as our parts are interchangeable."
- 1998 framing: "Historically, the three principal jet engine OEMs, Pratt & Whitney, General Electric ... and Rolls Royce, have been the sole source for substantially all replacement parts."
- Lufthansa Technik invested about $26M ($10M equity for 20% of HEICO Aerospace plus about $16M R&D cooperation over three years) to speed up PMA development. An airline MRO paid to create second sources.
- HEICO describes its parts as sold "at lower prices than those manufactured by OEMs" and as "the functional equivalent". (Industry marketing commonly cites 25-50% discounts, e.g. Prime Propulsion's PMA guide: "25–50% cost reduction". That is a vendor claim with no data.)
- Sources: HEICO 10-Ks above; https://www.primepropulsion.com/post/ultimate-guide-to-faa-pma-certification

### F5. The regulatory route: 14 CFR 21.303 and the four PMA bases (regulator)
- 21.303(a)(3)-(5): the applicant must supply drawings and specifications plus dimensions, materials and processes defining structural strength, and "test reports and computations" showing compliance with airworthiness requirements, unless it shows the design is identical to a type-certificated article. Licensing agreements must be evidenced.
- FAA Order 8110.42 routes, per the Wikipedia summary: (1) identicality without a licence (show the part is identical to the OAH part, typically by reverse engineering); (2) identicality by licensure (an OAH "assist letter"); (3) prior STC; (4) test and computation (general analysis against functional requirements and/or comparative analysis against the OAH part). "The modern trend is to combine techniques."
- Two-stage approval: design approval by the ACO, then production/quality-system approval by the MIDO. Since April 2011 (Fed. Reg. 74:53368), PMA holders must meet the same 21.137 quality system as other production approval holders.
- Sources: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/21.303 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval
- Not verified (faa.gov blocked): AC 21.303-4 sets the test-and-computation and identicality guidance. Identicality without a licence is in practice hard to get for critical (life-limited/rotating) engine parts, and AC 33-8 governs comparative testing of engine parts by criticality.

### F6. US law makes reverse engineering a lawful "proper means"; the real constraints are patents, contracts and data rights (statute and Supreme Court)
- Defend Trade Secrets Act, 18 USC 1839(6)(B): "improper means" "does not include reverse engineering, independent derivation, or any other lawful means of acquisition." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1839
- Bonito Boats v. Thunder Craft Boats, 489 U.S. 141 (1989): states may not give patent-like protection to unpatented designs. Copying functional attributes of unpatented products "is legitimate competitive activity"; "the federal patent laws must determine not only what is protected, but also what is free for all to use." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonito_Boats,_Inc._v._Thunder_Craft_Boats,_Inc.
- Constraints that remain: (a) in-force patents, which reverse engineering does not avoid. UTC's first count against HEICO was patent infringement on coatings. (b) Trade-secret misappropriation where knowledge came via ex-employees, suppliers, or OEM manuals under licence. This was the core of the UTC claim, and reverse engineering is lawful only if the article was "legitimately obtained." (c) Contract and licence terms: EULAs and manuals can contractually bar reverse engineering (Bowers v. Baystate). (d) DMCA 1201 for software and firmware, with only a narrow interoperability exception (17 USC 1201(f)). (e) Copyright in drawings and manuals, which is distinct from the part itself. Source for (c)-(d): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_engineering
- Reading: the law has favoured reverse engineering for decades. The knowledge gap is not a legal monopoly on the shape of a part. It is a monopoly on the substantiation data (loads, materials specs, process specs, failure history), together with the cost of litigation risk.

### F7. DoD procurement rules treat reverse engineering as a last resort (regulator)
- PGI 217.7504 sets the order of preference when the Government lacks data rights: performance specs, then sole source from the developer, then licensing / buying data / leader-company / second-source options, and only last: "Contracting activities shall not do reverse engineering unless— Significant cost savings can be demonstrated [and] The action is authorized by the head of the contracting activity."
- PGI 217.7506 (Spare Parts Breakout, DoDM 4140.01 Vol 9): reverse engineering may be used "if there is a clear indication" that its cost will be below the expected competitive savings. The default savings factor is 25% of remaining life-cycle buy value. Breakout costs (tooling, qualification, QC, data rights) are compared with savings, and if costs exceed savings, competition is "uneconomical."
- Screening suspense times: limited screening up to 12 months, full screening up to 3 years, exceptional cases up to 5 years. A firm's capability demonstration must be evaluated "promptly", and if it is not resolved within 60 days the firm must be told the status and an estimated completion date.
- AMSC code C = part requires engineering source approval. Alternate sources must qualify under the design control activity's procedures. AMC 3/4/5 codes = buy from the actual manufacturer or a sole source.
- DFARS 217.7505: no sole-source award where the price rose 25% or more in 12 months without HCA certification.
- Sources: https://www.acquisition.gov/dfarspgi/pgi-217.75-acquisition-replenishment-parts ; https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars/part-217-special-contracting-methods
- Reading: a stated structural bias. The procedures put buying from the OEM ahead of reverse engineering and require economic justification per part. Each part's breakout is a bureaucratic mini-project lasting months to years. This supports C7 (the system is structurally slow) in a specific way: the slowness is codified risk aversion, not ignorance.

### F8. Source-approval statute: Government must be "prompt" but need not delay buys; small businesses get testing paid (statute)
- 10 USC 3243 (formerly 2319): agencies must justify qualification requirements, publish least-restrictive standards, estimate offerors' testing costs, give "a prompt opportunity to demonstrate its ability", and promptly say whether qualification was attained. They need not delay a procurement to do this. If fewer than two qualified sources exist, the agency must periodically seek more and bear the testing and evaluation costs for qualifying small businesses (not production or setup costs). Requirements must be revalidated within 7 years, and waivers last up to 2 years. A 2025 amendment (subsection (e)) requires a process to "streamline and expedite the qualification of advanced manufacturing sources" in wartime or when the Civil Reserve Manufacturing Network is activated.
- FAR 9.202 mirrors this.
- Sources: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/3243 ; https://www.acquisition.gov/far/9.202
- Reading: Congress has noticed that qualification, not design, is the choke point for advanced manufacturing (3D printing) second sources. Supports C6; contradicts C5's "effectively solved".

### F9. DLA Source Approval Requests (SARs): process and timelines (UNVERIFIED; dla.mil blocked)
- From background knowledge, not re-verified this session: DLA Aviation, Land & Maritime and Troop Support accept SARs from would-be alternate sources for AMSC C/H/P-type items. Package categories range from an "identical/actual manufacturer" or "previously approved" data package to a full reverse-engineering or "similar item" package, with first-article testing often required. Practitioners commonly report SAR evaluations taking several months to more than a year. DLA has published processing-time goals in the low hundreds of days, and long backlogs for engineering-support-activity reviews by the military services, because the service engineering activity (not DLA) holds design authority. Vendors bear the cost of the package (reverse engineering, drawings, sample testing). Critical Safety Items need service ESA approval and are the slowest.
- Treat the numbers as unverified. The structural point (DLA buys but the service engineering authority approves) is consistent with PGI 217.7506's AMSC C language ("approved by the cognizant Government engineering activity").

### F10. The TransDigm counter-model: proprietary sole-source parts and pricing power (company filing; regulator/news)
- TransDigm FY2025 10-K: "approximately 90% of our net sales for fiscal year 2025 were generated by proprietary products"; aftermarket consumption lasts "approximately 25 to 30 years." Net sales were $8,831M and gross profit $5,311M, a gross margin of about 60%. Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1260221/000126022125000081/tdg-20250930.htm
- DoD IG audits (via Wikipedia; the PDF was blocked): the 2019 audit found extreme margins on some parts (a 9,400% markup cited on a metal pin), and TransDigm refunded about $16M after a 2019 hearing. A later report alleged $20.8M excess profit on 105 spare parts across 150 contracts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransDigm_Group
- Reading: this is the clearest evidence for C2. When the buyer lacks data rights and qualified alternates, sole sources capture rents. The monopoly comes from design authority, qualification and data rights, not from knowledge of how to machine the part.

### F11. Lack of government-owned technical data drives sustainment cost and readiness (regulator/GAO)
- GAO-22-104752 (Nov 2021): "DOD often does not acquire the IP it needs to operate and maintain its weapon systems". Three decades of GAO reports tie insufficient IP to lower readiness and higher later costs. The DoD IP Cadre had 5 temporary funded positions through FY2023, and DoD lacks expertise in IP valuation. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-104752
- GAO-23-105341 (Sept 2023), F-35: mission capable rate about 55% (March 2023); over 10,000 parts awaiting repair; DoD "has not identified or obtained the technical data it needs"; life-cycle cost over $1.7T, of which about $1.3T is operations and sustainment. Seven recommendations were still mostly open as of March 2026. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- Reading: supports C2 strongly for defence: data rights are the monopoly. It is mixed on C3. AI cannot conjure contractually withheld data, but it may cut the cost of regenerating it by reverse engineering, and the expensive part then becomes substantiation testing and engineering-authority approval.

### F12. Second sourcing always requires two approvals: design and production quality (regulator)
- PMA requires ACO design approval plus MIDO production approval, with a 21.137 quality system since 2011. For DoD, alternate sources also often need first-article testing. A shape-perfect reverse-engineered part therefore cannot enter service without a certified quality system, traceability and, for critical parts, test evidence.
- Sources: Wikipedia PMA page (citing 74 FR 53368); 14 CFR 21.303.
- Reading: contradicts C5 ("AI parts factory effectively solves bespoke parts") for regulated sectors. Making the part is the cheap step; certifying the maker and the part is the expensive one.

## Synthesis

- Where time and money go in second sourcing, in this domain: (1) generating substantiation data (material and process characterisation, testing, analysis) for test-and-computation PMA or DLA SAR packages; (2) regulator and engineering-authority review queues, which HEICO says are rate-limited by FAA resources and applicant track record; (3) legal risk: UTC v. HEICO ran 10.5 years with over $15M in defence costs and ended in a licence; (4) per-part economic justification, since DoD breakout must beat a default 25% savings factor and screening can take 12 months to 3+ years. Geometry and "knowing how it works" are the cheapest parts, since reverse engineering is lawful and well-trodden.
- The scarce layer is legitimate design authority and certification credit: who is allowed to say this part is airworthy or fit for service. This is held by OEMs and type-certificate holders, the FAA ACOs and DERs, and the military engineering support activities. Data rights are the second scarce layer.
- Counter-evidence on C3/C4: HEICO already does at industrial scale (400-550 parts a year, with the know-how) what the founder imagines AI enabling. It still captures only a minority of the aftermarket, because certification throughput, airline and lessor acceptance, OEM warranty and maintenance contracts, and litigation limit it. AI could lower the cost of the engineering-data package (perhaps the roughly $90k-per-PMA R&D figure), but it does not change the FAA/ESA queue or the liability structure.

## Stance summary
- C1: mixed/silent. Second sourcing fits "source parts", but the evidence shows a parallel certification chain (design approval and production approval) before parts can be sourced at all.
- C2: supports, for aerospace and defence. The mechanism is data rights, design authority and qualification, not raw knowledge.
- C3: contradicts. The binding constraints are legal, regulatory and liability-related, and these persist.
- C4: mixed. Engineering-data cost may fall; approval queues, testing and the litigation tail do not fall by 10x.
- C5: contradicts for regulated parts. Fabrication is not the bottleneck; qualification is. Congress (10 USC 3243(e), 2025) is only now creating a process to expedite qualification of advanced-manufacturing sources.
- C6: supports strongly.
- C7: mixed. The procedures show codified risk aversion (reverse engineering as a "last resort"), and OEM business models depend on proprietary aftermarket rents. But the system does produce second sources (20,000 HEICO PMAs), so it is slow and rent-heavy, not incapable.
