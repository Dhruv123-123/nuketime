## Findings

### F1. DoD AM Strategy (2021): AM is a "factory in a box" for point-of-need parts, but qualification and data rights are the barrier [VERIFIED, regulator/policy]
Source: US DoD, Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for R&E, "Department of Defense Additive Manufacturing Strategy", January 2021. https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/dod-additive-manufacturing-strategy.pdf
Key quotes:
- "An AM system is functionally a factory in a box, a digitally controlled production line that can be turned on or off easily." AM "contributes to a reduced risk of obsolete parts" and helps "mitigate diminishing manufacturing sources and material shortages (DMSMS)."
- "AM also allows parts to be fabricated enabling production closer to the time and point-of-need. This can reduce equipment downtime..."
- "Where parts cannot be acquired and data is not available, reverse engineering and AM part production is an approach to support legacy systems." (This matches the founder's C5 scenario: supplier gone.)
- The catch: "AM is currently well suited for low-volume, out-of-production or long lead time parts. However, the need to qualify and certify parts, including structural components, and their associated processes is a challenge and barrier to a broader use and ability to scale production of end use parts." Process variables "may add complexity to qualification and certification, potentially adding cost." Calls for a "risk-based approach", with the most rigour for critical safety items.
- On IP: "There are significant challenges and concerns regarding intellectual property rights and what opportunities are open with respect to reverse engineering end item parts for limited and rapid production. DoD will consider technical data access as part of the contracting process, to include alternative business models such as licensing rather than acquiring data." Also: "Current conventional or industry business profit models may need to be modified."
- It names digital repositories (JAMMEX, RAPTOR: "Repository of Additive Parts for Tactical and Operational Readiness") and admits that metrics for AM's value in "readiness and cost" were still being defined. In other words, there was no department-wide measured cost/time result as of 2021.
- Examples given: C-5 Galaxy AM expansion; a C-17 avionics cooling duct "family of parts approved for AM production"; the Navy/ONR "Quality Made" programme to speed up qualification with modelling and simulation.
Reasoning: the binding constraints are qualification, technical data and IP, and business models, not printing capability. This supports C6 and partly C2: the knowledge monopoly here is legal (data rights), not cognitive.

### F2. GAO-16-56 (Oct 2015): DoD didn't systematically track AM results [VERIFIED, regulator]
Source: GAO, "Defense Additive Manufacturing: DOD Needs to Systematically Track Department-wide 3D Printing Efforts", GAO-16-56. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-56
- AM was used mainly for design, prototyping and some production (e.g., medical). The reported savings were small and were in prototyping: the Army saved "thousands of dollars" on prototyping a Joint Service Aircrew Mask design change.
- Qualifying materials and certifying parts were flagged as the shared difficulty.
- DoD "did not systematically track department-wide activities, resources, results, or lessons learned". This led to a July 2017 Joint AM Steering Group.
Reasoning: a decade of hype, but the measured results base is thin and often self-reported.

### F3. Deutsche Bahn: the largest civil programme of printed rail spare parts [RECALLED - re-verify, company data]
Likely sources: Deutsche Bahn 3D printing pages and press releases (deutschebahn.com); Mobility goes Additive network (co-founded by DB in 2016).
- DB started printing spare parts around 2015. In company statements I recall it had passed 100,000 printed parts by about 2021-2022, across roughly 1,000+ different part types (plastic and metal: headrests, coat hooks, cable boxes, ventilation grilles, brake-system and wheelset components, older-fleet parts). It set annual targets in the tens of thousands of parts.
- Rationale stated by DB: its fleet runs 30+ years, and many original suppliers are gone or won't make small batches. Conventional re-tooling for a handful of parts can take months and cost thousands of euros in moulds. DB said printing cut lead times from months to days or weeks (claims of up to around 90% time reduction), with cost savings on low-volume parts.
- DB built a digital warehouse / internal print-on-demand network and buys printing from a supplier network, not mainly its own printers.
- The constraint DB itself cites: getting design data and permission from OEMs, plus rail approval for safety-relevant parts (railway standards, fire-safety norm EN 45545 for interior polymers). DB has said it needed to reach agreements with OEMs, or reverse-engineer parts where OEMs no longer exist.
Reasoning: this is the strongest support for C5 in a regulated asset class, but it is mostly non-structural or low-criticality parts. DB is the buyer that co-founded an industry network, not a startup.

### F4. Siemens Mobility RRX Rail Service Center, Dortmund-Eving: lead time cut by up to 95% [RECALLED - re-verify, OEM case study]
Likely source: Stratasys / Siemens Mobility case study and press (around 2018-2020), stratasys.com case studies; Siemens Mobility newsroom.
- Siemens Mobility's depot that maintains the Rhine-Ruhr Express (RRX) Desiro HC fleet uses FDM printers (Stratasys Fortus 450mc, ULTEM 9085 / Antero materials meeting rail fire norms) for spare parts and tooling.
- Widely quoted result: production lead times for some parts fell by up to 95% (weeks to hours/days). Example cited: a coupler cover / housing part whose conventional lead time was weeks was printed in days; per-part cost cuts of around 50% for some tools and parts.
- Siemens Mobility (with Stratasys) also printed spare parts for trains (e.g., for Bangkok, Russia's Sapsan service and others) where the OEM is the printer. That is, the OEM uses AM to keep its own aftermarket, not to hand it to independents.
Reasoning: this is a counterpoint to C7's framing. The incumbent OEM adopts on-demand manufacturing to strengthen its service contract (the RRX is a 30-year full-service contract), so it captures the efficiency gain rather than losing the monopoly.

### F5. Maersk onboard 3D printing trial (2016-2017): did not scale [RECALLED - re-verify, company/news]
Likely source: Maersk Tankers / Maersk Line press, 2016; trade coverage (Splash247, Marine Log, 3D Printing Industry).
- In 2016 Maersk tested Ultimaker desktop printers on vessels to print spare parts at sea, aiming to cut stock and the cost of delivering urgent parts to ships in remote ports.
- The trial did not lead to fleet-wide onboard printing. The practical problems were plastic-only parts, crew skills, part qualification and class approval, and a lack of OEM digital files. The industry's centre of gravity moved to shore-based printing hubs near ports. (Not verified this session; treat as likely.)
Reasoning: this contradicts the naive "print at point of use" vision of C4 and C5. The bottleneck was certified files and qualified materials, not the printer.

### F6. Wilhelmsen Ships Service / thyssenkrupp (Singapore) maritime AM: actual parts delivered, modest scale [RECALLED - re-verify, practitioner]
Likely source: Wilhelmsen press releases 2019-2022 (wilhelmsen.com); DNV statements.
- Wilhelmsen delivered its first commercial printed spare parts to Berge Bulk vessels around 2020. It claimed lead-time reductions in the range of about 80-90% and cost savings up to about 40% versus traditional delivery (including the avoided logistics of flying parts to ports).
- In 2021-2022 it started a classification-society-approved programme (with DNV) for printed parts on ships. It later spun off/rebranded the AM unit (I recall a scaling-back or restructuring).
Reasoning: this shows that in maritime most of the cost is logistics (sourcing plus delivery), and printing near the port is where savings are claimed. That supports C4's "deliver parts" stage, but the volume remained small.

### F7. US Navy: shipboard AM from 2014; metal printer on USS Bataan (2022-2023); first approved metal AM shipboard part [RECALLED - re-verify, regulator/military]
Likely sources: NAVSEA news releases; Navy.mil; DVIDS.
- In 2014 USS Essex became the first Navy ship fitted with a 3D printer (polymer).
- In 2018-2019 NAVSEA approved its first metal AM part for shipboard use: a drain strainer orifice for a steam line on USS Harry S. Truman (installed for a one-year evaluation). It had a long approval process for one simple part.
- Around 2022-2023 a hybrid metal AM system (Phillips Corp. / Haas plus Meltio wire-laser) was installed on USS Bataan as the first metal AM at sea.
- The submarine industrial base (around 2021-2024) used AM for submarine parts (e.g., a printed deckhouse component for a Virginia-class submarine, plus valves and fittings) to address supplier gaps and multi-year lead times. Navy leaders stated a goal of thousands of AM parts per year in the submarine supply chain, and an AM Center of Excellence opened in Danville, VA. Parts with historical lead times of 1-2+ years are the targets.
Reasoning: the Navy case is driven by a collapsed supplier base (DMSMS) and long lead times, not by a knowledge gap. Approval rigour (NAVSEA technical warrant holders) is the speed governor. This supports C6.

### F8. US Army: point-of-need labs and a 2019 AM directive centred on technical data rights [RECALLED - re-verify, regulator]
Likely sources: Army Directive 2019-29 "Enabling Readiness through Additive Manufacturing"; Rock Island Arsenal Jointless Hull Center of Excellence press.
- Army Directive 2019-29 encouraged AM at the point of need. It told programme managers to obtain technical data packages and data rights so the Army can print parts. That is an explicit admission that OEM data ownership is the barrier.
- The Army deploys Expeditionary Lab-Mobile and mobile parts hospitals (containerised AM plus machining) in theatre. Field units reported printing ad hoc parts (brackets, covers, knobs) in days instead of waiting weeks to months for supply.
- Rock Island Arsenal's Jointless Hull (large metal AM) was meant to make big vehicle hulls in one piece.
- Contested-logistics doctrine (around 2023-2026, Ukraine lessons) has raised interest in forward printing of drone parts and other items. Ukraine's forces have widely used desktop printing for drone and small parts.
Reasoning: the military's own policy says the knowledge bottleneck is legal access to technical data, not the capability to understand the part.

### F9. Air Force Rapid Sustainment Office and the ageing-fleet problem [RECALLED - re-verify]
Likely sources: Air Force RSO press; Air Force Life Cycle Management Center press; Tinker AFB / Oklahoma City Air Logistics Complex.
- The Air Force uses reverse engineering plus AM for parts whose original vendor no longer exists (B-1, B-52, KC-135, C-5). A frequently cited example is a C-5 lavatory part or a KC-135 part where the conventional quote was hundreds to thousands of dollars per unit with a long lead time, versus a printed part in days for less. Large percentage savings are claimed on individual parts, but on non-flight-critical items.
- Oklahoma City ALC's reverse-engineering cell reports parts where no supplier will bid because volumes are tiny. Reverse engineering a part (scan, material analysis, drawing, qualification) can take months, often longer than the printing itself.
Reasoning: the cost and time sit in reverse engineering and qualification, not fabrication. This partly supports C3 (AI could speed reverse engineering and drawing generation) but strongly supports C6.

### F10. Academic evidence: only a small share of spare parts are suitable for AM, and lifecycle costs aren't always lower [RECALLED - re-verify, study]
- Knofius, van der Heijden & Zijm (2016), "Selecting parts for additive manufacturing in service logistics", Journal of Manufacturing Technology Management. They built a screening method; in industry application only a small fraction of parts was flagged as promising.
- Westerweel, Basten & van Houtum (2018), "Traditional or additive manufacturing? Assessing component design options through lifecycle cost analysis", European Journal of Operational Research. AM is only cheaper over the life cycle when downtime and inventory costs are high, or when AM enables a better design. A printed part is often more expensive per unit and may be less reliable.
- Heinen & Hoberg (2019), "Assessing the potential of additive manufacturing for the provision of spare parts", Journal of Operations Management. Using industrial data, they found that AM's benefit concentrates in a limited set of slow-moving, high-holding-cost or long-lead-time parts. It is not a universal replacement.
- Roland Berger / Deloitte-type reports (2016-2020) on digital warehouses estimated that around 5-10% of spare-parts portfolios are technically and economically printable today. (Recalled, unconfirmed figure.)
Reasoning: this directly contradicts any universal claim in C4 and C5. On-demand manufacturing is a long-tail tool.

### F11. Aviation is the hardest case: FAA/EASA certification, PMA and DER pathways [RECALLED - re-verify, regulator]
- In aviation, a replacement part made by a third party needs PMA (Parts Manufacturer Approval) or similar, with design data showing equivalence. The certification path can take months to years.
- Airline AM work is mostly cabin parts that are not flight-critical. Examples: Lufthansa Technik AM centre; Air New Zealand and Etihad cabin parts; Emirates printed seat parts and video monitor shrouds with Siemens. Lufthansa Technik has said that certification and data, not printing, are the bottleneck.
- GE's printed LEAP fuel nozzle is OEM production, not aftermarket point of need.
Reasoning: for the airliner example in the founder's message, printing doesn't remove the gate. The certification regime is designed to slow things down for safety. That supports C6 and contradicts the strong version of C5 and C7.

### F12. Industry structure: OEMs use AM and digital inventory to keep the aftermarket [RECALLED - re-verify, practitioner]
- Siemens (Mobility and Energy, e.g., the Finspång gas turbine burner repair by AM), GE, Caterpillar, Daimler Buses/Mercedes-Benz Trucks (digital spare parts) and Deutsche Bahn's suppliers all adopted printed spares themselves. Siemens Energy reported cutting burner-tip repair time from weeks to days by printing the replacement tip (around 90% time reduction claimed).
- Digital-inventory startups (e.g., Spare Parts 3D, Replique (BASF spin-off), Ivaldi Group, 3YOURMIND, Craitor) raised modest rounds. Some pivoted, consolidated or remained small. Licensing files from OEMs has been the hard commercial part.
Reasoning: the money flows to whoever owns the design data and the qualification. Distributed manufacturing tends to strengthen, not dissolve, OEM control unless data rights change (right-to-repair laws, the military data-rights push).

## Synthesis

**Where the time and money go.** In this domain, the measured gains from on-demand AM (Siemens Mobility up to around 95%, DB months to days, Wilhelmsen around 80-90%, Siemens Energy around 90%, all company-reported) are in the **sourcing and delivery** stages, for the **long tail of obsolete, low-volume, long-lead-time parts**. They do not cover the bulk of the parts catalogue. The fabrication itself is cheap. The expensive, slow parts are:
1. obtaining or recreating the design data (reverse engineering, OEM licensing)
2. qualifying the material and process
3. certifying the part for a safety-relevant use (rail fire norms, class society, NAVSEA, FAA PMA)

**Real bottleneck.** Authoritative, legally usable technical data plus qualification and certification. The "knowledge monopoly" exists, but it is mostly a legal and IP monopoly (data rights, type certificates) and a liability monopoly (who signs off). It is not mainly an understanding gap. AI could compress reverse engineering (scan-to-CAD, material inference) and qualification analytics, but it doesn't grant data rights or regulatory approval.

**Contradictions to the founder's hypotheses:**
- C5 is overstated. Only a minority of parts (single-digit to low tens of percent) suit AM economically. Desktop onboard printing (Maersk) failed. Even the military, with an urgent need and huge budgets, takes months to approve a single metal shipboard part.
- C7 is partly contradicted. Incumbents (Siemens, DB, OEMs) have adopted these tools and use them to strengthen their service contracts. The system is slow largely by design (safety and certification), not because it was "built to be inefficient".
- C4 holds for a long-tail subset, where an order-of-magnitude lead-time drop is reported. It does not hold system-wide.

**Company shapes this points at:**
- A "qualified-part-as-a-service" company: AI reverse engineering (scan to CAD to material ID), plus a qualification and certification evidence pack, plus a licensed digital inventory, sold to fleet operators (rail, defence, maritime, energy) for obsolete parts.
- A tool for defence sustainment depots (DMSMS) that turns legacy parts into qualified technical data packages faster. The buyer is OCONUS/depot/NAVSEA/Army; the bottleneck is TDP creation.
- An AM qualification and inspection tool (in-situ monitoring plus CT plus AI analysis to cut qualification cost per part). This is C6's evolving inspection industry.
- A broker for digital-rights licences between OEMs and fleet operators. It is risky: earlier startups found this commercially slow.
