## Findings

### 1. GE Digital / Predix: the canonical industrial-AI repair flop [FETCHED]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Predix (software)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predix_(software)
- GE built Predix in 2013 and launched it in 2016. GE spent more than $7B on consultants, developer hiring and support. In 2016 CDO Bill Ruh projected at least $15B in software revenue by 2020. The Predix name was retired in 2022, and what remained was folded into a smaller Asset Performance Management (APM) suite inside GE Vernova (spun off 2 April 2024).
- Reasons the page gives: costly external consultants, overhiring, selling mainly to GE's own units, weak synergy with those units, no unifying vision, and a culture clash between manufacturing and a startup way of working.
- Caveat: the page carries tags for notability and possible paid editing.
- Related source: Wikipedia, "GE Digital", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GE_Digital. In 2018 GE planned a "$1.2B independent company" from its digital assets and planned to sell a majority stake in ServiceMax. In October 2019 it reversed and kept the business.
- [RECALL] The HBR piece "Why GE Digital Didn't Make It Big" (Nov 2018, Ramamurthy and others) argued that GE tried to build a horizontal platform for everyone instead of solving specific, high-value asset problems for paying customers. Commonly cited reasons: the data was locked in heterogeneous assets owned by customers, and the business case had to be proven site by site. GE's "Digital Twin" work survived as narrow APM software. Recalled revenue for GE Digital was about $1B a year, far below the target.
- Reading: software and analytics for diagnosis did not scale as a platform. Value came only when it was tied to specific assets, workflows and service contracts. The bottleneck was data access, integration and the customer's operational change, not the algorithm.

### 2. Uptake (Chicago, industrial predictive maintenance) [RECALL]
- Founded in 2014 by Brad Keywell (Groupon co-founder), with Caterpillar as an anchor partner and investor. It was valued at about $2.3B in 2017, among Chicago's most valuable startups.
- It went broad (rail, energy, mining, aviation, and the US Army for Bradley vehicle maintenance) and then went through rounds of layoffs from about 2019 to 2023. It refocused on fleets and data-driven maintenance for heavy equipment. Valuation reportedly fell well below the peak.
- Common critiques: horizontal "AI for everything" positioning, long enterprise sales and pilot cycles, and dependence on getting customers' OEM telematics data.
- Litigation: Uptake sued C3.ai and others in disputes over trade secrets and data. There were also disputes over access to OEM machine data (needs checking).
- Reading: same pattern as Predix. Diagnostic analytics sold as a horizontal platform hit pilot purgatory and data-access walls.

### 3. Sight Machine (manufacturing analytics) [RECALL; fetch failed]
- A San Francisco manufacturing-data platform founded in 2012. It raised roughly $80M or more, and investors included Two Roads, Jabil and others. It is a long-running "struggling but alive" case: it shifted from a general analytics platform toward partnerships with Microsoft (Azure) and Siemens, and toward a narrower data-foundation pitch. Detailed post-mortem numbers could not be retrieved.
- Reading: the scarce layer is contextualizing messy plant data and changing how operators work, not insight.

### 4. Enjoy Technology (Ron Johnson; in-home tech delivery, setup and service) [FETCHED, partial]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Enjoy Technology", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enjoy_Technology
- Founded in 2014 and launched in 2015. It went public through a SPAC in 2021, filed for bankruptcy in 2022 less than a year after listing, and was sold to Asurion for about $110M with court approval (Reuters headline cited on the page).
- [RECALL] The SPAC valued it at roughly $1.1–1.2B. It had large losses (hundreds of millions of dollars a year against revenue of roughly $80–100M), and its model depended heavily on carrier partners (AT&T, BT, Rogers) paying per visit. The labor-heavy "mobile store" cost structure did not cover its own costs.
- Reading: the in-home labor visit is the cost floor. Software and knowledge did not change the unit economics of a truck roll.

### 5. Asurion as the acquirer (Enjoy) and the survivors of device repair [RECALL]
- Asurion, the insurance-funded device protection and repair giant, bought Enjoy's assets and had earlier bought uBreakiFix. Device repair consolidated into players whose revenue comes from insurance or warranty, not from repair margin.
- Reading: the business is the financing and warranty wrapper plus a dense physical network, not the diagnosis knowledge.

### 6. Puls (on-demand device repair, then home services) [FETCHED, partial]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Puls (company)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puls_(company)
- Started as CellSavers in 2015 and was renamed Puls in 2017. It expanded from phone repair into smart-home setup, TV mounting, antennas, security systems and garage doors. Funding: $3M seed (Sequoia), $15M Series A (2016), about $25M Series B (2017), and a $50M round led by Temasek (2018). In 2023 it announced a generative-AI home-care assistant. Partners include Apple, TCL (a 5,000-person installation support team) and Antop.
- [RECALL] There were reports of layoffs and contraction around 2020–2023 (not verified here).
- Reading: it broadened from one device to many categories to fill technician utilization, which is a labor-utilization problem, not a knowledge problem. The AI assistant was layered on top and did not change the core economics.

### 7. Homejoy (on-demand home cleaning; analog for on-demand home services) [FETCHED]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Homejoy", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homejoy
- Raised about $38M (YC, a16z, First Round, Google Ventures). It operated in 31+ cities and shut down in July 2015. Stated reasons: profitability and lawsuits over worker classification.
- Other factors: costly international expansion, and cleaners leaving the platform to work directly for clients (disintermediation). A Forbes headline said it "couldn't hold on to its customers". It offered a $19 first clean against roughly $85 elsewhere.
- Reading: once trust is established, the marketplace leaks. Customer acquisition cost (CAC) does not pay back, and labor and regulation set costs.

### 8. Handy (home services marketplace) [FETCHED]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Handy (company)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handy_(company)
- Raised more than $110M through its 2015 Series D. It was sued in 2014 over misclassifying workers, and Angi bought it in 2018 for $165.5M (amount from the Angi page). That is a modest outcome next to the hype around Handy's late-stage rounds.

### 9. HomeAdvisor / Angi (lead-gen era of home repair) [FETCHED + RECALL]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Angi Inc.", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angi_Inc.
- HomeAdvisor had more than $300M in revenue in 2015. IAC bought Angie's List for $781.4M in 2017. In 2016 it paid a $1.4M class settlement over claims that payments from providers affected their ratings and rankings, and it acknowledged that provider revenue can affect search order. Revenue in 2025 was about $1.03B. By comparison, the page reports $1.132B for a 2018 period, so the business is flat to down.
- [RECALL] FTC v. HomeAdvisor (final order January 2023): HomeAdvisor paid $7.2M over claims that it deceived service providers about the quality and cost of leads. Contractors complained widely about paying for shared, low-quality leads. Angi later shrank its revenue to improve lead quality and moved away from fixed-price Angi Services.
- Reading: the "matching / knowledge of who can fix it" layer was monetized by taxing tradespeople, and it eroded trust on both sides. The scarce resource was skilled labor capacity, and the marketplace could not create it.

### 10. Thumbtack [FETCHED]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Thumbtack (website)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thumbtack_(website)
- Valued at $3.2B in 2021. Revenue was about $400M in FY2024 with about 300,000 pros. It took on $75M in SVB debt in 2024 and launched AI search and recommendations in 2024. This is the survivor case: slow growth, and its supply of pros has been roughly flat since 2012 (250k then, 300k in 2025).
- Reading: in 13 years, pro supply grew only about 20%. The marketplace does not create technicians.

### 11. Shyp (on-demand shipping logistics; analog for "deliver the part" convenience) [FETCHED]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Shyp", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shyp
- Raised $62.1M (Kleiner-led Series B) and expanded to six cities. It moved its couriers to employee status. In 2017 it cut most staff and left New York, Los Angeles and Chicago, and it shut down in March 2018.
- [RECALL] CEO Kevin Gibbon's farewell post blamed the company's own choices: scaling to many cities too early before the unit economics worked, and serving low-frequency consumers when it later pivoted to small e-commerce businesses.
- Reading: a last-mile pickup and packaging layer on top of incumbent carriers captures little margin. Logistics density belongs to the incumbents.

### 12. Cross-cutting pattern (synthesis, opinion)
- Industrial diagnosis platforms (Predix, Uptake, Sight Machine) failed or shrank because of data access and integration, pilot purgatory, horizontal positioning, and the fact that the buyer's real value sits in service contracts and uptime guarantees that OEMs already own.
- Consumer and home repair or service platforms (HomeAdvisor, Homejoy, Handy, Enjoy, Puls, Shyp) failed or shrank because of labor cost and supply of skilled workers, worker-classification law, disintermediation, high CAC with low repeat frequency, and the economics of truck rolls. None of them died for lack of diagnostic knowledge.

## Stance reasoning
- C1 (universal chain): silent to mixed. The failures cluster in two different stages: data and diagnosis (industrial) and labor and dispatch (home). That is consistent with the chain as a description, but in practice the economics differ completely by segment.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. OEMs did keep control of machine data, which hurt Uptake and Predix, so data monopoly is real. But the home-services failures were about labor and trust, not knowledge.
- C3 (AI removes the knowledge gap): contradicts. Predix spent more than $7B on analytics and diagnosis and did not get adoption. Puls and Thumbtack added AI assistants without changing their economics.
- C4 (10x cost drop in the four middle steps): contradicts. In the failed companies the dominant costs were labor visits, customer acquisition and integration. Shyp shows that convenience on top of incumbent logistics captured little value.
- C5 (AI parts factory): silent. None of the cases studied covers it.
- C6 (inspection persists): silent to mildly supports. Worker vetting and certification were costs that platforms could not remove.
- C7 (structurally inefficient, needs a rebuild): mixed. HomeAdvisor's lead model was extractive (FTC case), which supports misaligned structure. But many attempted "rebuilds" died, which suggests the incumbent structure reflects real constraints such as labor, liability and density.
