# e01: Gas turbine and power plant repair: OEM vs independent service providers (PSM, EthosEnergy, Sulzer, MD&A), hot-section repair, LTSA economics, outage durations and what drives them

Date of research: 2026-10-20.

## Method and limits

- The shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn ran out before this agent's first query, so no new searches were run. Research came from primary documents fetched directly by known URL: the European Commission GE/Alstom merger decision (M.7278, 2015; ~37k lines of text), GE Vernova's GER-3620 Rev P maintenance manual (Heavy-Duty Gas Turbine Operating and Maintenance Considerations), GE Vernova's FY2025 10-K, Calpine 10-Ks (FY2015, FY2019), Howmet FY2025 10-K, the PSM/Hanwha Power website, and CCJ Online (Combined Cycle Journal) user-group write-ups.
- Items marked [UNVERIFIED] are practitioner rules of thumb from background knowledge that I could not re-check this session. Treat them as hypotheses.
- Strongest evidence: the EC decision (regulator, built from customer and competitor questionnaires plus internal GE and Alstom documents) and the GE manual and 10-K (OEM's own numbers).

## Findings

### F1. The servicing market is a timed knowledge monopoly that opens only when a frame "matures" (regulator)
EC M.7278 GE/Alstom (2015), recital 60, and the summary of precedent before recital 1647:
> "As the turbine reaches its maturity (approx. 8-16 years), other service providers start entering the market as all the necessary know-how is no longer exclusive to OEM. Upon expiry of a warranty or a first long term services agreement (LTSA) with OEM, customers start tendering aftermarket services for their gas turbines. This means that competition exists only for the supply of aftermarket services of mature HDGTs."
> "...all the know-how is proprietary to the OEM and often protected by the intellectual property rights. No competition is taking place at this stage because gas turbines cannot be serviced by service providers other than OEM."
Source: https://ec.europa.eu/competition/mergers/cases/decisions/m7278_6808_3.pdf
Supports C2 strongly: it is a knowledge monopoly, but one bounded by time and frame maturity. Note what ends it: fleet operating experience accumulates, and ISPs reverse-engineer parts and build repair procedures over years. A step change in information access is not what ends it.

### F2. LTSA attach rates of 80-100%; warranties, lenders and insurers lock in the OEM (regulator)
EC M.7278, recitals 88-95: Alstom sold [80-100]% of its HDGTs with an LTSA over 5 years, and GE sold a service package with over [80-100]% of its HDGTs. Customer quotes:
- "Due to warranty and the lack of other resources in the industry, OEM will typically be the only provider with the know-how and appropriate parts."
- "During this warranty period one has to keep using OEM hot gas path parts."
- "GT services by OEM are normally required by project lenders to secure the stable operation of the power plants."
- "Fitting of reverse engineered parts can be risky if their provenance is not known and also may impact on insurance position."
- "Newest technology needs a LTSA otherwise may be too long reaction time in case of a failure."
Also: customers fold LTSA cost into total-cost-of-ownership bids, which adds "a certain degree of competition even in a monopoly market."
This is mixed for C2 and C7. The lock-in rests on risk allocation (warranty, finance, insurance) as much as on knowledge. Parts of the "inefficiency" are deliberate risk transfer that buyers choose, and buyers do get competition at the point of sale.

### F3. OEM shares in servicing its own mature frames: >60-70%, and >90% on the 9FA (regulator)
EC M.7278, recitals 1666-1669: "For all the frames GE has market shares above [60-70]%... for the 9FA, GE's has a market share above [90-100]%." PSM held about [10-20]% of 7FA servicing and was "GE's main challenger". "The other ISPs are significantly smaller than PSM, namely Ethos and PWPS." GE held [90-100]% of 9FA upgrades.
Supports C2 (OEMs capture the aftermarket). It also shows how small the ISPs are.

### F4. Entry barriers are capital, qualification, IP and addressable market, not only know-how (competitor testimony to regulator)
EC M.7278, recitals 1700-1703:
- "the build-up of a world class supply chain requires strong intensity of qualified engineering man power and capital. Also considering the IP point of view, the work to avoid any IP issue in redesign parts/processes for a specific GE frame, can be very expensive and time consuming."
- "No other ISP could reasonably undertake the risk to make the relevant investments, because currently 90% of the 9FA customers are locked in LTSAs with a term of 12-20 years. The barrier to entry in terms of investment cost required vs available market space is incredible."
- "Investment in re-engineering and tooling is huge (repair parts / spare parts)"
- Customers "are reluctant to be involved with ISPs until the product portfolio is available and proven."
- The costs of entering spare parts and upgrades are "very high"; for field services and parts repairs "the costs are lower."
- Ethos (recital 1695): "could certainly develop 9FA capability, however there is no addressable customer base given the GE lock, so the incremental investment does not make sense."
This contradicts C3 and C5. Even an ISP that has the knowledge, or could get it, does not enter, because the binding limits are tooling and casting capital, proof of reliability (fleet hours), IP clearance, and contractual lock-in. AI does not remove any of these. Ethos said explicitly that capability was not the barrier.

### F5. Service segmentation: field service, parts repair and spare parts are separable layers with very different barriers (regulator)
EC M.7278, recitals 1649-1650: there are three "core" services, (i) field services and on-site interventions, (ii) parts repair and reconditioning, (iii) supply of spare parts, plus (iv) upgrades. "Generally service providers are not able to offer all categories of services." Upgrades "require extensive development and engineering skills". Sulzer on the 9FA could offer "only... field services, part repairs/reconditioning and the supply of some spare parts for the compressor" (recital 1696). Sulzer was also "working on reverse engineered 9FA parts" (recital 1697). In 2012, only Chromalloy, PWPS and Wood Group (Ethos) were developing 9FA blades and vanes, and none covered the full range.
This maps onto the founder's chain. ISPs win first at "repair" (field labour, reconditioning) and last at "source parts" (new hot-gas-path parts, the high-barrier layer).

### F6. GE Vernova's own numbers: services are two-thirds of Power revenue, and LTSAs cover about 26% of the gas installed base (OEM 10-K, FY2025)
GE Vernova 10-K for FY2025 (filed 2026-01-29):
- Power segment revenue $19,767M, of which services $13,081M (66%) and equipment $6,686M. Segment EBITDA margin 14.7% (12.5% in 2024, 9.9% in 2023).
- "gas turbine installed base of approximately 7,000 units with approximately 1,800 units under long-term service agreements and an average remaining contract life of approximately 10 years."
- Company services RPO $85,993M (2024: $75,976M). Total RPO $150.2B.
- CSAs "generally range from 5 to 25 years"; "Customers generally pay us based on the utilization of the asset (per hour of usage for example) or upon the occurrence of a major maintenance event."
- Net CSA movements in 2025: billings $5,362M, revenues recognised $5,412M, "net unfavorable changes in estimated profitability of $151 million."
- Risk factors: LTSA profitability "depends on our ability to execute and estimates of product durability and reliability... and the availability of cost-reducing materials, technology, and skilled technicians." Also: "We operate in a supply-constrained environment and have experienced... shortages of materials and skilled labor."
- Services growth in 2025 was "driven by Gas Power higher parts volume and favorable price."
Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1996810/000199681026000015/gev-20251231.htm
Interpretation: the OEM model is a razor-and-blades annuity over 20-30 years. About 74% of GE's ~7,000 units are not under an LTSA (served transactionally by GE or by ISPs), so the aftermarket is less locked than the 9FA case suggests for older fleets. Pricing power is rising in 2025 ("favorable price"). An LTSA is a fixed-price bet on part durability, and the OEM also misestimates it ($151M unfavourable).

### F7. GE's own manual: spares, not knowledge, turn days into weeks (OEM technical document)
GER-3620 Rev P (Parts Planning section):
> "a planned outage such as a combustion inspection, which should only take two to five days, could take weeks if adequate spares are not on-site... it is up to the owner to purchase these spare parts on a planned basis allowing adequate lead times."
Personnel Planning: a cooldown of "4 to 24 hours may be required before service may be performed" and turning-gear time of "two to eight hours is normally allocated prior to starting the unit." Labour estimates assume crews "with trade skill (but not necessarily direct gas turbine experience), with all needed tools and replacement parts (no repair time) available", plus "technical direction, specialized tooling, engineering reports, and site mobilization/demobilization."
Source: https://www.gevernova.com/content/dam/gepower-new/global/en_US/downloads/gas-new-site/resources/reference/GER-3620-P.pdf
This supports the idea that parts sourcing and delivery dominate outage time (part of C4). It contradicts the idea that diagnosis and understanding are the bottleneck. Planned outages are scoped years ahead from counted hours and starts, so diagnosis is largely pre-computed. GE's model does say that the technical-direction layer is specialist, while craft labour can be generic.

### F8. In a planned outage, the work is schedule-driven, and the operating profile sets the schedule (OEM technical document)
GER-3620 Rev P:
- Baseline for heavy-duty units (Figure 7): HGP inspection at "24,000 hrs or 1200 starts"; major inspection at "48,000 hrs or 2400 starts".
- Maintenance factors: a trip from 80-100% load counts as 8 normal cycles ("1 Trip Cycle = 8 Normal Shutdown Cycles"), and a trip from peak load is 10:1. Heavy fuels raise the hours factor up to 3x for crude. DLN-1 extended lean-lean operation gives a combustion maintenance factor of 10.
- Worked example (7E.03): a mixed duty profile cuts the HGP interval from 1,200 starts to 667 starts (3.9 years) and from 24,000 to 19,700 hours.
- Appendix D: hot-gas-path parts (stage 1-3 nozzles, shrouds, buckets) are typically repaired at each HGPI and replaced after 3-4 HGPI intervals. Combustion liners are replaced after about 2-5 CIs. Notes tie some replacement lives to "GE Vernova approved repair process" or "Strip and Recoat is required at first HGPI".
- The OEM draws a "risk" line: "The use of non-GE Vernova approved parts, repairs, and maintenance practices may represent a significant risk. Pursuant to the governing terms and conditions, warranties and performance guarantees are predicated upon... conforming to GE Vernova approved operating instruction manuals and repair/modification procedures."
- Appendix D also says: "GE Vernova uses our unique proprietary knowledge of the part environment and use of specific inspection methods to address operational related damage."
This bears on C1. For planned outages the chain is not "notice → diagnose". It runs as count hours and starts → schedule an inspection years in advance → pre-buy parts → inspect → repair or replace. The chain is a calendar, not a fault response. Unplanned (forced) outages do follow the founder's chain. The approval language also supports C2 (the OEM uses warranty to monopolise "approved" repair knowledge) and C6 (approval is a gate).

### F9. ISPs compete on engineering, not just price: PSM's interval-extension upgrades (ISP marketing claims)
PSM (now Hanwha Power) website: GTOP hot-gas-path packages for GE 7F "extend inspection intervals to 32,000+ hours" (against a baseline of 24,000). FlameSheet combustor retrofits are also claimed to extend intervals. The site claims its solutions "routinely outperform OEM standards on inspection intervals, emissions, and lifecycle cost." Frames covered: GE 7F, 9F, 6B, 7EA, 9E, plus Siemens/MHI 501F.
Ownership history (EC M.7278 remedy): PSM was Alstom's ISP subsidiary. The EC required its divestment to Ansaldo as part of the GE/Alstom clearance (recitals 1968-1970) because PSM was "an important independent service provider". It later went to Hanwha.
Source: https://www.psm.com/ and the EC decision.
For C4 and C7: the largest ISP lever is not cheaper diagnosis. It is better parts design (coatings, cooling) that extends intervals by about 33%, which removes whole outages. ISPs that survive are effectively alternative OEMs for mature frames, with their own casting supply chains. These are company claims and not independently verified.

### F10. A big fleet owner can insource most major maintenance through scale and fleet homogeneity (Calpine 10-K)
Calpine FY2015 10-K: "Our large fleet of similar technologies has enabled us to build significant technical and engineering experience with these units and minimize the number of replacement parts in inventory. We leverage this experience by performing much of our major maintenance ourselves with our outage services subsidiary." In 2015, "our outage services subsidiary completed 15 major inspections and nine hot gas path inspections"; the fleet had a "forced outage factor of 2.3% and a starting reliability of 98.3%". Fleet: about 26.6 GW. Calpine FY2019 10-K: total LTSA commitments only "$217 million", payable over 1-20 years, which is small for a fleet of about 26 GW.
Sources: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/916457/000091645716000057/cpn_10kx12312015.htm ; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/916457/000091645720000007/cpn_10kx12312019.htm
Supports C2 (knowledge is the moat, and owners who build it escape OEM dependence) but contradicts C3. Calpine closed the gap with fleet scale (hundreds of similar machines, operating data, an in-house crew, and pooled rotable spares), not with information access. The real enabler is fleet-level pooling of data and spares. Calpine's EFOF of 2.3% suggests a well-run insourced model performs well, which counts against "built to be inefficient" (C7).

### F11. The hot-section parts layer is a concentrated superalloy casting oligopoly with pricing power (supplier 10-K)
Howmet FY2025 10-K: gas turbines (industrial gas turbine plus oil and gas) were "approximately 11% of the Company's revenue in 2025" (about $0.9B of $8.25B). Products include "investment castings for jet engines and industrial gas turbines (nickel superalloys...), including airfoils"; "turbine parts with advanced cooling and coatings". "Product price increases are in excess of material and inflationary cost pass through." Capex "expected to remain elevated with additional investments in capacity expansions." Demand is driven by "accelerating data center build-out."
Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/4281/000000428126000012/hwm-20251231.htm
Contradicts C5. Single-crystal and directionally solidified cooled airfoils with thermal-barrier coatings come from a few casting houses (Howmet, PCC, a few others) that have pricing power and capacity limits. An "AI parts factory" does not give you a vacuum investment-casting line, ceramic cores, or a coating qualification history. Metal additive manufacturing is used for some combustion parts and repairs (EthosEnergy, Siemens, and PSM have all marketed AM burner and repair parts [UNVERIFIED in this session]), but not, at fleet scale, for first-stage rotating blades.

### F12. Users say field-service quality is about people, procedures and checklists (practitioner forum)
CCJ, "7F USERS GROUP: Steering committee, OEM discuss ways to improve field service": users asked GE for "more emphasis on reading manuals and drawings", leadership skills, cost tracking for change orders, and timelier escalation. They noted that "technical field advisors often take a back seat to experienced craft workers". GE responded with communications training, a "Field Vision" app with standardised checklists, and a level-based qualification system for field engineers.
Source: https://www.ccj-online.com/7f-users-group-steering-committee-oem-discuss-ways-to-improve-field-service/
Partly supports C3: some of the knowledge gap is in-the-field procedure, where AI and digital checklists help. But the OEM itself already owns the manuals, so the gap is execution and skill, not missing information.

### F13. Hot-section repair is metallurgy, fixtures and NDE, which is physical process know-how (ISP practitioner presentation)
CCJ summary of a Sulzer presentation, "7F Users Group: Repairing second-stage nozzles": the repair sequence is dimensional checks (field versus shop), mechanical coating removal after a metallurgical sample, heat treatment to reveal defects, ultrasonic and dye-penetrant NDE, then correction of downstream deflection and twist with fixtures and jacks (loads of about 30 psi across the airfoil and a thermal gradient of about 250°F). Cracks are routed and welded with Inconel 625 filler, then stress-relieved. A "proprietary parent-metal bonding process" rebuilds thinned walls. Then recoating, heat treatment, and fixture and slide-through simulator checks of hook geometry, seal engagement, throat area and pitch.
Source: https://www.ccj-online.com/7f-users-group-repairing-second-stage-nozzles/
This evidence is mixed. The "understand" step is codified knowledge that AI could help spread. The "complete the repair" step is capital equipment (vacuum furnaces, coating booths, fixtures) and process qualification, and that tacit know-how is held as trade secrets.

### F14. OEM digital remote M&D already exists, and it strengthens the OEM's knowledge advantage (OEM document)
GER-3620 Rev P, "Digital Solutions for Asset Management": GE's Remote Monitoring and Diagnostic Center pairs "digital tools, subject matter experts in the M&D center" with "unique domain" experience to "accurately predict the effect of operation and events, extend useful life of parts, improve outage workloads". It is also used for digital outage workscoping. EC M.7278 (recital 471) records the installed base as "a source of scale for sourcing, information, innovation, sales opportunities and money."
This contradicts C3 as framed. AI-driven diagnostics already exist in this domain and are run by the OEM, using proprietary fleet data (thousands of units, millions of hours). Data advantage scales with installed base, so AI may widen the OEM's lead rather than close the gap, unless owners or ISPs pool fleet data (the Calpine model, or a user-group consortium).

### F15. EthosEnergy marketing names supplier lead times as the outage-planning risk (ISP marketing)
CCJ, EthosEnergy webinar "Rethink Traditional Repair Options" (May 2020): the pitch centres on "longer supplier lead times" and on spare inventory that is "harder to justify". The proposed fix is to remove component repairs from the outage critical path (through rotable exchange sets).
Source: https://www.ccj-online.com/ethos-webinar-3/
Supports the view that sourcing and logistics (rotable pools, lead times) is where outage time goes.

### F16. Background rules of thumb [UNVERIFIED this session; treat as hypotheses to check]
- Typical heavy-duty F-class outage durations: combustion inspection about 3-7 days (consistent with GE's "two to five days"), HGP inspection about 2-4 weeks, major inspection about 4-8 weeks. Unplanned rotor or blade-liberation events can take months, and some take more than a year when rotors or castings are long-lead.
- An F-class first-stage bucket set costs in the low millions of dollars. A full HGP parts set costs many millions. Repairs commonly cost a fraction of new (often cited at 30-60%) and are limited to about 2-3 repair cycles.
- Lost margin from downtime often exceeds the repair cost itself for a combined-cycle plant in tight markets, so owners pay premiums for speed (rotable exchange sets, OEM priority).
- Known fleet events (GE 9HA/7HA stage-1 blade oxidation in 2018-2019, Siemens Energy and MHI issues, GE 7F.04/7F.05 compressor issues) show that new-technology failures need OEM root-cause engineering and redesign. That is a genuine "understand" step that is hard for anyone outside the OEM.

## Synthesis

1. Where the money and time go. In gas turbines, cost is dominated by hot-section parts (new or repaired hot-gas-path components and rotors) and by lost generation margin during the outage. Labour is secondary. Time is dominated by part availability and lead time (GE: 2-5 days becomes weeks without spares) and by fixed physical steps: cooldown, disassembly, shop repair cycles, recoating, reassembly, and turning gear. For planned outages, "diagnosis" is largely pre-computed from hours and starts and checked by borescope. For forced outages on new frames, the "understand" step (root cause) is genuinely OEM-gated.
2. The knowledge monopoly is real but time-limited and enforced by more than knowledge. Regulator evidence shows OEM servicing shares of 60-100% on their own frames, with competition only after 8-16 years. But the lock is held as much by warranties, lender and insurer requirements, LTSA terms of 12-20 years, IP and redesign cost, casting and tooling capex, and proven reliability as by information. An ISP (Ethos) said capability was not the barrier; the addressable market was.
3. AI does not remove the binding constraints. These are certified parts made from superalloy castings, qualified repair processes, fleet operating evidence, and risk transfer (who pays when a blade liberates). OEMs already run AI-like M&D on their installed base, and that data advantage scales with fleet size.
4. Where a startup wedge might exist: (a) fleet-data pooling across owners and ISPs, to make independent condition-based interval extension credible to insurers and lenders; (b) rotable-pool and parts-logistics marketplaces that take the parts layer off the outage critical path; (c) qualification and inspection tech (automated borescope, NDE, digital records that insurers accept); (d) repair-process qualification of AM for combustion and non-rotating parts. Each of these attacks the trust and risk layer, not just knowledge.
