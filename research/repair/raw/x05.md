# x05: YC and recent seed-stage companies in repair, maintenance, parts, inspection (2022-2026): what they do and any traction

## Method and limits
- The shared WebSearch budget for this run (200 calls) was used up before this agent started, so no web searches ran. All evidence below comes from direct WebFetch of primary pages: YC company profiles, YC industry listings, the YC Requests for Startups page, and company sites (Gecko Robotics news, Tractian homepage). Fetched 2026-10-20.
- YC industry listing pages load only about 50 of N companies (Industrials shows 50 of 436, Aviation and Space about 50 of 64). Query parameters are ignored server-side, so the scan of the YC portfolio is partial and weighted toward the newest batches (S26/F26).
- Traction numbers come from company profiles, which are self-reported. Section C lists non-YC names from prior knowledge. Those were NOT re-verified this session and are labelled that way.

## B. YC's own thesis signal (Requests for Startups, Fall 2026), https://www.ycombinator.com/rfs
- "New Operating Systems for the Physical World" (Charlie Warren) covers construction, maintenance and fleet operations, coordinating AI agents, robots and human workers. Quotes: "the software for the physical world hasn't really changed in over 20 years." "The new operating systems will manage the robot and human labor together."
- "Data for the Real World" (Austin Tindle, Diana Hu) covers dense data from physical environments, including robotic inspection. Quote: "We're still working with sparse data from remote sensors designed for humans, not AI."
- Interpretation: YC frames the gap as DATA plus LABOUR plus coordination software, not as manuals or knowledge being locked up.

## D. Patterns and reasoning
1. Where YC money is going in repair: (a) sensing and early detection (Gecko, Gridware, Talos, Tractian-type); (b) physical repair labour by robots (Rodeo, RoboDock, Proprio, Aseon); (c) inspection documentation (GroundControl, F4, KorrAI); (d) full-stack outsourced uptime (Mantle). Very few target the "understand the manual / knowledge gap" layer on its own.
2. The companies with real traction (Gecko, Gridware, KorrAI, GroundControl, Tractian) all own a data-capture or compliance workflow. None is a pure knowledge layer. Gecko's revenue comes from robots collecting data humans could not collect cheaply, plus defense and energy contracts. That is a data and access bottleneck, not a knowledge one.
3. KorrAI is telling: revenue comes "from consulting budgets rather than software budgets", and "engineers use the results to prepare the safety reports they sign". AI substitutes for consultant hours, while a licensed human keeps the sign-off. This supports C6 (inspection and sign-off persist) and a weak version of C2/C3 (consultant knowledge work is compressible). It contradicts the strong C3, since liability and sign-off do not disappear.
4. F4 Industries deliberately markets "without AI, deterministic" analysis for inspection. Buyers in regulated inspection value determinism and auditability over generative AI. That is a caution on C3 and C6.
5. Dawn's staged autonomy (read-only, then one-click approval, then unattended only on proven fault types) shows how trust is actually earned. Diagnosis automation advances fault class by fault class, not all at once.
6. Mantle and Rodeo bet that the scarce input is technician labour and uptime ownership. Proprio cites 400,000 unstaffed data center technician roles. That points to labour, not knowledge, as the binding constraint.
7. Bespoke parts (C5): YC F26 and S26 contain several "AI-native factory" companies (twentyfour26, Sinter, Applied Kinetics), but all are pre-traction, and none targets obsolete or out-of-production repair parts with certification. No evidence here that C5 is "solved". It is an active, early bet.
8. Aviation MRO is under-represented at YC despite the founder's airliner example. Possible reasons: certification (FAA Part 145 and PMA), long sales cycles, OEM data rights. This is an open whitespace, or a sign that it is hard to enter.
