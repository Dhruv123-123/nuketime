## Findings: verified this session (primary or near-primary)

### F1. F-35 ALIS: the most automated diagnostic and logistics system ever fielded is limited by data integrity, not by diagnostic logic (GAO-20-316, March 2020)
Source: GAO-20-316, "F-35 Sustainment: DOD Needs a Plan to Address Risks Related to Its Central Logistics System". https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-316.pdf (product page https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-316)
- ALIS "is intended to help maintainers manage tasks including aircraft health and diagnostics, supply-chain management, and other maintenance events." It downloads health-related codes from the Portable Memory Device.
- Missing records: DoD IG (June 2019) found that of 263 spare parts delivered to one location in June 2018, **213 (81%) had no electronic records** (Electronic Equipment Logbooks).
- Users at all 5 locations GAO visited track data **outside ALIS** because they "do not always trust the data". One location estimated **5,000-10,000 hours per year** of manual tracking.
- Alert fatigue: "by continuously ignoring alerts in ALIS caused by missing or inaccurate data, squadrons could be at risk of ignoring an alert for a legitimate aircraft issue."
- Groundings caused by the system itself: one location reported **9,262 hours (9% of possible flight hours)** grounded from Oct 2018 to Sep 2019 because of unresolved ALIS Action Requests, mostly missing or inaccurate parts records. Another reported **2,200 hours in 6 months** waiting for contractors to resolve parts-related ARs. A third location: **16,221 hours (2%)** non-mission capable from ALIS issues; another: **3,246 hours (0.5%)** in FY2018. These are undercounts, because commanders sometimes fly when ALIS says not to.
- Knowledge and control bottleneck: users "must submit an AR for every issue and wait for a response that can sometimes take months", and "DOD incurs a fee each time an AR is submitted". Users said more transparency "could reduce reliance on contractor support".
- Readiness then: full mission capable 31.6% against a 60% target; mission capable 59.5% against 75%.
Kind: regulator (GAO audit). Hard data, though some figures are self-reported by sites.

### F2. ALIS's diagnoses were unreliable enough that it is being replaced (ODIN)
Source: Wikipedia "Lockheed Martin F-35 Lightning II", Maintenance and logistics section, citing DoD and GAO sources. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_F-35_Lightning_II
- ALIS "had numerous problems, including unreliable diagnoses, excessive connectivity requirements, and security vulnerabilities". It is being replaced by the cloud-based ODIN (live at Yuma, Lemoore and Nellis in 2021). OBK hardware performance is reported as double that of ALIS.
- GAO product page update: DOD issued an ALIS Redesign Strategy in Nov 2021, and in **April 2026** documented a performance-measurement process. A 2021 DOD report said "ALIS still lacks the ability to collect performance measures that affect the performance of the F-35 aircraft fleet."
Kind: news/secondary summary of regulator sources.

### F3. The F-35 repair bottleneck is depot capacity, parts and technical data, not fault identification (GAO-18-75, GAO-23-105341, June 2026 GAO)
- GAO-18-75 (Oct 2017): average depot repair time **172 days, twice the objective**. Depot repair capability **6 years behind schedule**. Parts shortages kept F-35s from flying **~22% of the time** (Jan-Aug 2017). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-75
- GAO-23-105341 (2023): mission capable rate **~55%**. **More than 10,000 components awaiting repair.** O-level maintenance "hurt by a lack of technical data and training". DOD "has not identified or obtained the technical data it would need". Sustainment is about $1.3T of the >$1.7T life-cycle cost. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- June 2026 GAO (per Wikipedia, citing Breaking Defense and Defense News, 12 June 2026): fleetwide FMC fell from **38% (FY2021) to ~25% (FY2025)**, and MC from **67% to 44%**. Causes cited: spare parts, maintenance and depot capacity, engines, supply chain. DoD launched a **$13.7B** sustainment strategy.
Kind: regulator. This supports C2 in the IP/technical-data sense: the government lacks data rights. That is a legal and contractual knowledge monopoly, not an intelligence gap.

### F4. NFF costs the US DoD more than $2 billion a year (commonly cited figure)
Source: Wikipedia "No fault found", citing D. Werner, Aerospace America (AIAA) 2015. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_fault_found
Causes are grouped into (a) how the user perceives a possible fault and (b) the diagnostic methods available to the maintainer (after Söderholm 2007, Reliability Engineering & System Safety 92(1)).
Kind: news/secondary. The $2B figure is widely repeated; the primary derivation is unknown.

### F5. Intermittent faults are a major physical root cause of NFF; better reasoning cannot see a fault that is not present at test time
Source: Wikipedia "Intermittent fault", citing Qi, Ganesan & Pecht (2008) Microelectronics Reliability 48(5), and Bakhshi, Kunche & Pecht (2014). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermittent_fault
- Example: a hard-drive supplier's NFF returns had a significantly higher re-return rate than expected, which implies they were real intermittent failures.
- Manufacturers "may assume a cause instead of paying to find the root cause". Time domain reflectometry can locate intermittent wiring leakage under operational stress.
Kind: study (via secondary).

### F6. Fault tree analysis is a design-time safety tool and was never built for field diagnosis
Source: Wikipedia "Fault tree analysis". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_tree_analysis
- Developed in 1962 at Bell Labs for the Minuteman I launch control system. Boeing used it for civil aircraft from about 1966. The 1970 FAA change to 14 CFR 25.1309 (probability criteria) spread it across civil aviation.
- Limits: it "is not good at finding all possible initiating faults", each tree covers only one top event, and common-cause dependencies are hard to model.
Kind: history.

### F7. Standards: BITE (ARINC 604) and onboard maintenance systems (ARINC 624) have been standardised for decades
Source: Wikipedia "ARINC". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARINC
ARINC 604 gives guidance for designing BITE and defines the Centralized Fault Display System. ARINC 624 is the onboard maintenance system standard. Automated fault detection, logging and correlation on airliners is decades-old, mature infrastructure, not a new frontier.
Kind: history/standard.

### F8. AI diagnosis literature names coverage and expertise as hard limits
Source: Wikipedia "Diagnosis (artificial intelligence)". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagnosis_(artificial_intelligence)
Expert-system diagnosis needs experience that is "typically only available after a long period of use", has incomplete coverage (unseen behaviour gives no diagnosis), and must be rebuilt after small system changes. Model-based diagnosis is limited by incomplete fault models. There is a diagnosability trade-off between sensor count/cost and unambiguous isolation. The DX workshop series has run since 1989. AI diagnosis is a 35-year-old field, and its limits come from observability, not from a lack of reasoning.
Kind: practitioner/academic view.

### F9. Prognostics limits: run-to-failure data are scarce
Source: Wikipedia "Prognostics" (PHM Society material). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prognostics
Data-driven PHM "need[s] substantial training data". Run-to-failure data are hard to obtain because running systems to failure is lengthy and costly. When future usage differs from past usage, data coverage is nearly impossible. The JSF programme made "substantial investments in PHM".
Kind: study/practitioner view.

### F10. MEL deferral: the system is built to tolerate faults rather than diagnose them immediately
Source: Wikipedia "Minimum equipment list". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_equipment_list
The MEL lets aircraft depart with regulator-approved inoperative items within time limits, with repairs done during overnight stops. This structural feature lowers the value of instant diagnosis for many faults, because the binding constraint is the maintenance window and parts positioning.
Kind: regulator framework.

### F11. Aviation LLMs exist, but evidence on troubleshooting is thin
Source: arXiv 2311.17686, "AviationGPT" (Wang et al., MITRE-affiliated authors, Nov 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17686
The paper claims "over a 40% performance gain in tested cases" on aviation NLP tasks (information extraction, report querying). It reports **no maintenance troubleshooting accuracy result**.
Kind: study. Weak evidence for C3.

### F12. Commercial MRO AI platforms make qualitative claims only
Source: Lufthansa Technik AVIATAR page. https://www.lufthansa-technik.com/en/aviatar
The only figures given: new aircraft types produce "50 times more data than older generations", and 38,000 aircraft are forecast in operation by 2025. Predictive maintenance outcomes are described qualitatively with no removal or NFF numbers.
Kind: OEM/MRO view (marketing).

## Background findings (from the literature, NOT re-verified this session)
- **B1** [BACKGROUND] NFF/unjustified removal rates for avionics LRUs are commonly cited at **30-60%**, and for some LRU types above 50%. Industry sources (ATA/IATA, Boeing AERO articles of the 2000s) frequently quote that around half of avionics removals test NFF at the shop.
- **B2** [BACKGROUND] Military CND (cannot duplicate) and RTOK (retest OK) rates in 1980s-1990s USAF/USN studies were often 20-50% at the shop. BIT false alarm rates in fighters of that era (e.g., F-16, F/A-18) were reported as very high in some studies (a majority of BIT indications not confirmed). The 1980s-90s "BIT false alarm problem" drove programmes such as smart BIT and AI-based BIT filtering.
- **B3** [BACKGROUND] Cranfield/EPSRC NFF studies (Khan, Phillips, Jennions, Hockley 2014; "NFF events in maintenance engineering" Parts 1 and 2) find the causes are mostly organisational: incentives (a line mechanic is measured on turn time, so swapping a box is rational), inadequate troubleshooting time at the gate, poor feedback loops between shop findings and the line, test-condition mismatch between the aircraft environment and shop ATE (vibration, temperature, humidity), wiring and connector faults misattributed to LRUs, and TSM/FIM ambiguity groups that list several LRUs.
- **B4** [BACKGROUND] Boeing's Fault Isolation Manual (FIM) and Airbus's Trouble Shooting Manual (TSM) map a fault code or message to an ordered procedure. They often end in "replace LRU X; if fault persists, replace LRU Y". BITE isolation to a single LRU is designed for the probable cause, and dispatch pressure rewards swapping.
- **B5** [BACKGROUND] Airbus AIRMAN and Boeing AHM forward faults in flight via ACARS so parts and procedures are ready at landing. Airlines have reported large reductions in delays. Delta TechOps (APEX/predictive) publicly claimed maintenance cancellations fell from ~5,600 a year (2010) to ~55 (2018), but that is attributed to a broad reliability programme, not to diagnosis alone.
- **B6** [BACKGROUND] Each unjustified removal costs the shop test (often $1-5k+), logistics and spares pool inventory, and erodes MTBUR. The repeat-fault "rogue unit" phenomenon (units that repeatedly fail on aircraft and pass on the bench) is well documented. Serial-number tracking of rogue units is a known mitigation.

## Reasoning / synthesis

**How good is automated diagnosis already?** On fault *detection* it is very good. Modern airliners and fighters have extensive BITE (ARINC 604), centralised maintenance computers (ARINC 624), real-time fault forwarding and FIM/TSM procedures keyed to fault codes. Fault *isolation to a single component* is much weaker. It is limited by ambiguity groups, by intermittent and environmental faults, by wiring and connectors that BITE cannot see, and by fault models built at design time (FTA/FMEA) that do not cover every initiating fault. The F-35, the most ambitious autonomic logistics design, shows that when diagnostics depend on data and on contractor-controlled systems, the integrity of records and the speed of the AR loop dominate. Bad data grounded aircraft for up to 9% of flight hours at one site. The diagnostic intelligence was not the binding constraint.

**Why do false removals persist?**
(1) Physics/observability: intermittents and test-environment mismatch.
(2) Incentives: dispatch reliability and turn-time metrics reward box-swapping, and the removal cost lands on a different budget (the shop or the pool).
(3) Feedback-loop failure: shop NFF findings rarely flow back to line troubleshooting.
(4) Ambiguity is built into the manuals.
(5) Data quality and trust: ALIS alert fatigue.
(6) Knowledge/IP control: data rights and AR processes sit with the OEM.
AI can help with (3), (4) and partly (5), for example by mining shop findings and fleet history to rank causes and flag rogue units. It cannot remove (1) without new sensing, and cannot remove (2) or (6) without changes to contracts and metrics.

**Implications for the hypotheses**
- C1 is broadly consistent, but aviation adds deferral (MEL) and a split between detection and isolation. "Diagnose" is in practice a loop of swap and retest, not a single step.
- C2 is mixed. The knowledge gap that matters is partly data rights and contractual (F-35 technical data), not an inability to reason. Much NFF comes from physics and incentives, not ignorance.
- C3 is contradicted. Diagnosis is limited by observability and data integrity, and AI diagnosis has existed since 1989 with known coverage limits. LLM evidence on troubleshooting is thin.
- C4 is mixed to contradicted. In F-35 data the cost and time sit in depot repair (172 days), parts shortages (22% of time), 10,000+ components awaiting repair and data errors, not in figuring out what is broken. NFF ($2B+/yr in DoD) is a real target where better isolation can save a lot, perhaps a large fraction of NFF cost, but not 10x across the whole chain.
- C5 is silent in this evidence.
- C6 is supported: release to service, data integrity and the danger of ignored alerts show that verification is essential, and the ALIS lesson is that automated systems themselves need assurance.
- C7 is mixed. Misaligned incentives and contractor-controlled loops look structural (supports), but much of the "inefficiency" is deliberate safety tolerance (MEL, redundancy) and physics.

## Company-shape notes
- NFF/rogue-unit analytics that closes the shop-to-line feedback loop (joins shop teardown findings, BITE logs and serial-number history to re-rank FIM steps). The value is sold as reduced unjustified removals and spares pool size.
- Maintenance data integrity / digital records layer (the ALIS lesson: 81% of parts arriving without e-records). Also relevant to sovereign or defence users who want independence from the OEM loop.
- Intermittent-fault sensing hardware (wiring TDR, in-situ vibration/thermal monitoring) that makes invisible faults observable. This is a deeptech wedge, because AI alone cannot see what is not measured.
- Defence sustainment tools that operate within government-owned technical data (the services take over F-35 sustainment management by Oct 2027 and transfer elements by 2028).
