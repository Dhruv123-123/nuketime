## A. The failures: what the filings actually say

### 1. Fast Radius (Chicago; UPS-backed "cloud manufacturing" / virtual warehouse)
- Founded in partnership with UPS, and UPS led its 2019 round (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Radius). It ran a manufacturing cell inside the UPS Worldport campus in Louisville. The Q2-2022 10-Q XBRL tags reference UPS warehouse rental, sublease and shipping agreements.
- Went public in Feb 2022 via a SPAC merger with ECP Environmental Growth Opportunities Corp. The Q2-2022 10-Q equity statement shows a **$75.0M PIPE**. The line "Effect of Business Combination and recapitalization, net of redemptions and issuance costs" is only **$3.03M**, which means SPAC trust redemptions wiped out nearly all of the trust cash (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1832351/000095017022016662/fsrd-20220630.htm).
- **Unit economics were the killer.** 9M-2022 (10-Q Q3-2022, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1832351/000095017022025107/fsrd-20220930.htm): revenue $20.6M (+52% YoY), cost of revenue $19.0M, so a **7.7% gross margin**. The 9M-2021 figure was a **negative 3.7%** gross margin. Q2-2022 gross margin was 3.6%. The 9M-2022 net loss was **$89.0M on $20.6M revenue**. Six-month G&A was $52.9M versus $16.5M a year earlier, inflated by public-company costs.
- Debt: current term loans of $26.8M (SVB and SVB Innovation Credit Fund VIII) were all classified current. Cash was $21.4M at 30 Sep 2022.
- Chapter 11 filed **7 Nov 2022** (D. Del. 22-11051). The CEO blamed "recent headwinds in the capital markets" that "inhibited our ability to adequately put in place the capital structure needed." The press release claims **>$200M invested** in its Cloud Manufacturing Platform, a "$360B addressable market", and ">2,000 manufacturing customers and 23,000 software users since 2020" (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1832351/000095017022022842/fsrd-ex99_1.htm). The company also cut about 20% of staff (8-K, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1832351/000095017022022842/fsrd-20221104.htm).
- Substantially all assets were sold to **SyBridge Digital Solutions**. The sale was approved 12 Dec 2022 and closed 16 Dec 2022. After secured lenders, **nothing was left for common stockholders** (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1832351/000119312522307631/d247830d8k.htm). The buyer was a conventional tooling and injection-molding roll-up, and it bought the software and customer list cheaply.
- Reading: the "digital warehouse" story attracted capital, but the business underneath was a low-margin job shop (about 0 to 8% gross margin) carrying software-company opex. The 2022 SPAC redemption wave removed the cash cushion. Demand for digital warehousing was not the binding constraint. ">2,000 customers" yielding about $27M a year implies roughly $13k per customer per year: a long tail of small, episodic orders.

### 2. Shapeways (NYC/Eindhoven; 3D-printing marketplace + OTTO software + MFG.com)
- SPAC with Galileo Acquisition Corp, announced Apr 2021 (Wikipedia). [MEMORY, UNVERIFIED: about $410M implied enterprise value at announcement.]
- FY2023 10-K (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1784851/000162828024013659/shpw-20231231.htm):
  - Revenue was **$34.5M** in 2023 versus $33.2M in 2022 (+4%). Gross margin was **42%** versus 43%. That is a healthy margin, unlike Fast Radius.
  - Customer count rose 14%, while average revenue per customer fell 10%.
  - Direct sales of manufactured parts were about 77% of revenue and the marketplace about 14%. Quote: "To date, we have not recognized a material amount of revenue from software." The software pivot (OTTO, launched Q4 2021, plus the MFG.com and MakerOS acquisitions in Apr 2022) produced essentially no revenue.
  - Operating expenses jumped to **$59.9M** from $38.3M, against $14.5M of gross profit. Items included a $4.0M write-off of prepaid Desktop Metal equipment services, a $12.8M impairment on assets held for sale, and a $1.1M goodwill impairment on MFG.
  - Linear AMS, a Michigan metal AM contract manufacturer, cost $8.9M cash in May 2022.
  - The net loss was **$43.9M** (2023) versus $20.2M (2022). The accumulated deficit was $176.9M and year-end cash $12.2M, with a going-concern paragraph. The largest customer was about 17% of revenue. Headcount was 220.
  - Strategic review: "Potential buyers have expressed interest in either the manufacturing business or the software business, but not both." This is evidence that the integrated digital-inventory-plus-factory thesis was not valued as one thing.
- Chapter 7 filed **2 Jul 2024** (8-K, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1784851/000162828024030980/shpw-20240702.htm). Just before that, Shapeways took a $669.5k secured note from 3DP Custom Manufacture LLC. The CEO, CFO, COO and seven directors all resigned, leaving no employees.
- **The profitable core survived.** The Dutch unit "had remained profitable" (Wikipedia). The founders (Vogelaar, Schouwenburg) bought the EU assets through Manuevo BV/WVS International, reopened 1 Aug 2024, bought the US IP in Dec 2024 and acquired Thangs. The company had 88 staff in 2025 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapeways).
- New CEO letter (https://www.shapeways.com/blog/a-letter-from-our-new-ceo-marleen-vogelaar): priorities are factory stability first, then B2B "spare parts management, machinery lifespan optimization." Marketplace data was lost in the bankruptcy, and the acquired software is "outdated and not scalable." Vogelaar elsewhere: the marketplace "hadn't been invested in since 2018" (https://www.shapeways.com/blog/bringing-shapeways-home-and-welcoming-thangs).
- Reading: the killer was not low gross margin. It was the SPAC-era opex and acquisition binge (software and metal AM) on a flat $34M revenue base, with software that never monetised. A lean factory with real customers survived, and is now pitching spare parts as the next market.

### 3. Context: the wider AM-SPAC collapse (the supply side of "AI parts factory")
- **Desktop Metal**: SPAC Dec 2020, valued at $1.5B privately in 2019. Nano Dimension agreed to buy it for **$135M–$183M** (Jul 2024), and it filed Chapter 11 in Jul 2025. Bloomberg headline: "3D Printer Facing Liquidation Blames Legal Fees for Bankruptcy" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_Metal).
- **Markforged** built Digital Forge, a cloud and AI platform for "parts at the point of need". Nano Dimension acquired it for **$116M** (25 Apr 2025). Wikipedia says Nano sold it to **Stratasys for about $42M on 27 May 2026** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markforged; not cross-checked).
- [MEMORY, UNVERIFIED] Velo3D was delisted and restructured in 2024. Several other 2021 AM SPACs (Fathom, Shapeways, Fast Radius, Markforged, Desktop Metal, Velo3D) lost more than 90% of their value.
- Reading: the capital markets valued "digital manufacturing platforms" as software. The underlying economics were capex-heavy, utilisation-sensitive job-shop economics.

## B. The survivors and pivots: what each is doing now (company sites, Oct 2026)

### 4. Replique (Mannheim; BASF spin-off)
- It no longer leads with "digital warehouse." The homepage calls Replique an "All-In-One Auftragsfertiger" (all-in-one contract manufacturer) covering 3D printing, **CNC, casting, forging, injection molding**, finishing, engineering services and a material hub. It is "a single contract partner" fulfilling through a global network of certified partners. Segments: prototypes and pilot series, qualified series, **spare parts and SOS demand**. It claims "100+ customers", including logos for Roche, Danfoss, MAN, BASF and Alstom, and a "procurement effort reduced by up to 90%" (https://www.replique.io/).
- Reading: it pivoted from "3D-printed digital inventory platform" to a procurement and contract-manufacturing broker that is agnostic about process. The customer value is a single PO and a qualified supplier. 3D printing is not the value.

### 5. Ivaldi Group (Seattle/Oslo; maritime digital inventory)
- The site now sells training and certification, infrastructure implementation, pilot projects, consulting and "Business Intelligence" (inventory analysis), plus a partner network called BEAM. Industries are maritime, mining, facility maintenance and automotive. Visible prices are for consulting products: a **$150** 20-minute intro meeting and a **$490 "Step-by-Step Guide to Digital Inventory & Parts On-Demand"** (https://ivaldi.com/).
- A testimonial describes "about three years of pilot work" in South Africa and Namibia.
- [MEMORY, UNVERIFIED] Ivaldi partnered with Wilhelmsen Ships Service from about 2019 on 3D-printed ship spares, with first commercial deliveries in 2020. Wilhelmsen later reduced its emphasis on the program.
- Reading: the shift toward consulting, guides and pilots suggests a long sales cycle and pilot purgatory. The bottleneck is customer change management, part qualification and getting OEM data, not printing.

### 6. Craitor (now San Diego; defense)
- The site now reads "production layer for critical hardware, deployed where it's needed" and "manufacturing sovereignty" (https://craitor.com/). Its product, **FieldFab**, is a ruggedised printer that is MIL-STD-810H compliant **while printing**, rated −40°F to 122°F. It is "in service with all four US military services," developed under a CRADA with the USMC, NIWC Pacific and Army DEVCOM, and used in 35+ field exercises. It is sold via **GSA Advantage**, is TAA compliant, and a novice can print a part in under 15 minutes (https://craitor.com/fieldfab).
- Reading: hardware sold to a buyer (the military) that has (a) an extreme cost of downtime and logistics, (b) its own technical data rights for many parts, and (c) a budgeted procurement channel. This is the clearest case of product-market fit in the set.

### 7. 3YOURMIND (Berlin; AM workflow software)
- Products: "AI powered" **Part Identification**, which screens a parts catalogue for AM candidates, and **Distributed Manufacturing** order software. Marketing claims are "up to 30% lower operational costs", "inventory reduction of up to 75%" and "up to 75% fewer lost sales". No named customers appear in text (https://www.3yourmind.com/).
- The blog has tilted to defense: RIMPAC participation with Phillips Corp, FLEETWERX/FLEET-X, and "digitized sustainment." The blog post "Why Digital Part Inventories Power Manufacturing" says buyers need "traceability, qualification, and version control" and "qualified part data". It gives no hard numbers (https://www.3yourmind.com/blog/why-digital-part-inventories-power-manufacturing).
- Reading: AI is already used for the "which parts are printable" triage step, which is exactly the AI-knowledge step in C3/C4. Yet the binding constraints the vendor itself names are qualification and data rights.

### 8. Spare Parts 3D (Paris/Singapore; DigiPart software)
- The site rendered empty, so there is no primary data this session. [MEMORY, UNVERIFIED] DigiPart uses AI and ML to screen spare-parts catalogues, and its customers include rail (SNCF and RATP-type operators), aerospace and defense. Industry rule-of-thumb figures put the share of a spare-parts catalogue that is technically and economically printable in the low single to low double digits (about 5–10%). In other words, most spares are not candidates.

### 9. Deutsche Bahn / Mobility goes Additive (the canonical buyer-side success)
- The primary pages failed this session (404 or DNS errors). [MEMORY, UNVERIFIED] DB has 3D-printed on the order of 100,000+ spare parts across several thousand part types since 2015, focusing on obsolete and low-volume items such as headrests, coat hooks and housings. It founded the "Mobility goes Additive" network. A key enabler is that DB as operator either owns the part data or reverse-engineers non-safety-critical parts.

### 10. Academic evidence
- Chekurov et al. (2018), "The perceived value of additively manufactured digital spare parts in industry: an empirical investigation," IJPE (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpe.2018.09.008). The full text was blocked (403). [MEMORY] It found the main barriers were lack of digital models, IP and ownership, quality and certification, and the cost of printing relative to conventional parts. Firms saw value mostly for obsolete, low-volume and slow-moving items.

## C. Synthesis: what worked and what killed them

What killed them:
1. **Job-shop unit economics wearing software multiples.** Fast Radius ran at a 7.7% gross margin with an $89M nine-month loss on $20.6M revenue.
2. **SPAC capital structure.** Redemptions left Fast Radius with about $3M from the trust. Venture debt (SVB) accelerated. Public-company G&A roughly tripled.
3. **Software that did not monetise.** Shapeways: "not ... a material amount of revenue from software." Buyers wanted either the factory or the software, never both.
4. **Acquisition and capex binges** into metal AM and Desktop Metal equipment, while revenue stayed flat.
5. **Long-tail, low-value demand.** About 2,000 customers at about $13k a year for Fast Radius. The average revenue per customer fell 10% at Shapeways.

What worked:
1. **A lean, profitable factory with loyal customers.** The Shapeways Netherlands unit stayed profitable and was relaunched by its founders.
2. **Buyers with data rights and a catastrophic cost of downtime.** Craitor and 3YOURMIND are both now selling to defense: point of need, GSA channel, CRADA co-development.
3. **Process-agnostic procurement brokerage.** Replique moved to CNC, casting and forging, offering one PO and qualified suppliers.
4. **Operator-led programs** like Deutsche Bahn's, where the asset owner controls the data and accepts the risk on non-critical parts.

The real bottleneck is not knowledge of how to fix a part, and not the printer. It is three things:
- (a) legal access to and ownership of the part's technical data (OEM IP)
- (b) qualification and certification of a substitute part for the specific asset, plus who carries the liability
- (c) the small share of catalogues that are actually printable, combined with lumpy demand, which gives poor factory utilisation.

AI is already applied to part triage (3YOURMIND Part ID, DigiPart), and it has not changed (a) or (b).

## D. Stance on hypotheses
- C1 (universal chain): silent. Nothing here addresses it directly. Digital-inventory players sit only in the source and deliver steps.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. The binding constraint is data rights and IP (a form of monopoly), plus qualification. It is less about know-how. Operators that own their data (DB, the military) succeed.
- C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicts. AI part screening has existed since about 2019–2021 (DigiPart, 3YOURMIND), and the businesses still struggled or pivoted. The vendors themselves name qualification, traceability and data rights as the constraints.
- C4 (10x cost drop in diagnose/understand/source/deliver): contradicts or mixed. Fast Radius reached a 7.7% gross margin even with UPS logistics integration. Digital inventory claims are "up to" 30% opex and 75% inventory reductions on a subset of parts. That is nowhere near 10x across all repair.
- C5 (bespoke parts solved by an AI parts factory): contradicts. Fast Radius and Shapeways were literally on-demand parts factories, and both went bankrupt. Desktop Metal went bankrupt. Markforged sold for about $42M. Only a small share of spares is printable, and survivors added CNC, casting and forging.
- C6 (inspection and certification essential): supports. Qualification, traceability and version control are the gate. Survivors sell "qualified" parts and certified partner networks (Replique, 3YOURMIND), and Ivaldi sells training and certification.
- C7 (industry structurally inefficient; rebuild needed): mixed. Inefficiency in spares (obsolescence, inventory write-offs) is real and is why buyers pilot. But attempts to "rebuild" it as a platform failed on economics and capital structure. Pivots toward the incumbent shapes (contract manufacturer, defense supplier) survive.

## E. Company shapes implied
- Point-of-need hardware plus a data vault for defense and remote operators, where data rights are owned and downtime is catastrophic (the Craitor shape).
- A qualification-and-certification layer for substitute and reverse-engineered parts: engineering data package, test evidence, approval documentation (a PMA/DER analog for non-aero sectors).
- Process-agnostic spares procurement with an obsolescence broker, rather than a 3D-print-only service (the Replique shape).
- Avoid: an "AI parts factory" as an asset-heavy, low-utilisation job shop financed like software.
