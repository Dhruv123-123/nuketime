## Findings

### 1. Ogilvie: guilds survived because they served distributional interests, not efficiency (VERIFIED, abstract)
- Source: Sheilagh Ogilvie, "The Economics of Guilds," Journal of Economic Perspectives 28(4), Fall 2014, pp. 169-92. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.28.4.169
- What the page says: guilds let business groups and political elites jointly extract rents. Members shared monopoly gains with rulers in exchange for enforcement. Institutions persist "not because they are efficient but because they serve the distributional interests of powerful groups."
- Kind: study. Opinion/interpretation grounded in a large evidence review.

### 2. Ogilvie, The European Guilds (Princeton 2019): skills were real, but access was rationed and innovation blocked (VERIFIED, publisher page)
- Source: https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691137544/the-european-guilds
- The book covers thousands of guilds between 1000 and 1880. It concedes that guilds "fostered work skills and transmitted useful techniques." Its main claims are that guilds denied apprenticeships to outsiders, blocked innovations that threatened members, manipulated markets, and excluded women, migrants and Jews as "vile encroachers." On its account, guild quality regulation kept poor consumers from buying cheap goods.
- Implication: even the leading critic does not say the knowledge was fake. She says the knowledge was real and that access to it was the monopoly. Knowledge plus access control is the rent.

### 3. Epstein: guilds existed to transmit tacit skill, and mobile journeymen spread technique (FROM LITERATURE)
- Source: S. R. Epstein, "Craft Guilds, Apprenticeship, and Technological Change in Preindustrial Europe," Journal of Economic History 58(3), 1998, pp. 684-713. Also S. R. Epstein and M. Prak (eds.), Guilds, Innovation and the European Economy, 1400-1800 (CUP 2008). Wikipedia's Guild article cites Epstein and Prak 2008 for the view that guilds supported "training, quality control, and technological adaptation" (VERIFIED as a citation): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild
- Argument: craft knowledge was largely tacit and experiential. It could not be bought in a spot market, because masters would not train competitors and apprentices could walk out once trained. Guild apprenticeship rules solved that hold-up problem. Itinerant journeymen (the Wanderjahre) spread innovations between cities. On this view, guild opposition to new technology was selective, not systematic.
- Ogilvie's reply: "'Rehabilitating the guilds': a reply," Economic History Review 61(1), 2008 (cited on Wikipedia's Guild page, VERIFIED as a citation). She points to the many apprenticeships that took place outside guilds, uneven enforcement of training rules and masterpieces, and guild campaigns against new technology.

### 4. Apprenticeship was leaky; much training happened without guild enforcement (FROM LITERATURE)
- Patrick Wallis, "Apprenticeship and Training in Premodern England," JEH 68(3), 2008: a large share of London apprentices left before their term ended (on the order of 40-50% did not finish). They probably acquired skills faster than the 7-year terms implied. Contracts were flexible in practice.
- Prak, Crowston, De Munck, Kissane, Minns, Schalk, Wallis, "Access to the Trade: Monopoly and Mobility in European Craft Guilds in the 17th and 18th Centuries," Journal of Social History (2020): across several cities, guilds admitted a substantial share of outsiders and immigrants. This contradicts a pure "closed shop" reading.
- Reading: the barrier was less "nobody else knows how" and more "the legal right to sell is restricted." That is a regulatory and certification moat, not a knowledge moat.

### 5. Theory: guilds beat clans at spreading knowledge, and open markets beat guilds once they can be enforced (FROM LITERATURE)
- de la Croix, Doepke and Mokyr, "Clans, Guilds, and Markets: Apprenticeship Institutions and Growth in the Pre-Industrial Economy," QJE 133(1), 2018. In their model, family or clan transmission keeps techniques inside lineages. Guilds widen the pool of teachers. Competitive apprenticeship markets, which the authors associate with England and the Low Countries, spread innovations fastest.
- Implication: the gains came from changing the institution that governs who may learn from whom. Improving the knowledge medium was not the main lever.

### 6. Printing raised city growth by about 60% (VERIFIED)
- Jeremiah Dittmar, "Information Technology and Economic Change: The Impact of the Printing Press," QJE 126(3), 2011, pp. 1133-72. https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/126/3/1133/1854310
- European cities that adopted printing in the 1400s grew about 60% faster between 1500 and 1600 than similar cities. There was no pre-adoption difference between adopters and non-adopters. Distance from Mainz is used as an instrument.
- Caveat: this measures cheaper information in general. It does not measure the specific dissolving of craft secrets.

### 7. The Encyclopédie codified the mechanical arts at scale (VERIFIED)
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die
- 17 volumes of text plus 11 volumes of plates (1762-1772), about 2,784 plates. It was the first general encyclopedia to describe the mechanical arts in detail. Diderot planned to visit workshops and interview artisans; much of the content in fact came from experts and from the Académie's Descriptions des arts et métiers.
- First-edition print run was 4,225 copies, against a French average of about 2,000. About 25,000 sets of all editions were sold by 1789. The first edition cost 980 livres. The economics articles denounced monopolies, guilds included.
- Gap: the Encyclopédie missed coke-smelted iron, which was still little known outside Britain and Holland. Codification lagged frontier practice.

### 8. Upper-tail knowledge from the Encyclopédie predicts industrial growth; mass literacy does not (VERIFIED, abstract)
- Squicciarini and Voigtländer, "Human Capital and Industrialization: Evidence from the Age of Enlightenment," QJE 2015; NBER w20219. https://www.nber.org/papers/w20219
- City-level density of Encyclopédie subscribers is "a strong predictor of city growth after the onset of French industrialization." Initial literacy does not predict later growth. Matching British patents to an 1840s French firm survey shows upper-tail knowledge raised productivity in innovative technology.
- Implication: codified knowledge paid off where a local elite could absorb and apply it. Having the book was not enough; you needed people who could use it.

### 9. Books of secrets: print turned craft recipes into a mass market, but the label "secret" stayed a selling point (VERIFIED, light)
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Books_of_secrets. Main scholarly source: William Eamon, Science and the Secrets of Nature (1994), FROM LITERATURE.
- The Secreti attributed to "Alessio Piemontese" (Girolamo Ruscelli, 1555) was mainly practical and technological recipes. It was widely reprinted with no copyright and sold as cheap pamphlets to lower-class readers. Eamon counts more than 100 editions, FROM LITERATURE. The genre spread knowledge that had been confined to tradesmen and families.
- Counterpoint: recipe disclosure did not end guilds. Most guilds survived another 250 years, until legal abolition.

### 10. Guilds ended by law, not by encyclopaedias (VERIFIED for 1791 and 1864)
- France: Turgot's edict of February 1776 abolished the jurandes and was reversed within months after his fall (FROM LITERATURE). The d'Allarde decree (March 1791) and the Le Chapelier law of 14 June 1791 abolished guilds and compagnonnage and banned strikes. The loi Ollivier annulled the Le Chapelier law on 25 May 1864. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolition_of_guilds_in_1791
- The Wikipedia Guild page says guilds declined as patent systems and centralised states took over regulation, and that most had died off by the mid-19th century (Krause, Death of Guilds, 1996). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild
- Reading: the 1751-1772 Encyclopédie preceded abolition by about 20-40 years. Codified knowledge supplied the intellectual case, and states removed the legal monopoly. Knowledge diffusion was necessary but not sufficient.

### 11. Tacit knowledge resists text: Bessemer, the bread machine, Collins (VERIFIED partly)
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit_knowledge. Polanyi (1966): "we can know more than we can tell." Licensees of Bessemer's patent could not make his process work, and he could not convey the know-how to them. Matsushita's bread-machine team learned kneading by apprenticing with a baker and needed about a year of iteration to reproduce it. In the sapphire Q-factor case, Western labs could not replicate a Moscow team's measurements until the two teams took them together.
- FROM LITERATURE: Harry Collins's 1974 TEA-laser study found that no lab built a working laser from published papers alone. Only labs that had personal contact with a working group succeeded.
- Implication for C3: written or AI-mediated explicit knowledge does not close the gap for hands-on, embodied steps. Diagnosis-as-information is the easier part; making it work with your hands is harder.

### 12. Mokyr: what matters is the cost of access to knowledge, and the 2025 Nobel (VERIFIED for the Nobel)
- Joel Mokyr shared the 2025 Sveriges Riksbank Prize "for having identified the prerequisites for sustained growth through technological progress." The committee stressed that self-sustaining innovation needs explanations of why things work, not just evidence that they do. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Mokyr
- FROM LITERATURE: The Gifts of Athena (2002) frames the Industrial Enlightenment as a fall in "access costs" to useful knowledge, through encyclopaedias, scientific societies and technical manuals. Kelly, Mokyr and Ó Gráda, "The Mechanics of the Industrial Revolution," JPE 2023, argue that Britain's edge came from a large body of skilled mechanics and artisans trained through apprenticeship. Codified science only paid off with that hands-on workforce.
- This is the closest historical analogue to the founder's thesis, and it supports a narrowed version of it. AI is a step change in access cost. The binding constraint then shifts to the skilled hands that can execute and to the institutions that certify them.

### 13. Collective invention: open sharing among firms sped up improvement (FROM LITERATURE)
- Robert Allen, "Collective Invention," JEBO 1983: Cleveland (UK) blast-furnace operators published their design data, and furnace heights and temperatures improved quickly.
- Alessandro Nuvolari (2004): Lean's Engine Reporter (from 1811) published monthly performance figures for Cornish pumping engines. Average duty rose several-fold over the following decades.
- Implication: openly published performance data on machines in the field speeds up improvement. This is a direct analogue for shared repair and failure data.

## Synthesis against the founder's claims
- C1 (universal chain): silent. Guild sources describe production more than repair stages.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. Both camps agree the craft knowledge was real and partly tacit (Epstein, and Ogilvie concedes skills were transmitted). Ogilvie shows the rent came from legal control of access and the right to trade, enforced with political elites. Printed disclosure (books of secrets, the Encyclopédie) did not by itself break the monopoly: guilds lasted until legal abolition in 1776-1791 (France) and the 19th century elsewhere. The monopoly was knowledge plus legal privilege plus certification, with privilege as the load-bearing part.
- C3 (AI makes the gap vanish): contradicts. Every previous fall in the cost of information (printing, books of secrets, the Encyclopédie) left a tacit residue: Bessemer, Collins, the coke-iron gap, and the need for skilled mechanics (Kelly, Mokyr and Ó Gráda). Gains concentrated where absorptive human capital existed (Squicciarini and Voigtländer).
- C4 (10x cheaper intermediate steps): mixed. Information-cost shocks produced large but not 10x gains: about 60% faster city growth over a century (Dittmar). Collective invention gave several-fold efficiency gains over decades. Large, but slow, and dependent on institutions.
- C5 (bespoke parts solved): silent or contradicting. The coke-iron and Bessemer cases show that making things depends on process know-how, not just the specification.
- C6 (inspection persists and must evolve): supports. Guild quality marks, masterpieces and searches were the inspection layer and the main legal moat. Ogilvie argues they mainly limited competition. Who certifies is where the power sits. Inspection lasted longer than secrecy.
- C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed, leaning supports. Ogilvie: institutions persist because they serve distributional interests, not efficiency. Epstein: the structure solved a real hold-up problem in training. Both camps say rebuilding needed legal and institutional change, not only a better knowledge tool.

## Where the money and time go (historical reading)
- The costly, slow part was gaining the legal right and certified status to practise (7-year apprenticeships, master fees, masterpieces, guild membership), plus embodied hands-on skill. Information itself got cheap first, with printing from the 1450s and encyclopaedias from the 1750s. Rents survived that drop for centuries.

## Real bottleneck
- Legal and certification privilege combined with embodied tacit skill. Codified explicit knowledge was not the bottleneck.

## Company-shape implications
- An AI layer for diagnosis and knowledge (the "printing press" layer) is useful but probably commoditises. The analogue of the Encyclopédie did not capture the rents.
- Durable value probably lies in: (a) the certification and sign-off layer (a modern "guild mark": AI-assisted inspection, return-to-service records accepted by regulators); (b) networks that pair codified knowledge with skilled field technicians (the "mechanics" in Mokyr's sense); (c) shared field-failure data (the Lean's Engine Reporter model); (d) pushing through legal or regulatory unlocks, as right-to-repair is doing now (by analogy with Turgot and Le Chapelier).
