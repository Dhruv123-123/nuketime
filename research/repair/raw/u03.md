## Findings

### Share of events due to maintenance error
1. RECALLED. Boeing, Rankin, "MEDA Investigation Process", Aero Quarterly Q2 2007 (boeing.com/commercial/aeromagazine). Maintenance was a contributing factor in about 15% of commercial jet hull-loss accidents (Boeing's analysis of 1982–1991 accidents). The same article says maintenance error causes about 20–30% of engine in-flight shutdowns, costing up to about US$500k each, and about 50% of engine-related flight delays and cancellations. It also says MEDA investigations find on average about 4 contributing factors per error. Hard data, from an OEM.
2. VERIFIED-THIS-SESSION (via Wikipedia, "Maintenance resource management", citing a USAF Safety Center fact sheet and a 2002 Naval Safety Center review). About 18% of USAF aircraft mishaps in 1992–2002 were directly attributable to maintenance human error. US Coast Guard 2002 data put maintenance error in about one in five aviation mishaps. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maintenance_resource_management
3. RECALLED. UK CAA CAP 1367, "Aircraft Maintenance Incident Analysis" (2016), analysed UK MORs over about a decade. The dominant occurrence types were incorrect installation of components, incomplete installation, and parts or tools left in place or panels not secured. Fitting the wrong part was a smaller category. The point that matters: most events are execution and omission errors, not "didn't know how" errors.

### Error types: mostly omissions and slips, not knowledge
4. RECALLED. James Reason, Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents (1997), and Reason and Hobbs, Managing Maintenance Error (2003). In an analysis of a major carrier's maintenance lapses, omissions were about 56% of errors, incorrect installations about 30%, wrong parts about 8%, and other about 6%. Reassembly and installation is the error-prone phase, not disassembly or diagnosis. Hard data, a classic study.
5. RECALLED. Hobbs and Williamson, "Associations between errors and contributing factors in aircraft maintenance", Human Factors 45(2), 2003. Covers about 600 Australian maintenance occurrences. Memory lapses were the most common error type and were associated with fatigue, time pressure and interruptions. Rule violations were associated with pressure, poor procedures and the organisational norm. Knowledge-based errors were associated with inadequate training or knowledge, but were a minority category. Hobbs' later ATSB overview (AR-2008-055, https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-12/ar2008055.pdf, 403 this session) reports the same pattern. This is the key evidence on the question "would better knowledge fix them?". The answer is: only the knowledge-based minority.
6. RECALLED. McDonald et al. (2000), Trinity College Dublin, EU ADAMS project. About a third (about 34%) of routine maintenance tasks were carried out in a way that differed from the documented procedure. Technicians often believed their own method was better or faster. Procedural non-compliance is common and often deliberate: the knowledge exists in the manual but is not used.

### Causes in landmark accidents: knowledge was usually available and not used
7. VERIFIED-THIS-SESSION. BA Flight 5390 (1990, AAIB). 84 of about 90 windscreen bolts were about 0.66 mm too small in diameter, and 6 were too short. The shift maintenance manager picked replacements by matching them against the old bolts, "without reference to maintenance documentation", under time pressure before departure. The AAIB faulted company policy for having no independent check on a single-person critical task, and faulted the lack of supervisory monitoring. This is a parts-sourcing and identification error: the correct part number was in the documentation. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways_Flight_5390
8. VERIFIED-THIS-SESSION. Continental Express 2574 (1991, NTSB). Stabiliser leading-edge screws were removed and never replaced after a shift change. The NTSB cited failure to follow procedures, management failure to enforce compliance, and corporate culture (Lauber). Handover and communication failure, not lack of knowledge. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Express_Flight_2574
9. VERIFIED-THIS-SESSION. Air Midwest 5481 (2003, NTSB). A contractor mechanic who had never worked on the type mis-rigged the elevator cables under supervised on-the-job training. The supervisor skipped the post-rigging check and was also acting as the QA inspector. The FAA knew of training deficiencies at the facility. This case is partly a genuine knowledge and training gap, but the barrier that failed was the skipped inspection. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Midwest_Flight_5481
10. VERIFIED-THIS-SESSION. Alaska 261 (2000, NTSB). Jackscrew lubrication and end-play check intervals were extended without adequate technical data, a 4-hour lubrication task was done in about 1 hour, and FAA oversight was deficient. The NTSB tied the extensions to cost-cutting. These were organisational and economic decisions, not ignorance. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Airlines_Flight_261
11. VERIFIED-THIS-SESSION. American 191 (1979, NTSB). Airlines took off the engine and pylon together with a forklift instead of following McDonnell Douglas' procedure, which saved about 200 labour hours per aircraft. The manufacturer had warned against the method but "had no authority to approve or disapprove a customer's maintenance methods". Deficient information-sharing among operator, OEM and FAA meant earlier damage events were not passed on. This is the one case where an information-flow gap between organisations shows up directly. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines_Flight_191
12. RECALLED. Japan Air Lines 123 (1985). The faulty aft pressure-bulkhead repair was done by Boeing's own repair team, which used a single splice plate instead of the continuous plate required. The OEM's own specialists made the fatal error. This counters the idea that OEM knowledge equals reliable repair.
13. RECALLED. Alaska 1282 door-plug blowout (Jan 2024; NTSB final 2025). The bolts were removed during rework at Boeing's Renton factory and not reinstalled, and no removal record was created. The NTSB pointed to Boeing's deficient training, guidance and oversight and its parts-removal documentation practices. This is a modern case showing that work-tracking and documentation systems, not technical know-how, are the failure point, even at the OEM.

### Programmatic responses (practitioner view)
14. RECALLED. FAA maintenance human factors programme and Gordon Dupont's "Dirty Dozen" (Transport Canada, 1993): lack of communication, complacency, lack of knowledge, distraction, lack of teamwork, fatigue, lack of resources, pressure, lack of assertiveness, stress, lack of awareness, and norms. Only 1 of the 12 is "lack of knowledge". Regulators and industry answered with Maintenance Resource Management (FAA AC 120-72), fatigue risk management, required independent inspection (RII / duplicate inspection), and non-punitive reporting (MEDA, ASAP). Design for maintainability (error-proof connectors and keyed parts) came later. Both are aimed at execution, memory and culture, not at knowledge.

## Reasoning: would better knowledge (AI) fix them?
- Rough decomposition, from findings 4–6 and 14. Most maintenance errors are omissions and memory lapses (about 50%+), deliberate procedural deviations (about a third of tasks deviate), and errors driven by communication or handover failures. Knowledge-based mistakes are a minority, plausibly 10–20%. This is my estimate from the literature pattern, not a measured figure.
- AI could help with several of these, but mainly as a job aid and work-state tracker, not as a store of knowledge. Possible uses: step-by-step guidance with confirmation of each step, shift-handover state, part-number verification at the point of fitting (BA 5390), interruption recovery, and fatigue-aware scheduling. That is closer to the "procedures and documentation usability" fix that human-factors researchers have pushed for decades (FAA document design research, the poka-yoke idea). Some partial evidence from the literature is that improved, usable task cards and electronic task cards reduce errors.
- Danger: AI-generated or "understanding" content that departs from approved data is itself a non-compliance risk. Aviation requires approved data (AMM/IPC). An unverified AI procedure looks like the Alaska 261 interval extensions: "not supported by adequate technical data".
- In every case above, the decisive missing barrier was inspection, independent check or oversight (BA 5390, Air Midwest, Continental 2574, Alaska 261). This strongly supports C6.

## Stances
- C1 mixed: the chain fits, but most error enters at the repair-execution and reassembly step and the handover between steps, which C1 treats as trivial.
- C2 mostly contradicted for aviation safety: the knowledge usually existed in manuals. The failures were use of the knowledge, procedures, time pressure, fatigue, communication and economics. American 191 is a partial support case (OEM-operator information flow).
- C3 contradicted: removing the knowledge gap does not remove the main error modes.
- C4 silent to mixed: no cost data here. Errors are concentrated in execution, so cheaper diagnosis and understanding does not address the main safety cost.
- C5 contradicted by analogy: wrong-part (BA 5390) and unapproved-method (AA191, JAL123) cases show that bespoke or non-standard parts and methods are where certification risk lives.
- C6 strongly supports.
- C7 mixed: the inefficiency is partly deliberate redundancy (duplicate inspections and RII are built-in "inefficiency" that buys safety). Some genuine waste exists, such as procedure usability and paper-based handovers.
