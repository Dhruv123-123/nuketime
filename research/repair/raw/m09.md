# m09: Semiconductor fab tool service (Applied, Lam, ASML, KLA service revenue; third-party refurbishers): how is the knowledge locked and what is downtime cost?

Date of research: 2026-10-20.

## Method and limits

- The shared WebSearch budget for this run was already used up when this agent started (every search call came back "limit reached"). I did not get around the limit. Instead I went straight to primary documents at known addresses: SEC EDGAR annual filings (10-K / 20-F), read in full and searched for keywords, plus the Federal Register / govinfo text of the October 2022 BIS export-control rule and a few Wikipedia pages used only to get dates.
- Most numbers below are therefore **hard data from audited filings**. What is missing: practitioner and analyst opinion (trade-press interviews, analyst notes on third-party service share, SEMI E10 / OEE survey numbers). Where I give a number from memory or work one out myself, it is labelled.

## Findings

### F1. OEM service is a ~$27B/yr business across four firms, ~23-36% of their revenue, growing faster than systems in most cases (data)
- **Applied Materials, FY2025 (to Oct 26 2025):** Applied Global Services (AGS) net revenue was $6,385M, 23% of the $28,368M total (FY24: $6,225M). AGS growth came from "higher customer spending on long-term service agreements and spares." Source: AMAT 10-K FY2025, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/6951/000162828025056742/amat-20251026.htm
- **Lam Research, FY2026 (to Jun 28 2026):** "Customer support-related revenue and other" (CSBG: services, spares, upgrades, Reliant non-leading-edge and refurbished tools) was $8,347M, 36% of the $23,233M total. FY25 was $6,944M and FY24 $5,984M, so it rose 20.2% "mainly due to revenue from spares and non-leading-edge equipment." Source: Lam 10-K FY2026, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/707549/000070754926000037/lrcx-20260628.htm
- **KLA, FY2026 (to Jun 30 2026):** service revenue was $3,126M, about 23% of $13,579M total, up 16% "due to growth in our installed base of tools" (FY25 $2,683M, FY24 $2,330M). Source: KLA 10-K FY2026, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/319201/000031920126000027/klac-20260630.htm
- **ASML, CY2025:** "Net service and field option sales" were EUR 8,193M, 25.1% of EUR 32,667M total, up 26.2%, against 12.4% for systems. ASML gives the reasons as the growing installed base, "higher levels of lithography tool use," and NXE field upgrades. 2023: EUR 5,620M; 2024: EUR 6,494M. Source: ASML 2025 Annual Report / 20-F, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/937966/000162828026011378/asml-20251231.htm
- My total: about $6.4B + $8.3B + $3.1B + ~$9.2B (EUR converted at ~1.12) = **~$27B/yr** of OEM aftermarket revenue at four Western OEMs. Tokyo Electron's field-solutions business is extra and was not checked.

### F2. Service is not uniformly a high-margin "monopoly rent". At Applied it earns less than new tools (data; partly contradicts C2/C7)
- AMAT FY2025 segment table: AGS gross margin **33.4%** (gross profit $2,134M on $6,385M) and operating margin **28.1%**. Semiconductor Systems: gross margin **54.2%**, operating margin **35.5%**. AGS operating margin also fell from 29.1% to 28.1%.
- ASML 2025: service and field option gross margin is (8,193 - 4,025.3) / 8,193 = **~50.9%**. Systems: (24,474 - 11,384) / 24,474 = **~53.5%**. So service roughly matches systems; it does not exceed them.
- Reading: a large part of the service price pays for real labour and logistics: field engineers next to every fab around the clock, and parts inventories (AGS holds $2.3B of inventory). It is not pure information rent. A "knowledge monopoly" story would predict service margins far above product margins. At AMAT and ASML they are equal or lower. The pure-knowledge part (software, upgrades, Equipment Intelligence) may carry high margins, but it is not reported separately.

### F3. The lock is installed base plus qualification, not only manuals (data from filings)
- Lam 10-K: "once a semiconductor manufacturer has selected a particular supplier's equipment and qualified it for production, the manufacturer generally maintains that selection for that specific production application and technology node as long as the supplier's products demonstrate performance to specification in the installed base."
- The same logic applies to parts. A spare from a non-OEM source has to be requalified against the process recipe. When the cost of that requalification and its yield risk are weighed against ~$8k average revenue per wafer (F8), a fab will usually pay the OEM premium. (This is my inference; the filings do not quantify requalification cost.)
- Lam also says it must keep its installed base satisfied "through the delivery of high-quality and cost-efficient spare parts **in the presence of competition from third-party spare parts providers**." A third-party parts market therefore exists, and the OEM names it as competition.

### F4. OEMs are actively moving service to multi-year subscriptions, which deepens lock-in (data plus strategy statements)
- AMAT 10-K: "Our strategy is to continue to shift the AGS' service and spares business to a subscription agreement model ... providing us a more predictable revenue stream." Demand is expected to grow "as our installed base of systems and chambers increases and customers renew long-term service agreements," and it is driven by the "**growing service intensity of newer tools**." AGS backlog was $7,141M, 48% of the company's $15.0B backlog.
- ASML revenue note: service contracts are "stand-ready obligations," and "for service contracts where the price is not fixed, the transaction price has a variable component that is based on the performance of the system." In other words, uptime-linked pricing already exists.
- (Not verified this session; from memory of AMAT investor-day material, roughly 2021-2024: more than 60% of AGS revenue under long-term agreements, with renewal rates above 90%. Treat as unconfirmed.)
- Reading: each new tool generation needs more service ("service intensity"). Newer tools get more complex, not less, so the knowledge gap can grow at the leading edge even while it shrinks for old tools.

### F5. Lithography service is very labour-heavy: about 10,000 ASML customer-support staff, with AI already used inside the OEM (data)
- ASML 2025 AR: "With around 10,000 customer support employees, including service engineers and applications specialists, we work around the clock." Customer Support and Sales headcount was 10,235 FTE out of a total of 44,209 at end-2025.
- ASML: "Our customer support engineers also use AI in the field, for example with machine diagnostics that can make preventive maintenance more efficient." It also runs a "Customer Support Academy" and tracks "time-to-competence" to close skill gaps.
- Reading for C3: the incumbent is applying AI to diagnosis first. If AI makes diagnosis cheaper, the first beneficiary is the OEM, which owns the tool telemetry, the fleet data and the design files.

### F6. Even the OEM loses knowledge: ASML's own lifetime-extension program exists partly because engineers are retiring (data; supports C2's "knowledge gap" framing, cuts against "knowledge monopoly")
- ASML: the PAS 5500 (introduced early 1990s, ~1,800 units being refurbished) is supported "until at least 2035" through the PAS Life Time Extension program. That program "addresses challenges such as obsolete electronics, limited end-of-life support from suppliers and **loss of domain knowledge as experienced engineers retire**." It "involves redesi[gning]" parts.
- ASML guarantees more than 2,000 service parts for PAS/XT/NXT platforms, tracks expected run-out dates, and redesigns parts (especially electronics) before they become obsolete. About 200 TWINSCAN AT systems are managed toward end of life, "guaranteeing the availability of spare parts for as long as possible."
- About 95% of all ASML systems sold in the last 30 years were still in use in 2025. ASML has refurbished and resold more than 600 systems, 27 of them in 2025. ASML also estimates that **of machines no longer serviced by ASML, 33% are still active**. So some old tools keep running under self-maintenance or third-party maintenance.
- Reading: on legacy tools the binding problem is **obsolete components and engineers retiring**, not one firm hoarding knowledge. This is the real-world version of C5 (bespoke parts when the supplier is gone). Note that the OEM itself solves it through redesign, not ad-hoc fabrication.

### F7. Part obsolescence moves down the supply chain (data)
- KLA 10-K: suppliers that discontinue parts can force KLA into large one-time "end-of-life" buys, with possible inventory write-offs. It cites "a few large suppliers have discontinued manufacturing certain DRAM chips that are incorporated in a number of our products."
- AMAT 10-K risk factors list "manufacturers' ability to reconfigure and re-use equipment, resulting in diminished need to purchase new equipment and services from us, **and challenges in providing parts for reused equipment**," and "the availability of spare parts to maximize the time that customers' systems are available for production."
- Reading: the scarce layer for old tools is qualified, obsolete electronic and mechanical parts. Some of these can be redesigned or remanufactured, which is a real opening for C5-style firms, but each redesign still needs requalification.

### F8. Downtime cost: fab-level losses run to tens of millions of dollars per day (hard data) and roughly $1,000s to $10,000s per tool-hour (my estimate)
- **TSMC Aug 2018 virus:** malware arrived on a new tool during installation ("misoperation by our staff when installing a new equipment that contained malicious software"). It caused "malfunction of a number of our computer systems and fab tools," and the affected tools were recovered by Aug 6, about 3 days. The loss was **NT$2,596M (US$85M)** in cost of revenue. Source: TSMC 2018 20-F, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1046179/000119312519108390/d665387d20f.htm
- **TSMC Jan 2019 photoresist contamination** (12/16nm): estimated loss **NT$3,400M** (~US$110M). Same source.
- **TSMC earthquakes:** losses net of insurance were ~**NT$3B** (Q2 2024) and ~**NT$5.3B** (~US$170M; Q1 2025). Source: TSMC 2025 20-F, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1046179/000162828026025362/tsm-20251231.htm
- **TSMC 2025 baseline:** revenue US$121.4B, gross margin 59.9%, 15.02M 12-inch-equivalent wafers shipped. That is **~$13.9M revenue per hour** company-wide, **~$8,080 average revenue per wafer**, and ~1,700 wafers/hour. Same source; my arithmetic.
- **Per-tool estimate (my derivation, not sourced):** an EUV scanner costs up to ~$200M (NXE:3600D) and High-NA ~$370M (figures via the Wikipedia ASML article). Over a 5-year depreciation life that is ~$40M/yr, or about $4,500/hour, in capital cost alone, before lost output. In a litho-constrained fab, an hour of bottleneck-scanner downtime loses throughput at fab margin, so the true cost per bottleneck tool-hour is plausibly tens of thousands of dollars. Non-bottleneck tools that have redundancy cost much less. Downtime cost is **dominated by whether the tool is the bottleneck**, not by the repair cost itself.
- Reading for C4: on the cost side, the four "intermediate" steps (diagnose, understand, source, deliver) matter because each hour of downtime is extremely expensive. That is exactly why fabs already pay for on-site OEM engineers and consignment spares: they buy speed, not knowledge.

### F9. Governments treat servicing know-how as a national-security chokepoint (regulator)
- The BIS Oct 7 2022 interim final rule (87 FR 62186) put US persons' "support" under licence. "Support" explicitly includes "**servicing** items not subject to the EAR" in the PRC when that would assist production of advanced logic, NAND or DRAM. It covers "operation, installation (including on-site installation), maintenance (checking), repair, overhaul, or refurbishing" of items used in IC production. Licences for PRC-owned fabs face a presumption of denial. Effective Oct 12 2022. Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-10-13/html/2022-21658.htm
- ASML 2025 AR: US measures include "limitations on support by US persons for non-US origin items destined for advanced-node fabs in China," which "have restricted, and may continue to restrict, our ability to supply specific products and services." Lam: export controls are "restricting the sales of equipment and spare parts by U.S. equipment suppliers." AMAT: it needs licences to "provide services to" certain Chinese customers.
- Reading: this is the strongest evidence that service knowledge (people and parts) is a real, separate lever. Regulators restrict *servicing*, not just tool sales, because cutting off service degrades an installed fab. **If AI had made the knowledge gap "essentially gone," restricting service would be pointless.**

### F10. The China natural experiment: rebuilding the service and equipment layer without OEMs takes years and billions (news, via Wikipedia summaries of Reuters/SCMP)
- YMTC was added to the Entity List on Dec 15 2022. By Feb 2023 it had cut ~10% of staff and cancelled equipment orders. By Oct 2023 a new fundraising was needed partly to cover "replacing U.S. equipment," which had used up an earlier $7B. Reuters (Jul 2026, per Wikipedia): since 2022 YMTC had replaced "around half of its equipment with Chinese-made machinery." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangtze_Memory_Technologies (secondary; Reuters/SCMP are primary)
- Dec 2025 (Reuters, per Wikipedia ASML article): China built a prototype EUV machine in Shenzhen "with help from former ASML engineers," with working chips expected 2028-2030. Knowledge moved **through people**, not documents.
- Lam 10-K: Chinese government initiatives "could increase competition from domestic equipment and spare parts manufacturers in those countries." China was 34% of Lam's FY2026 revenue (42% in FY24).
- Reading: a well-funded, motivated actor *can* break the OEM service lock. It takes ~3-4+ years, billions of dollars, and the hiring of tacit-knowledge holders. AI did not make this fast. That contradicts C3's "essentially gone" for leading-edge tools.

### F11. Third-party ecosystem exists but is small and works at the parts/consumables level (data)
- Ultra Clean Holdings (UCT) FY2025 Services segment covers "ultra-high purity parts cleaning, process tool part recoating, surface encapsulation and high sensitivity micro contamination analysis" for chipmakers and OEMs. Services cost of revenue was $184.1M at 72.3% of services revenue, implying **~$255M revenue and ~28% gross margin**. In 2025 UCT took a $73.5M goodwill impairment on Services. Source: UCT 10-K FY2025, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1275014/000162828026010744/uctt-20251226.htm
- Compared with ~$27B of OEM service revenue (F1), the visible independent layer is about 1% of OEM service, and its margins are thin.
- OEMs themselves sell refurbished tools: Lam Reliant, AMAT's 200mm business (moved into Systems from FY2026), KLA "refurbished systems, remanufactured legacy systems," and ASML (600+ refurbished systems). **The OEMs have absorbed the refurbishment market** rather than ceded it.
- AMAT warns about "manufacture or sale of equipment or spare parts that use our technology without authorization." OEMs treat third-party copies of parts as an IP problem.
- (Not verified this session: independent brokers and refurbishers such as SurplusGLOBAL, and many Asian 200mm specialists, work mainly in legacy 150/200mm tools.)

### F12. Security angle: the service channel is itself an attack surface (data)
- The TSMC 2018 virus came in on an OEM tool during installation (F8). TSMC then built "an automated virus-scan system to prevent fab from installing virus infected tools" (2019 20-F). Lam's 2026 10-K adds risks from AI agents, such as "prompt injection" and "compromise of valuable intellectual property."
- Reading for C6: inspection and verification of tools, software and service actions entering a fab is a real and growing need. Any AI-mediated remote diagnosis becomes a new security exposure that fabs will gate tightly.

## Stance by claim

- **C1 (universal chain): mixed.** The steps exist, but at fabs they are not sequential. Detection and diagnosis are continuous (sensor fleets, Equipment Intelligence). Parts are pre-positioned on consignment, engineers are already on site, and "return to service" requires **requalification against process specifications** (yield and recipe checks), which is the fab's equivalent of safety inspection. A lot of the value sits outside the chain entirely: upgrades and field options that improve tool performance rather than repair it.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed, leaning supports.** The lock is real: qualification lock-in, trade secrets, subscriptions, and export controls that treat servicing as a chokepoint. But part of the gap is OEMs losing knowledge too (ASML engineers retiring), and AGS margins below systems margins suggest service pricing is largely labour and logistics, not pure rent.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicts** for leading edge. Newer tools have "growing service intensity." OEMs own the telemetry and are deploying AI themselves. China's rebuild took years and depended on hiring former OEM engineers. AI may narrow the gap for documented legacy tools.
- **C4 (10x cost drop in the four middle steps): mixed.** The steps' share of cost is large because downtime costs about $28M per day at TSMC scale. But fabs already buy speed (on-site engineers, consignment spares). The remaining waste is in requalification and in the bottleneck. A 10x drop in diagnose plus source cost would matter less than a 10x drop in downtime-hours.
- **C5 (bespoke AI parts factory solves obsolescence): mixed.** Obsolescence is real: KLA's end-of-life DRAM buys, ASML redesigns, AMAT's "challenges in providing parts for reused equipment". But the parts must be ultra-high-purity, contamination-qualified and recipe-qualified. ASML's answer is engineered redesign, not rapid fabrication. There is an opening in legacy 200mm, constrained by qualification and IP claims.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports.** Requalification, contamination analysis (UCT's micro-contamination lab services), malware screening of tools (TSMC), and AI-agent security risks (Lam) all point to a growing verification layer.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): contradicts / mixed.** Fab tool service is arguably the *most* optimised repair ecosystem anywhere: about 10k ASML field staff working around the clock, uptime-linked contracts, fleet analytics, and 95% of 30-year-old systems still running with OEM-run lifetime-extension programs. It is costly because downtime is costly. It is structurally *concentrated*, not structurally inefficient.

## Where money and time go
- Money: OEM service ~$27B/yr, with AGS at 33% gross margin, so most of the price covers labour and parts logistics. The fab's real cost is lost output while a bottleneck tool is down (TSMC: ~$13.9M revenue/hour company-wide) and scrapped wafers from process excursions (2019 photoresist event: ~US$110M).
- Time: in fabs, time goes into recovery and requalification and into waiting for qualified parts on obsolete platforms, not into initial diagnosis, which is already instrumented.

## Real bottleneck
Qualified tacit expertise (field and applications engineers, a scarce and export-controlled resource) combined with process requalification of any part or repair, sitting behind OEM ownership of tool telemetry and design data. For legacy tools, the binding constraint is obsolete components and retiring engineers.

## Company-shape implications (speculative)
1. Legacy-fab (150/200mm, "More than Moore") lifetime-extension provider: reverse-engineered, requalified replacement subassemblies and electronics for orphaned tools, plus AI-assisted knowledge capture from retiring engineers. The demand evidence is ASML's PAS-LTE, KLA's end-of-life buys, and the 33% of non-ASML-serviced tools still active. Expect pushback from OEMs on IP and the fact that OEMs already have refurbishment businesses.
2. Fab-side (not OEM-side) multi-vendor tool health and diagnostics layer, which gives the fab leverage in subscription negotiations. The barrier is OEM data access and the security gating of the fab.
3. Tool and service-action security and verification (malware screening, AI-agent action audit) for fabs: a C6 opening, supported by TSMC's 2018 incident.
4. Non-China sovereign or second-source service capacity (US/EU/Japan/India new fabs, CHIPS-funded) facing engineer shortages: training and AI copilots for fab technicians.

## Surprises
- Applied's service segment has *lower* gross margin (33%) than its systems (54%). Service is not obviously the profit monopoly that a right-to-repair framing would predict.
- ASML admits "loss of domain knowledge as experienced engineers retire" as a driver of its own lifetime-extension program. The knowledge holder is also losing knowledge.
- Export controls regulate *servicing* as a weapon, which is the clearest proof that repair knowledge is a strategic asset.
- The TSMC 2018 shutdown was caused *by* the tool-installation and service channel itself (malware on a new tool).
- ASML already prices some service contracts on system performance (outcome-based).
