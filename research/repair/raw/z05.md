## 1. Theory: what "diagnosis opacity" means in this literature

- **Credence good** (Darby & Karni 1973; surveyed in Dulleck & Kerschbamer 2006, "On Doctors, Mechanics, and Computer Specialists", JEL 44(1)). The expert both diagnoses the customer's need and sells the treatment. Even after treatment, the customer may never learn what was needed. There are three fraud types:
  - **overtreatment**: more or pricier treatment than needed
  - **undertreatment**: insufficient treatment
  - **overcharging**: billing for work not done
- Dulleck & Kerschbamer's unifying model says fraud disappears when either of two conditions holds:
  - (a) **liability**: the expert must fix the problem
  - (b) **verifiability**: the customer can verify what was billed

  It also needs homogeneous customers and no economies of scope between diagnosis and treatment. Fraud arises when these assumptions break.
- **Taylor (1995), "The Economics of Breakdowns, Checkups, and Cures", JPE 103(1).** Under short-term contracts the expert recommends treatment for all customers whatever their true state, so every customer whose car is not faulty is overcharged (as summarised in Rasch & Waibel 2015, p. 4). Commitment to long-term contracts or bundled pricing reduces this. Source pointer: https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261958 (403 on fetch; summary taken via Rasch & Waibel).
- **Key implication for the founder:** in this theory the problem is not only that the customer lacks knowledge. Three things together create the problem:
  - the diagnoser is also the seller
  - the customer cannot verify, ex post, what was needed or done
  - billing rules are weak

  Customer knowledge (an AI second opinion) attacks only the "customer type is unobservable" part. It leaves the incentive structure in place.

## 2. Hard empirical findings (field and lab)

### F1. Insurance, not ignorance, produced the largest measured overcharge. Kerschbamer, Neururer & Sutter (2016), PNAS 113(27):7454-7458
- Design: a natural field experiment with computer repair shops in Vienna. Each shop got a laptop with the same simple defect.
- Control bills averaged about EUR 70.
- When the "customer" mentioned that an insurer would pay, **the repair bill rose by more than 80%**. The increase came mainly from overprovision of parts and overcharged labour time.
- A survey of shop owners attributed this to insured customers caring less about cost.
- Source: Europe PMC record (DOI 10.1073/pnas.1518015113, PMC4941439), https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC4941439 (also pnas.org).
- **Interpretation:** overcharging scales with who pays, not only with who knows. Diagnosis opacity is the precondition. The size of the extraction is set by payer structure (third-party payment, moral hazard). This is directly relevant to fleet, aviation and insured repair, where a third party pays.

### F2. German garage audit (ADAC). Rasch & Waibel (2018), "What Drives Fraud in a Credence Goods Market? Evidence from a Field Study", Oxford Bulletin of Econ & Stats 80(3):605-624 (WP: DICE DP 180, 2015)
- The ADAC (German automobile club) sent test cars with 5 planted, easy-to-detect faults to garages. All 5 faults are on carmakers' standard inspection checklists:
  - a dead licence plate lamp
  - low spare-wheel pressure
  - a loose exhaust
  - low coolant
  - a mis-aimed headlight
- The 303 garages tested were filtered to 134 corporate garages.
- **Only 6 of 134 (4.5%) billed for repairs not performed.** The authors stress that this is a lower bound: they cannot see pricier-than-performed repairs.
- **Average competence: 4.24 of 5 planted faults found.** Garages miss about 15% of easy, checklist-listed faults.
- Garages that overcharged were significantly less competent (p=0.007).
- More competition (more garages within 10 km, which means cheaper second opinions) lowered overcharging. Financial distress and low reputational concern (sites near motorways with one-off customers) raised it.
- Source: https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/dicedp/180.html; full text https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/109212/1/822346028.pdf
- **Interpretation:** outright billing fraud at mainstream garages is single-digit percent. The bigger, and roughly equal, failure is incompetence: missed faults, which is undertreatment. Low competence and fraud go together.

### F3. US undercover garage visits. Schneider (2012), "Agency Problems and Reputation in Expert Services: Evidence from Auto Repair", J. Industrial Economics 60(3):406-433
- Undercover visits to garages with a car that had known pre-set conditions.
- Summary from Rasch & Waibel:
  - "pervasive overtreatment and undertreatment", but **no evidence that reputation (signalling a repeat customer) reduces these problems**
  - **overcharging occurred in 3 of 51 visits (6%)**
  - average overcharge was **$32 per incident**, and summed overcharges were **2% of total charges**
  - garages with spare capacity at the time of the visit were more likely to recommend repairs. That is demand-driven overtreatment: idle bays generate recommendations.
- Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jindec/v60y2012i3p406-433.html (abstract not public); numbers via Rasch & Waibel 2015, footnotes 6 and 28.
- **Interpretation:** strictly defined overcharging is a small share of the repair bill (2%). Over- and under-treatment, which are diagnosis-quality problems, are "pervasive", but their dollar size is harder to measure.

### F4. Price knowledge and gender in phone quotes. Busse, Israeli & Zettelmeyer (2017), "Repairing the Damage: The Effect of Price Knowledge and Gender on Auto Repair Price Quotes", JMR 54(1):75-95 (NBER w19154)
- A large field experiment: callers asked garages for a quote on a fixed, already-diagnosed repair.
- Shops quote higher to callers who cite a higher expected price.
- Women who signal they are uninformed get higher quotes than men. **The gender gap disappears when the caller cites a market price.**
- Women are more likely to win a concession when they ask.
- Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w19154
- [memory, unverified] Effect sizes are on the order of tens of dollars on a ~$365-$400 radiator job, i.e. single-digit to ~10% price differences.
- **Interpretation:** this is price opacity, not diagnosis opacity (the repair was already named). Simple information such as an AI or price app can remove this layer, but the layer is roughly 5-10% of the bill, not 10x.

### F5. Athens taxis as a credence-goods field test. Balafoutas, Beck, Kerschbamer & Sutter (2013), RES 80(3):876-891
- Passengers who seem not to know the optimal route get significantly longer detours (overtreatment).
- Passengers who seem unfamiliar with the tariff system face about a **15-percentage-point** higher chance of a manipulated bill (overcharging).
- Perceived income had no detectable effect.
- Follow-up (Balafoutas et al. 2015, via Rasch & Waibel): passengers who say their employer will reimburse them are **about 13% more likely** to be overcharged.
- Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v80y2013i3p876-891.html
- **Interpretation:** when experts can spot an ignorant customer, they extract more. Customer information visibly lowers fraud. The second-largest lever is again the payer (reimbursement).

### F6. Lab evidence on which institution matters. Dulleck, Kerschbamer & Sutter (2011), "The Economics of Credence Goods: An Experiment on the Role of Liability, Verifiability, Reputation, and Competition", AER 101(2):526-555
- 936 participants.
- **Liability (the expert must actually fix the problem) is crucial for efficiency.**
- Verifiability (the customer can check what was billed) has at best a minor effect.
- Reputation has little effect.
- Competition lowers prices but does not improve efficiency when liability is absent.
- Source: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.2.526
- **Interpretation:** this is the most important counterweight to C3/C4. Making information transparent ("verifiability") did little in the lab. Outcome guarantees ("liability") did a lot. An AI that tells the customer what is wrong is a verifiability or second-opinion tool. A business that guarantees the fix (fixed price, pay on success) is the institution that works.

### F7. Reputation in emissions inspection. Hubbard (2002), "How Do Consumers Motivate Experts? Reputational Incentives in an Auto Repair Market", J. Law & Econ 45(2):437-468; and Hubbard (1998), RAND J. Econ 29(2):406-426
- California smog-check data.
- Consumers return **about 30% more often** to a station that passed them than to one that failed them.
- Demand responds to a station's overall failure rate. Consumers therefore discipline inspectors toward leniency, so inspection is under-strict.
- Inspector behaviour differs by firm organisation, in line with agency theory.
- Sources: https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jlawec/v45y2002i2p437-68.html ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v29y1998isummerp406-426.html
- **Interpretation (C6):** when the inspector is chosen and paid by the inspected party, inspection degrades. The incentive design of inspection matters as much as its technology.

### F8. Other lab results relevant to unbundling diagnosis from repair
- **Greiner, Zhang & Tang (2017), Health Economics 26 (Suppl. 3).** Separating prescription (diagnosis) from treatment reduced overtreatment but increased undertreatment. Independent pricing lowered efficiency through coordination failures.
  - Implication: unbundling diagnosis from repair is not a free win.
- **Angerer, Glätzle-Rützler & Waibel (2021), Health Economics 30(5).** Monitoring that the customer can request reduced both undertreatment and overcharging, even when monitoring was costly. Random third-party monitoring helped only if frequent.
  - Implication: a cheap, on-demand audit (AI) is the kind of monitoring that works.
- **Kerschbamer, Sutter & Dulleck (2017), Economic Journal 127(600).** Equal-markup pricing, which theory says removes the incentive to cheat, fails because sellers' social preferences differ.
- Source: Europe PMC search API results (pmid records).

### F9. Regulator view on structural (not just informational) restriction. FTC (May 2021), "Nixing the Fix: An FTC Report to Congress on Repair Restrictions"
- "there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions."
- Diagnostic software and firmware "are often necessary today to make repairs". Some manufacturers limit access, or add code that prevents independent shops from developing alternative diagnostic software.
- Price evidence:
  - independent servicers maintain diagnostic imaging equipment for **$150-$250/hour**, against **$500-$600/hour (four-hour minimum)** for OEM servicing (IAMERS)
  - alternative collision parts are **15-50% cheaper** than car-company parts (ABPA)
  - parts competition cut OEM auto parts prices by **about 8%**
  - "none of the comments or empirical research ... rebuts the right to repair advocates' argument that repair restrictions increase the price consumers pay for repairs."
  - Apple cut out-of-warranty battery replacement from **$79 to $29** in 2018.
- Source: https://www.ftc.gov/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions (PDF read in full)
- **Interpretation (C2):** what the FTC documents is a monopoly over access (tools, software, parts, part pairing), not purely over knowledge. A 2-4x price gap (OEM versus independent hourly rates) is close to the founder's order-of-magnitude intuition, but it comes from control of tools, software and parts, not from diagnosis fraud.

### F10. Consumer-complaint and survey context (cited in Rasch & Waibel 2015)
- In the 2010 joint survey by CFA, NACAA and NACPI, faulty auto repairs ranked **#1** among consumer complaints.
- California's Bureau of Automotive Repair shut down shops of one chain over overcharging and overtreatment.
- 2002 COMsciences poll: **74%** of consumers "fear they are often cheated by auto body repair shops".
- Market sizes: about $52bn US auto mechanics revenue in 2012 (IBISWorld), and about EUR 30bn German car repair turnover.
- The FBI estimates **up to 10%** of US health-care spending is fraudulent. This is the largest credence-goods market, and it gives a ceiling-type benchmark.
- **Interpretation:** perceived fraud (74% fear being cheated) is far higher than measured fraud (2-6% of garages or charges). The distrust is itself a cost: search, delay and deferred maintenance.

### F11. [memory, unverified] Swiss dentists. Gottschalk, Mimra & Waibel (2020), "Health Services as Credence Goods: A Field Experiment", Economic Journal 130(629)
- A healthy undercover patient visited about 180 dentists.
- **About 28%** recommended unnecessary treatment.
- Overtreatment varied with dentist characteristics, and price did not explain it.

### F12. [memory, unverified] Historical US estimate
- US DOT studies in the late 1970s were widely cited as finding that **about 53 cents of every auto-repair dollar** was wasted (unneeded, overpriced or faulty work).
- This was a contested, survey-based estimate, often quoted at around $20-40bn a year in Senate hearings. It is much higher than modern undercover-audit measures (2-6%), so treat it as an upper historical bound, not a current fact.

## 3. Answer to the question: how much overcharging comes from diagnosis opacity?

- **Strict overcharging (billing for work not done) at mainstream garages is small.** It is about 4.5-6% of garages (ADAC; Schneider) and about **2% of total charges** (Schneider).
- **Overtreatment and undertreatment are "pervasive" (Schneider).** Undertreatment from incompetence is at least as large: garages miss about 15% of easy faults. Dentists overtreated about 28% of healthy patients [unverified]. These are diagnosis-quality failures, not only diagnosis-honesty failures.
- **Information signals by the customer move prices by roughly 5-15%:**
  - Busse et al.: the gender quote gap vanishes when a price is cited
  - Athens taxis: +15pp manipulated bills when the passenger seems uninformed
- **Payer structure moves prices by more than knowledge does:**
  - +80% when insured (Kerschbamer et al.)
  - +13% when reimbursed (taxis)
  - In fleet, aviation and B2B repair, where a third party or deep-pocketed operator pays, the extraction channel is "who pays", and that is not fixed by AI knowledge.
- **The largest documented price gaps (2-4x) come from access restrictions**, not opacity:
  - OEM $500-600/h against independent $150-250/h
  - OEM parts 15-50% dearer
  - These are monopoly over tools, software and parts (FTC).
- **The institution that best fixes credence-goods fraud is liability (guaranteed outcome), not verifiability (DKS 2011 lab).** Second-opinion competition helps (Rasch & Waibel). Customer-requested monitoring helps (Angerer et al.).

**Rough decomposition (my synthesis, not a measured number):** of the "excess" in a typical consumer auto repair bill relative to the efficient price:
- low single digits: outright fraud
- roughly 5-15%: price discrimination on visibly uninformed customers
- an unknown but likely larger share: over- and under-treatment from mixed incompetence and incentives
- in OEM-locked segments, a 2-4x markup from access restrictions

So diagnosis opacity is a real but **mid-sized** driver of overcharging in consumer repair. The large multiples come from structural lock-in and third-party payment.

## 4. Stance on hypotheses

- **C1 (universal chain): silent.** The theory does model "diagnosis then treatment" as two linked steps. Its key point is that the bundling of diagnosis and treatment in one seller causes the fraud.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed.**
  - Information asymmetry is the defining feature, and uninformed customers are exploited (taxis; Busse et al.).
  - But measured strict fraud is small.
  - The big rents come from access and tool monopolies (FTC) and from payer moral hazard. These are not knowledge gaps.
- **C3 (AI erases the gap): contradicts / mixed.**
  - AI can cut price opacity and give cheap second opinions, which the literature says reduces fraud: competition and second opinions (Rasch & Waibel), customer-requested monitoring (Angerer).
  - But credence goods stay credence goods. The customer still cannot verify what was done inside the machine.
  - The lab shows verifiability has only a minor effect without liability (DKS 2011).
  - Experts adapt: the gender gap closes only on the dimension signalled.
  - Insurance-driven overcharging (+80%) is untouched by customer knowledge.
- **C4 (10x drop in combined cost): contradicts.**
  - The information-rent part of the bill is about 2-15% in audits.
  - Only access-locked OEM segments show 2-4x gaps.
  - A 10x drop is not supported by the credence-goods evidence.
- **C5 (AI parts factory): silent.** Indirectly, the FTC shows alternative parts at 15-50% cheaper, and that OEM software locks against salvaged or remanufactured parts. Parts competition is legally and technically blocked, not just slow.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports.**
  - Hubbard shows that inspectors chosen by the inspected party drift lenient (consumers return 30% more to stations that pass them).
  - Lab monitoring results show that inspection incentive design matters.
  - Independent, AI-assisted audit is a natural fix, but who pays the inspector is the crux.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.**
  - The literature says the inefficiency is an equilibrium outcome of bundled diagnosis and treatment, unverifiability, short-term contracts (Taylor), third-party payers and access restrictions.
  - It is not "slop". It is rational behaviour under bad institutions.
  - The fixes it identifies are institutional (liability, fixed-price guarantees, independent diagnosis, competition), not only technological.

## 5. Company-shape implications

1. **Guaranteed-outcome repair.** Liability is the institution that works (DKS 2011). Examples: fixed-price, pay-only-if-fixed, warranty-backed repair, underwritten by AI diagnosis that is accurate enough to price the risk. The AI is the underwriting engine, not the product.
2. **Independent AI diagnosis and audit for third-party payers.** Insurers, fleets, lessors and MRO buyers lose about 80% when the shop knows a third party pays. Tractable-style estimate auditing generalises to industrial and aviation work orders. Customer-requested monitoring works (Angerer 2021).
3. **Competence tooling for technicians.** Missed faults (15% of easy faults in ADAC tests) and undertreatment are as costly as fraud. Low competence predicts fraud. AI that raises technician competence lowers both.
4. **Access-layer plays (diagnostic software and data interop, alternative or remanufactured parts).** These go where the 2-4x gaps are, but they depend on right-to-repair law and on DMCA §1201 exemptions.
5. **Caution:** a pure "AI tells the consumer what's wrong" app attacks the smallest slice (strict overcharging, about 2% of charges; quote discrimination, about 5-15%). Experts can adapt to it.

## 6. Surprises
- Strict billing fraud in audited garages is low: 4.5% of German corporate garages, 6% of US visits, 2% of charges. Distrust (74% fear being cheated) far exceeds measured fraud.
- Insurance coverage (+80%) is a bigger fraud driver than customer ignorance.
- In the lab, verifiability (transparency) barely helps. Liability is what matters.
- Incompetence and fraud co-occur: the shops that cheat are the ones that miss faults.
- Unbundling diagnosis from treatment cut overtreatment but raised undertreatment (Greiner et al. 2017).
- Reputation fails to discipline experts in auto repair (Schneider; DKS 2011), yet it over-disciplines inspectors toward leniency (Hubbard).
