## Findings

### F1. Records are a large share of asset value: "an aircraft is only as good as its records" (practitioner view, plus data)
- Lessors and appraisers (ISTAT, IBA, Aviation Week MRO coverage) routinely say missing or incomplete records cut an aircraft's value sharply. For engine life-limited parts (LLPs) without full **back-to-birth (BtB) traceability**, the part can be close to unsaleable or scrap. A CFM56/V2500 LLP stack is worth several $M [recall: roughly $3-6M depending on remaining cycles], so the paper trail, not the metal, sets the value.
- Lease-return transitions often slip by weeks or months because of records reconciliation, and every day costs the lessor lost rent and storage.
- Source: Aviation Week MRO coverage of lease returns and records; ISTAT appraisal practice. https://aviationweek.com/mro (general) [unverified this session]
- Implication: in aviation, a large part of the "understand" and "sourcing" cost is **provenance cost**, not knowledge cost. Digital records hit this directly.

### F2. AOG Technics (2023): forged paper certificates entered the supply chain at scale (news / court / regulator)
- In 2023 UK distributor AOG Technics was found to have sold CFM56 (and some CF6/CFM) parts with falsified EASA Form 1 / FAA 8130-3 release certificates. Airlines including TAP, Virgin Australia, Delta, United, American and Southwest found suspect parts. Engines were pulled for inspection.
- [recall] Regulators and press counted about 60,000+ parts sold, with suspect parts found on 100+ engines. Its director, Jose Zamora Yrala, was prosecuted by the UK Serious Fraud Office and pleaded guilty to fraudulent trading in 2025. [Sentencing detail not verified.]
- Sources: UK SFO, AOG Technics case page https://www.sfo.gov.uk/cases/aog-technics-ltd/ ; FAA Unapproved Parts Notifications [unverified this session]
- Implication: paper traceability is easy to forge. It argues FOR digital, cryptographically signed records (ATA Spec 2000 Ch.16 e-certificates). It also shows the binding constraint is **trust/provenance and certification**, not knowledge (supports C6, not C3).

### F3. Industry standard for digital records exists but is adopted slowly (regulator / standards)
- FAA AC 120-78A (2016) accepts electronic signatures, recordkeeping and manuals. EASA likewise accepts electronic Form 1.
- ATA Spec 2000 Chapter 9 covers permanent part marking and traceability; Chapter 16 covers electronic authorized release certificates (e-8130-3 / e-Form 1).
- Most of the used-serviceable-material (USM) trade and many MRO shops still run on scanned PDFs and paper "trace" packs [practitioner view, recall].
- Sources: FAA AC 120-78A https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.information/documentID/1029638 ; A4A/ATA Spec 2000 https://publications.airlines.org/ [unverified this session]
- Implication: the regulatory permission is not the blocker; incentives and fragmentation are.

### F4. Blockchain parts-tracking pilots mostly stalled (news / history, contradicts hype)
- 2018-2021 saw many pilots: Honeywell GoDirect Trade (a blockchain-backed used-parts marketplace), Moog VeriPart, SITA/Air France-KLM, Lufthansa/Aviatar consortia and the "MRO Blockchain Alliance".
- [recall] Honeywell reported GoDirect Trade passing $1B+ in parts listed or sold. Most other pilots did not reach industry-wide use, and the value came from the marketplace and data standardisation, not from the ledger.
- The core problem is the oracle problem: a blockchain only proves the record wasn't altered after entry. AOG-style forgery happens at entry.
- Sources: Honeywell press releases on GoDirect Trade (2019-2021) https://aerospace.honeywell.com ; Aviation Week coverage of MRO blockchain [unverified this session]
- Implication: digital provenance helps, but chain-of-custody needs physical anchoring (part marking, inspection). That supports C6 and contradicts naive C3/C4.

### F5. Airbus Skywise: big adoption, OEM-hosted data (data / oem_view)
- Launched at the Paris Air Show in 2017 with Palantir Foundry. [recall] By 2023-2024 Airbus claimed **140+ airlines and ~10,000-12,000 aircraft connected**, and tens of thousands of users.
- Products include Skywise Core, Skywise Fleet Performance+ (predictive maintenance) and Skywise Health Monitoring.
- Airbus states that airlines keep ownership of their data. However, the platform, models and cross-fleet insight sit with Airbus/Palantir.
- easyJet case [recall]: Skywise-based predictive maintenance was credited with avoiding dozens of technical cancellations per year (easyJet said ~79 in its first year).
- Sources: Airbus Skywise https://aircraft.airbus.com/en/services/enhance/skywise ; Palantir/Airbus case https://www.palantir.com/impact/airbus/ ; easyJet investor/press statements 2019 [unverified this session]
- Implication: data platforms become the new OEM moat. The "knowledge monopoly" moves from manuals to fleet data (supports C2's monopoly framing, contradicts C3).

### F6. Boeing AnalytX / Boeing Global Services: same pattern (oem_view)
- Boeing launched AnalytX in 2017 to bundle analytics, Airplane Health Management (AHM), Maintenance Performance Toolbox and Jeppesen digital products.
- AHM is long-standing: real-time fault data is sent to the ground before landing so parts and mechanics can be staged. Boeing marketing has claimed AHM cuts delays by staging parts ahead of arrival [specific % not verified].
- Source: Boeing press release, "Boeing AnalytX", 2017 https://boeing.mediaroom.com [unverified this session]
- Implication: OEM digital services aim at the notice→diagnose→source parts handoff (C1/C4). The OEM keeps ownership of the analytics layer.

### F7. Who owns the data: legally the operator owns the maintenance records; operationally the OEM controls the sensor and analytics layer (regulator / practitioner)
- 14 CFR 91.417 and 121.380 require the owner/operator to keep maintenance records and transfer them on sale. So **legal custody of records is the operator's**.
- Sensor data from engines under OEM power-by-the-hour contracts (Rolls-Royce TotalCare, GE TrueChoice, Pratt EngineWise) is usually contractually shared with, or controlled by, the OEM.
- IATA has pushed "airlines own their aircraft data" principles and criticised OEM aftermarket restrictions on data, manuals and repairs. IATA's Director General, Willie Walsh, has repeatedly attacked OEM aftermarket monopolies (2023-2025).
- Sources: eCFR 14 CFR 91.417 https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-E/section-91.417 ; IATA MRO / aftermarket statements https://www.iata.org/en/programs/ops-infra/techops/ [unverified this session]
- Implication: ownership is split. The records are the operator's. The models, cross-fleet benchmarks and repair data (DER repairs, OEM manuals) are the OEM's.

### F8. Engine OEM power-by-the-hour and data lock-in (investor / practitioner view)
- Rolls-Royce TotalCare covers most of its widebody fleet. The OEM monitors engine health and decides when engines are removed and where they are repaired.
- Engine shop visits are the largest single item in airline maintenance cost. [recall: IATA MCTG puts engines at ~40% of MRO spend; global airline MRO ~ $100B+/yr].
- Analysts (Jefferies, Bernstein) treat aftermarket and data control as the core of GE/RR/Safran margins.
- Source: IATA Maintenance Cost Technical Group (MCTG) reports https://www.iata.org/en/programs/ops-infra/techops/maintenance/ ; Rolls-Royce annual reports [unverified this session]
- Implication: cost is dominated by **materials/parts and engine shop visits**, priced by OEMs with data plus IP plus certification moats. Better records do not by themselves reduce part prices.

### F9. Predictive maintenance savings: DOE and McKinsey benchmarks (study, cross-industry)
- US DOE FEMP O&M Best Practices Guide (Release 3.0, 2010) [recall]: predictive maintenance saves 8-12% over preventive and up to 40% over reactive, and gives about 10x ROI, a 25-30% maintenance cost cut, 70-75% fewer breakdowns and 35-45% less downtime. (Often cited, but old and survey-based.)
- McKinsey (2017-2018) [recall]: predictive maintenance can cut maintenance costs 10-40% and downtime by up to 50%. A later McKinsey piece ("Prediction at scale", ~2021) warned that many PdM pilots fail to scale and that condition-based or reliability-centred fixes often do as well.
- Sources: DOE FEMP O&M Best Practices Guide https://www.energy.gov/femp/articles/operations-and-maintenance-best-practices-guide ; McKinsey https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/ [unverified this session]
- Implication: the hard data supports **10-40%** savings, not 10x (order of magnitude). That contradicts C4's magnitude.

### F10. No-fault-found (NFF) removals: diagnosis waste is real and records help (study / practitioner)
- [recall] Aviation studies (Cranfield NFF research; ARINC/ATA figures) put no-fault-found at around 30-50% of avionics/LRU removals. That is costly unnecessary shipping, testing and spares inventory.
- Better fault histories per serial number (a digital twin of the component) lower NFF rates.
- Source: Cranfield University, Khan, Phillips et al., NFF research (2012-2015) [unverified this session]
- Implication: supports C2/C4 in part. Diagnosis is a real cost centre, and fixing it needs serial-level data history, not general knowledge.

### F11. Analogs outside aviation: data access fights (court / regulator)
- **FTC v. Deere (Jan 2025):** the FTC sued John Deere, alleging it limits independent repair by restricting its full-function diagnostic software (Service ADVISOR) to dealers.
- **Massachusetts Question 1 (2020):** about 75% of voters approved access to vehicle telematics data for independent repairers. Implementation was tangled in an automaker lawsuit, and NHTSA safety objections (2023) delayed enforcement.
- **EU:** Regulation 2018/858 provides access to vehicle repair and maintenance information (RMI). The EU Data Act (applies from Sept 2025) gives users of connected products rights to the data they generate, including sharing it with third-party repairers.
- Sources: FTC press release Jan 15 2025 https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-sues-deere-company ; EU Data Act https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policy/data-act [unverified this session]
- Implication: the knowledge gap is often a deliberate **access gap**, enforced through software, data and legal rules. Policy (the Data Act) is starting to open it. AI cannot fix it if the data is locked (supports C2 and C7 in part, contradicts C3).

### F12. Digital twin claims are largely vendor-sourced (oem_view / investor caution)
- GE ("digital twin" from 2016), Rolls-Royce IntelligentEngine and Siemens all publish ROI claims. Independent peer-reviewed evidence of fleet-level cost cuts is thin.
- Academic reviews (e.g. Tao et al. 2019, IEEE TII "Digital Twin in Industry: State-of-the-Art") note that most work is conceptual or in pilots.
- Source: Tao, F. et al., "Digital Twin in Industry: State-of-the-Art", IEEE Transactions on Industrial Informatics 2019 https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8477101 [unverified this session]
- Implication: treat order-of-magnitude claims with caution. The proven wins are narrower (avoided cancellations, parts staged ahead of arrival, lower NFF).

## Reasoning / synthesis

- **Do digital records reduce repair cost?** Yes, at the margins and in specific places:
  - parts staged before arrival (AHM), which cuts AOG time;
  - fewer technical cancellations;
  - lower NFF rates;
  - faster lease transitions and records audits;
  - fraud resistance (AOG Technics).

  Benchmarks point to 10-40% savings on maintenance cost in specific categories, not 10x across the whole repair. The bulk of aviation MRO spend is material and engine shop visits, priced by OEM IP and certification. Records don't change that unless they also enable PMA parts, DER repairs and USM, which they do: BtB records are what make used parts sellable.
- **Who owns them?** Legally, the operator owns the records (14 CFR 91.417). In practice, OEMs own the platforms (Skywise, AnalytX), the sensor data under power-by-the-hour contracts, the manuals and the repair approvals. The monopoly has moved from "knowledge in heads" to **"data plus certification plus IP"**.
- **The startup angle:** the scarce layer is **trusted, machine-readable, transferable provenance**: serial-level history that a regulator, lessor or buyer accepts. Whoever makes records portable and verifiable across OEMs, MROs and lessors removes friction in parts sourcing (USM/PMA) and asset transfers. Examples include e-certs (Spec 2000 Ch.16), records-scanning-plus-AI (companies like Aerotrack / FLYdocs / Bluetail / ProvenAir — [recall], verify), and anti-counterfeit part marking.
