## Findings

### F1. US franchised dealers 2025: labor is about 45-57% of repair revenue at retail prices, parts the rest [FETCHED, data]
Source: NADA Data 2025, Service and Parts Department, pp. 10-12. https://www.nada.org/media/4695
- Total service and parts sales: **$164.6B** from **276.1M repair orders**, with **278,424 technicians**. Sales were $116.5B in 2018, so they are up 41%.
- Service labor sales total **$75.81B**: customer mechanical $31.48B, customer body $4.63B, warranty $15.46B, sublet $5.07B, internal $12.22B, other $6.94B.
- Parts sales total **$93.42B**: customer mechanical $24.21B, customer body $4.14B, **wholesale $24.54B**, counter $4.32B, warranty $16.67B, internal $8.55B, other $11.00B.
- Parts sales per dollar of service labor sales: **$1.43** (NADA). But about $28.9B of parts is wholesale or counter sales to other shops and DIYers, with no dealer labor attached.
- My calculation, comparing like with like:
  - Customer-pay mechanical: labor $31.48B vs parts $24.21B, so **labor is 57%** and parts 43%.
  - Warranty (the OEM pays): labor $15.46B vs parts $16.67B, so **labor is 48%**.
  - Customer body: labor $4.63B vs parts $4.14B, so **labor is 53%**.
- Average customer mechanical labor rate: **$186/hr**. Average sales per customer repair order: **$494**; per warranty repair order: **$551**.
- Dealer parts inventory: $9.72B, or about $572k per dealership. That is the parts-availability buffer.
- Reading: in auto mechanical repair at a dealer, labor and parts are roughly equal shares, with labor slightly ahead. Parts are billed at retail with dealer markup, so parts' share of *economic* cost is lower than the billed share. There is no separate line for "diagnosis"; it is billed as labor hours (diagnostic time) inside the labor dollars.

### F2. Collision repair: costs are rising per claim, driven by parts count, labor rate and complexity [FETCHED, data]
Sources: III / ISO data https://www.iii.org/fact-statistic/facts-statistics-auto-insurance ; CCC Crash Course https://www.cccis.com/crash-course
- Collision claim severity: $3,377 (2015), $3,828 (2019), $5,377 (2022), **$5,489 (2024)**. That is +63% since 2015, while frequency fell from 6.01 to 4.16 claims per 100 car-years.
- Property damage liability severity: $3,628 (2015) to **$6,770 (2024)**, +87%.
- CPI for motor vehicle body work: +50.5% from 2016 to 2025 (III page).
- CCC: average labor rate +4.5% in 2024 after +7.5% in 2023. EV labor rates are about 30% higher than ICE. Labor hours per claim were **27.3 in 2024, up from 23.0 in 2013**. EV repairs need about 4 more labor hours than ICE.
- CCC: "part dollars are contributing a shrinking share of cost increases compared with labor and miscellaneous costs." The page gives no percentage split. [SIBLING s05, same page]: 13.5 parts replaced per estimate (EV 22, ICE 15.9). Scans appear on 86.3% of DRP repairable appraisals (about $157 each), and ADAS calibrations on 29.9% (about $547). 56.3% of calibrations are added on a supplement, not caught at first estimate.
- [BACKGROUND, verify] Industry estimating data (CCC/Mitchell) has long put a repairable collision claim at roughly: replacement parts 35-45%, body and refinish labor 35-40%, paint materials 8-10%, and the rest sublet, scans and calibration. These numbers are directionally consistent with the NADA body-shop split in F1 (labor 53%), but I could not fetch the source table this session.
- Reading: the "diagnose/understand" step in collision is the estimate. It is a small slice of the dollar. The dollars are labor hours times a rising rate, plus physical parts. Diagnostic scans and calibrations ($157 and $547 line items) are the closest thing to a "knowledge" fee. They add complexity cost, and they are growing.

### F3. Collision consolidator P&L: gross margin about 47%; margin comes from parts, paint, sublet and calibration more than labor [FETCHED, data]
Source: Boyd Group (Gerber Collision) Q2 2026 interim report. https://s25.q4cdn.com/123825503/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/BoydGroup_Other_ShareholderReport.pdf
- Q2 2026: sales $1,013.7M, cost of sales $533.6M, **gross margin 47.4%**, operating expense 33.9%, adjusted EBITDA margin 13.4%.
- Quote: "Gross margin benefitted from increased paint and parts margins ... also positively impacted by increased sublet and scanning and calibration margins, partially offset by lower labor margins."
- Quote: total cost of repair (TCOR) "is also positively impacted by wage inflation and inflation on the cost of parts." It names "inability to maintain, replace or grow technician capacity" as a key risk.
- Reading: the shop's profit pool is mostly in **parts markup, paint and materials, and scans/calibrations**. Labor is the shop's main *cost* and its constrained input. This has a direct implication: a cheaper sourcing or diagnosis step would mostly squeeze the parts and scan margin that shops currently earn, rather than freeing up capacity.

### F4. Aviation: materials are 60%+ of engine and component MRO; labor is 20% or less of what an engine shop visit costs [FETCHED, consultant analysis]
Source: IATA / Oliver Wyman, Reviving the Commercial Aircraft Supply Chain (Oct 2025), pp. 25-27.
- Quote: "Materials content is highest in engine and component maintenance (60%+), while much lower for airframe and line maintenance (20% or less). In engine and component maintenance, labor is concentrated in disassembly, inspection, test, and reassembly."
- **Exhibit 16**, my reading of the chart text (turbofan, direct cost to the MRO; "labor excludes internal repairs"):
  - **Non-LLP shop visit:** materials (non-LLP) about 60%, piece-part repairs 20-30%, **labor 10-15%**, other 5-10%.
  - **LLP shop visit (about 2x the cost):** LLPs 50-60%, other materials 15-20%, repairs 10-15%, **labor 5-10%**, other 0-5%. Total materials 65-80%.
  - Correction to sibling note m01: it gave labor as 5-10% for the non-LLP visit. The chart shows 10-15% non-LLP and 5-10% LLP.
- Segment shares of 2024 MRO spend: engine 51.8%, component 18.2%, airframe and modifications 17.3%, line 12.7% (Exhibit 13).
- Line maintenance: "mainly labor-intensive, routine, day-to-day checks and minor repairs."
- Engine OEM gross margins: "**-5% to 10% on the manufacturing side and 20 to 35% on the MRO side**." GE's and Rolls-Royce's MRO services were 74% and 66% of commercial revenue in 2024.
- Licensed MROs "are typically restricted by OEM commercial license terms from using other approved repairs ... PMAs, and even USM."
- Weighted estimate, my calculation: if engine and component (70% of spend) are about 65-75% materials and repairs, and airframe and line (30%) are about 80% labor, then **materials and outside repairs are roughly 50-60% of total airline MRO spend and labor about 40-50%**. This is an estimate; the IATA MCX split of material, labor, LLPs and outside repairs is only in the members-only part of the report.
- Reading: in the largest aviation segment, the dollar goes to **physical parts (LLP discs and shafts, hot-section blades) and OEM-controlled piece-part repairs**, sold at 20-35% MRO margins. Engineering knowledge is embedded in those parts and repairs as IP. It is not sold as a separate "diagnosis" fee.

### F5. Aviation scale and unit costs [FETCHED, data]
Source: IATA MCX FY2024 public report.
- Global MRO spend **$103.9B in 2024**, 11.5% of airline operating costs (10% in 2019) and 10.8% of revenue.
- MCX sample (28 airlines, 2,703 aircraft): maintenance cost **$1,522 per flight hour**, $3,758 per flight cycle, **$5.05M per aircraft per year**. Average fleet age 10.6 years; dispatch reliability 98.86%.
- Engine share rose from 41% (2019) to 50% (2024). Widebodies are 20.3% of the active fleet but 42% of MRO cost.
- Airline salaries rose 8% year on year in 2024 and were 26% above 2019.

### F6. US airlines outsource about half of flight-equipment maintenance [FETCHED, data]
Source: MIT Airline Data Project, US DOT Form 41 Schedule P-5.2. http://web.mit.edu/airlinedata/www/2020 12 Month Documents/Expense Related/MX/ (Total Flight Equipment Maintenance Expense; Total Outsourced Flight Equipment Maintenance Expense)
- Total flight-equipment maintenance expense, all US sectors: $9.43B (2000), $10.34B (2010), $11.49B (2015), **$13.04B (2019)**, $9.70B (2020).
- Outsourced: $4.35B (2010), $5.49B (2015), **$6.41B (2019)**, $3.84B (2020).
- My calculation: outsourced share went from **42% (2010) to 48% (2015) to 49% (2019)**, then 40% in 2020.
- Reading: about half of the airline maintenance dollar is paid out as a bundled third-party price (labor, materials and margin together), mostly to engine OEM networks. For the airline, that money is a contract price, not a labor-vs-parts choice.

### F7. Aviation cost inflation in 2023-25 hit materials harder than labor [SIBLING m01, Oliver Wyman MRO surveys]
- 2023: materials +8.3% (pre-pandemic 3-4% a year), labor +7.3% (pre-pandemic 2-3%).
- 2025: materials up more than 7%; wages grew 6.6% against an expected 5.8%.
- 80% of respondents named **piece-part availability** as the top cause of longer turnaround times. Engine visits run up to 300+ days in some cases.
- Reading: in aviation, time is lost waiting for parts, and money goes on parts.

### F8. Home warranty / appliance and systems repair: about $246 per dispatch; supplier parts are about 22% of service cost; the visit (labor and travel) dominates [SIBLING s06 (Frontdoor 10-K), s01]
- Frontdoor (American Home Shield) 10-K FY2025: cost of services rendered was 45% of revenue. Direct supplier parts and equipment spend is about **22% of cost of services**, excluding parts that contractors buy themselves. That works out to about **$246 per service request**.
- 2022 margin squeeze: "industry-wide parts and equipment availability challenges and inflation can impact our ability to estimate the cost to settle claims."
- s01 synthesis: the truck roll plus labor (call-out plus hourly) is about **10-25% of the device's price before any work starts**, and it is the largest share of a typical appliance ticket. Austria's Reparaturbonus subsidises 50% of the repair cost, which targets the labor price.
- [BACKGROUND, verify] Typical US appliance service tickets are a $75-150 diagnostic or trip fee, plus $150-300 of labor, plus parts. Parts are often $50-300, except sealed-system and control-board jobs. The commonly quoted rule of thumb is that labor and trip charges make up half or more of a typical appliance repair bill.
- Reading: in low-ticket, in-home repair, the dollar goes to **getting a skilled person to the house**, often twice when parts aren't on the truck. Diagnosis is bundled into the visit fee, so an AI diagnosis is worth something mainly if it avoids a visit or a second trip.

### F9. Industrial plant maintenance: labor time is 25-35% wrench time; the rest is logistics; reactive work is about half [SIBLING m07]
- NIST: US manufacturers spent about **$74.5B** on maintenance, with losses from unplanned downtime of about **$119B**, 1.6x the direct spend. 45.7% of maintenance was reactive.
- Wrench time is 25-35%. Of the rest: travel 10-20%, obtaining parts, materials and tools 10-15%, waiting, coordination and permits 10-20%, breaks and late starts 10-15% (Palmer / Limble / Fiix).
- IDCON: craftspeople may spend 20-30% of their time on "pure thinking" (diagnosis and understanding).
- Maintenance cost benchmark: 2-3% of replacement asset value for world-class; typical plants 3-6% (unverified).
- [BACKGROUND, verify] Typical industrial maintenance budgets (SMRP and Solomon-type benchmarks) split roughly 35-50% internal labor, 30-40% materials and spares, and 15-30% contractors. Refineries and chemicals tend toward higher contractor shares during turnarounds.
- Reading: the dollar splits roughly evenly between labor and materials, but the bigger number sits outside the maintenance budget: downtime. Within labor, the knowledge-work slice (diagnosis and planning) is about 20-30% of craft time; logistics takes more.

### F10. Mining and heavy equipment: parts and components are about half or more of direct maintenance spend [SIBLING m13, BACKGROUND]
- Parts and components (including rebuilds and exchange units) are typically the largest share of direct maintenance spend, often around half or more. Labor is roughly a third, followed by contractors and overhead.
- The dominant economic cost is lost production from unavailability. One idle ultra-class haul truck can forgo several thousand USD per hour.
- [SIBLING m13, Senate PSI 2014 Caterpillar report, to verify] Dealer and OEM aftermarket parts are a disproportionate profit pool for OEMs.

### F11. Offshore wind: technician labor is about 4% of OpEx and materials about 1.5%; vessels are about 78% [SIBLING m08, NREL Cost of Wind Energy Review 2024, WOMBAT model]
- Fixed-bottom offshore OpEx is $135/kW-yr. Maintenance is $113, made up of labor $5, materials $2 and **equipment (vessels) $105**. Operations $22, insurance $19, port fees $1.
- Floating: total OpEx $108, of which vessels $61 and port fees $17.
- UK (BVG / Crown Estate guide 2025): OPEX about £85k/MW/yr. Turbine maintenance £34.2k, balance of plant £11.6k, **statutory inspections £1.0k (about 1%)**, insurance £11.2k, offshore logistics £7.2k.
- Land-based wind: OpEx $43/kW-yr, about 25% of LCOE.
- Reading: this is the sharpest counter-example to "labor vs parts" framing. In offshore wind, the dollar goes to **access** (heavy-lift vessels, weather windows), not to people or parts, and certainly not to diagnosis.

### F12. Price of OEM-gated service labor: 3-5x independent rates [SIBLING v09, Copyright Office 1201 record]
- Medical imaging: IAMERS testimony cited "$150-200 per hour for ISO repair compared to $600-800 per hour for OEM repair with a four-hour minimum."
- Reading: where the OEM controls access, the premium shows up in the *labor rate*, and the rate prices access and authorisation. This is one place where C2's "knowledge monopoly" visibly sets a price, but the lock is legal and technical (passwords, service keys, DMCA), not missing know-how.

## Synthesis: where the repair dollar goes, by domain

| Domain | Labor share | Parts / materials share | Other big bucket | Main source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Auto mechanical (dealer, customer pay) | ~57% | ~43% (retail, incl. markup) | Diagnosis billed inside labor | NADA 2025 |
| Auto warranty (OEM pays) | ~48% | ~52% | | NADA 2025 |
| Collision | ~40-53% | ~35-45% parts + ~8-10% paint | Scans and calibration growing; total loss 22% | NADA, CCC, Boyd; split partly BACKGROUND |
| Airline engine shop visit | 5-15% | 60-80% materials + 10-30% piece-part repairs | OEM MRO margins 20-35% | IATA/OW Exhibit 16 |
| Airline airframe and line | ~80%+ | ≤20% | | IATA/OW |
| Airline MRO total | ~40-50% (est.) | ~50-60% (est.) | ~49% outsourced (US) | My weighting; Form 41 |
| Appliance / home systems | Visit + labor dominant (≥50%) | ~22% supplier parts (Frontdoor) | Repeat trips | Frontdoor 10-K, s01 |
| Industrial plant | ~35-50% internal labor | ~30-40% | Downtime losses 1.6x spend; contractors 15-30% | NIST, BACKGROUND |
| Mining | ~1/3 | ~1/2+ | Lost production | m13 BACKGROUND |
| Offshore wind | ~4% | ~1.5% | Vessels ~78% of OpEx | NREL |

Cross-cutting patterns:
1. **No domain has a separate "knowledge" or "diagnosis" line item that is a large share of the dollar.** Diagnosis is billed as labor hours (auto), bundled into the visit fee (appliance), embedded in OEM piece-part repairs and licensed IP (aviation), or is a small slice of craft time (industrial, 20-30% of labor time at most).
2. **The split follows asset value and part value.** For high-value machines with precious parts (engines, components, mining rebuilds), materials dominate. For low-value products and in-place work (appliances, airframe and line checks, plant crafts), labor and the visit dominate.
3. **Access and downtime can swamp both.** Offshore vessels, AOG time ($10k-$150k per hour, sibling m02), and plant downtime (1.6x the maintenance budget) are bigger than the repair bill itself.
4. **Margins sit in parts.** Engine OEM MRO margins of 20-35% vs -5% to 10% on new engines; Boyd's gross margin is driven by parts, paint and calibration margins; OEMs earn their aftermarket rent on parts and licensed repairs, not on labor.
5. **Labor is the constrained input, even where it is a small share of cost.** It is constrained by technician count (NADA 278k dealer techs; Boyd risk factor; aviation shortfalls), not by knowledge per technician.

## Stances on the claims
- **C1 (one chain): mixed.** The cost data does not break out by these stages anywhere. Real repair bills are organised by labor, parts, sublet and access, and several of the chain's steps are fused: diagnosis sits inside labor, sourcing inside parts markup. Wind adds a large "get access" stage, and aviation engines run on planned overhaul, not notice-and-diagnose.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed.** The OEM rent is real and large: engine MRO margins of 20-35%, an OEM labor rate 3-5x the independent rate in imaging, and licence terms barring PMA and USM. But it is collected through **parts, licensed repairs and access authorisation**, not as a fee for knowledge.
- **C3 (AI ends the gap): contradicts, on this evidence.** The dollars sit in physical parts, labor hours and access. Closing an information gap does not by itself change parts prices set by IP and licence terms, or the labor and vessel hours needed.
- **C4 (10x cut in diagnose/understand/source/deliver): contradicts on magnitude.** The four steps are a minority of cost in every domain measured. Diagnosis is a small fraction of labor; sourcing and delivery costs are mostly the parts' price and inventory carrying (dealers hold $9.7B of parts). Even a 10x cut in those steps moves the total repair bill perhaps 10-30% in auto and appliances, and less in engines, where materials are 60-80%. The exception is that **parts *prices*** (when OEM-gated) are a large pool, and alternative sourcing (PMA, USM, aftermarket) does cut them, but by tens of percent, not 10x.
- **C5 (bespoke parts solved): mixed / silent.** Materials are 60-80% of engine costs, so cheaper qualified parts are the biggest lever in aviation. But the data shows the binding issue is certification, licence terms and lessor acceptance (OW), not manufacturing capability. No evidence here that "AI parts factory" parts get accepted.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): mixed.** Inspection is embedded in labor (aviation disassembly and inspection are the main labor content) and is essential. But as a separate cost line it can be tiny (statutory inspection ~1% of UK offshore wind OPEX). It matters more as a gate to return to service than as a cost bucket.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.** Profit pools are deliberately placed in parts and licensed repairs (engine OEMs sell new engines at -5% to 10% margins and earn back on MRO). That is "built for rent capture", which partly supports C7. But much of the cost is physics: labor hours, heavy vessels, high-value consumable parts. Those would remain under any rebuild.

## Where the money and time go
- Auto: about half labor (at $186/hr dealer rates and 27 labor hours per collision claim), half parts at retail markup. Severity is rising from complexity (more parts, scans and calibrations), not from knowledge gaps.
- Aviation: about half the money is engine shop visits, where materials and OEM repairs are 75-90% of direct cost and labor 5-15%. Time is lost waiting for piece parts.
- Appliances: the truck roll and technician time dominate; parts about a fifth to a third.
- Industrial and mining: labor and materials roughly even; downtime costs more than either.
- Offshore wind: vessel access is about 78% of OpEx.

## Real bottleneck
The scarce layer depends on the domain, but it is consistently physical or contractual, not informational:
- Aviation: certified parts supply and the IP and licence control over them.
- Auto, appliances and plants: technician hours and the cost of putting a technician on site.
- Offshore wind: heavy-lift access.
The knowledge component is real but small, and it is mostly paid for through the labor hour.

## Company shapes the evidence points at
1. **Qualified alternative parts and repairs for high-materials-share domains** (aviation engines and components, mining rebuilds): PMA and DER repair development, USM brokering, AI-assisted reverse engineering plus a certification package. This attacks the 60-80% materials share and the 20-35% OEM margins.
2. **Technician-hour multipliers** where labor is the main cost and the constrained input (auto, collision, appliances, plant crafts): first-time-fix guidance, correct parts on the first visit, kitting and planning. The value is fewer repeat trips and more wrench time, priced per technician.
3. **Access robotics or logistics** where access dominates (offshore wind vessels, blades, towers): cut vessel-days rather than diagnosis time.
4. **Parts-price transparency and sourcing** for collision and auto: the parts and paint margin is where shops earn their gross margin, so there is a big pool, but expect resistance from shops whose profits depend on it.
