## Findings

### 1. StandardAero (engine MRO), the cleanest public data point [VERIFIED]
Source: StandardAero S-1, filed 2024-09-06, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2025410/000119312524215250/d838237ds1.htm

Financials:
- Revenue: $4,563.3M in 2023, up 10.0% from $4,150.5M in 2022. 1H2024 revenue was $2,582.9M, up 12%.
- Adjusted EBITDA: $561.1M in 2023 (12.3% margin), up from $475.4M (11.5%) in 2022. 1H2024 margin was 13.0%.
- Debt: $3,275.7M total debt against $60.3M cash at 6/30/2024. That is roughly 5.5-6x Adj. EBITDA, a typical PE-held leverage level.

Contracts and market position:
- "approximately 77% of our revenue was derived from long-term contractual agreements."
- About 80% of 2023 Engine Services sales came from platforms where it holds a #1 or #2 global position.
- Military end-users were about 19.6% of 2023 revenue.

What the moat is:
- Barriers to entry: "New entrants must obtain extensive approvals and certifications from government regulators and OEMs."
- It holds "exclusive or semi-exclusive licenses directly with the OEM" and is "the only independent service provider in North America officially authorized to service a number of our platforms." It reports a "100% historical success rate" retaining OEM licenses.
- Risk factors: losing an OEM authorization could mean "restricting us from benefits like discounts on parts, technical information".
- OEM service divisions have "control over sources and pricing of approved parts" and "traditionally hold a sizeable aftermarket service market share on newer platforms."
- The top four OEM customers were about 43% of 2023 revenue. The OEMs are simultaneously licensors, competitors and customers.

Component repairs:
- It developed roughly 2,000-2,500 new proprietary component repairs since 2016 (the S-1 gives both figures).
- "Part repairs often can be delivered more quickly than and at a significant discount to a new replacement engine part."

Interpretation:
- An independent MRO earns its money by holding scarce OEM licenses, technical data and approved-parts access.
- It also develops engineered repairs that must themselves be approved.
- This is a knowledge and permission monopoly in the literal sense, which supports C2. But the knowledge is gated by legal and regulatory permission, not by ignorance. That contradicts C3: AI does not grant an OEM licence or FAA/EASA approval.

### 2. StandardAero ownership chain: repeated PE flips with large value creation [VERIFIED (Wikipedia)]
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/StandardAero

| Year | Event | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 2004 | Carlyle and Meggitt buy Dunlop Standard Aerospace | ~$1.4B (StandardAero ~$670M) |
| 2007 | Sold to Dubai Aerospace | $1.9B (with Landmark Aviation) |
| 2015 | Veritas buys | $2.1B |
| 2019 | Carlyle (with GIC) buys | $5.0B |
| Oct 2024 | NYSE IPO (SARO) | ~$10.4B valuation at debut (per a Reuters headline cited on the page) |

- Enterprise value roughly 2x'd in 5 years under Carlyle's second ownership.
- At $10.4B equity plus ~$3.2B debt, IPO enterprise value was roughly $13-14B, or about 20x+ trailing Adj. EBITDA (my calculation).
- The public market pays a premium multiple for a licence-protected, contracted repair stream.

### 3. AAR: consolidating heavy maintenance cheaply; capacity and labour, not knowledge, are what it buys [VERIFIED (Wikipedia) + BACKGROUND]
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AAR_Corp.

[VERIFIED]
- AAR announced the purchase of HAECO Americas in November 2025 for $78M.
- HAECO Americas had about 1,600 employees and was the second-largest North American heavy airframe maintenance firm. AAR ranks first.
- In December 2025 AAR agreed to buy Aircraft Reconfig Technologies for $35M to add engineering and certification capability.

[BACKGROUND]
- In 2024 AAR bought Triumph Group's Product Support business for ~$725M.
- AAR revenue is roughly $2.3-2.8B. Its airframe MRO is low-margin and labour-heavy; parts supply/trading is higher-margin.

Interpretation:
- About $49k per employee for the #2 heavy-maintenance shop says airframe heavy checks are a low-value, labour-rate business.
- Value sits in parts, engineered repairs and certification capability. Buying a certification/engineering shop is buying permission to repair, which bears on C6.

### 4. VSE: a pure-play aviation-aftermarket roll-up [VERIFIED partial + BACKGROUND]
Source: https://ir.vsecorp.com/

[VERIFIED]
- VSE announced the acquisition of Atech Turbine Components on 2026-08-31.
- It describes itself as "a leading provider of Aviation distribution and repair services."
- Investor Day is set for 2026-12-09.

[BACKGROUND]
- In 2024-25 VSE sold or exited its fleet (Wheeler/truck parts) business to become a pure aviation aftermarket company.
- Acquisitions included Turbine Controls (~$120M, 2024) and Kellstrom Aerospace (2025).
- Stated logic: combine parts distribution with component MRO, and use OEM exclusive distribution agreements as the moat.
- Segment Adj. EBITDA margins are mid-teens; management has guided margin expansion through mix shift to repair and distribution.

Interpretation: the money is in controlling distribution rights and repair licences, which bears on the sourcing and delivery steps of C4.

### 5. Aviation analog: TransDigm and the sole-source aftermarket pricing monopoly [BACKGROUND, regulator]
- TransDigm runs EBITDA-as-defined margins of ~50%+ on proprietary, often sole-source, aerospace components. Aftermarket is roughly half of revenue and the majority of EBITDA.
- DoD Inspector General audits:
  - DODIG-2019-060 found "excess profit" on spare parts sold to DLA, including one part at 4,451% over a fair price.
  - A December 2021 audit (DODIG-2022-043) found $20.8M of excess profit on 105 of 112 parts reviewed. The cause was refusal to provide uncertified cost data where parts were sole-source.
- This is the strongest evidence that a monopoly over parts (including design data and approvals) captures large rents in repair (C2).
- The binding mechanism is IP and qualification, not knowledge per se. AI-designed replacements would still need qualification (C5 contradicted in regulated sectors).

### 6. HEICO and PMA parts: the existing "alternative parts factory" is a certification business [BACKGROUND]
- HEICO sells FAA Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA) replacement parts and DER-approved repairs, typically priced well below OEM list (commonly cited 30-50% discounts).
- HEICO trades at premium multiples (~30x+ EBITDA in 2024-25), with ~20%+ EBITDA margins in its Flight Support Group.
- Each PMA requires FAA-approved test and computation or identicality data, so the scarce asset is approval throughput.
- C5 already exists commercially in aviation (reverse-engineered alternative parts), but the cost and time sit in certification, not in making the part.

### 7. Collision repair: Caliber and the consolidators [BACKGROUND]
Deals:
- Caliber merged with ABRA in 2018 (OMERS/Leonard Green era).
- Hellman & Friedman took a majority in late 2019 at a reported ~$7B enterprise value, with Leonard Green and OMERS rolling stakes.
- Caliber now has ~1,700-1,800+ centres and multi-billion-dollar revenue.
- Other consolidators: Gerber/Boyd (public, Boyd Group ~CAD multiple 12-15x EBITDA), Crash Champions (Clearlake), Classic Collision (Carlyle-linked), VIVE.

Margin drivers:
- Insurer direct-repair-program (DRP) referral volume. Insurers steer claims to scaled MSOs that guarantee cycle time and severity.
- Parts procurement scale and paint/supply rebates.
- Labour productivity and cycle time. ADAS calibration and scanning are now a growing, higher-margin service line.
- Single-shop EBITDA margins run mid-to-high single digits; scaled MSOs run low-to-mid teens.

Multiples:
- Independent shops sell for ~3-6x EBITDA.
- Platforms trade at ~10-15x. Multiple arbitrage is a core return lever.

Bottleneck:
- The technician shortage is the most-cited constraint (TechForce Foundation and the Collision Industry Foundation cite tens of thousands of open roles a year).
- OEM repair procedures are now largely accessible by subscription, which partially closes the knowledge gap. Labour and insurer gatekeeping remain.

Takeaway: collision is a case where the information to repair was "democratised" (OEM position statements, repair procedures, ADAS specs). The industry still consolidated, because the scarce layers are referral flow (insurers), skilled labour and capital equipment.

### 8. HVAC and residential services: the hottest roll-up category [BACKGROUND]
Platforms and owners:
- Apex Service Partners (Alpine Investors; partial sale/recap 2024 at a reported multi-billion valuation)
- Wrench Group (Leonard Green/TSG; recapitalised 2024 with a new sponsor)
- Sila (Goldman/others)
- Authority Brands (Apax)
- Service Experts (sold by Lennox in 2013; PE-owned since)
- Legacy Service, and many more

Margin drivers:
- Pricing discipline (flat-rate pricing).
- Recurring maintenance memberships, which create replacement-sale pipeline.
- Centralised call centre and dispatch.
- Replacement-sales training ("comfort advisors"); replacement installs are the profit engine, not repair.
- OEM equipment rebates and procurement.
- Tech recruiting academies.

Multiples:
- Small owner-operators sell at ~4-7x EBITDA.
- Scaled platforms change hands at ~12-20x. That spread is the arbitrage.

History:
- 1990s HVAC roll-ups (Group Maintenance America, American Residential Services, Service Experts' first incarnation) largely failed or were absorbed. Causes: integration failure, technician attrition and debt.
- Modern sponsors explicitly try to avoid that pattern with operating playbooks.

Interpretation:
- The PE return in HVAC comes mostly from sales, pricing and capital structure, not from diagnostic efficiency.
- Diagnosis of a residential HVAC fault is usually quick for a competent tech. The binding constraints are technician supply (industry associations cite ~100k+ unfilled roles) and customer acquisition.
- This contradicts C4's premise that the intermediate knowledge steps dominate cost in HVAC.

### 9. Fire and life safety: inspection is the moat and the sales channel [BACKGROUND]
Players:
- Pye-Barker Fire & Safety (Altas Partners, Leonard Green and others; 150-200+ add-ons since ~2018; reported multi-billion valuation)
- Sciens Building Solutions
- Summit Fire & Security
- APi Group (public; "inspection-first" strategy)

APi Group's model:
- It states that statutorily required inspection, service and monitoring revenue (code-mandated under NFPA 25/72) produces follow-on repair work, with a commonly cited pull-through of several dollars of service/repair per dollar of inspection.
- It targets inspection/service at a majority of revenue. Adjusted EBITDA margin is ~13%, with a goal of 16%+.

Why it's attractive:
- Non-discretionary demand created by code.
- Recurring, route-dense revenue.
- Licensed-technician scarcity, with state licensing as a barrier.
- Fragmentation: thousands of local firms.

Multiples:
- Add-ons at ~5-8x.
- Scaled platforms are valued in the high-teens to 20x range.

Interpretation:
- This strongly supports C6: inspection is essential. It is also the most valuable step commercially, because it is the regulated "notice" trigger that generates the repair.
- It contradicts C7's framing that the industry is "built to be inefficient". The structure is deliberately built around regulatory inspection mandates, which concentrate profit in licensed inspection routes.

### 10. Elevators: the clearest knowledge/access monopoly in field service [BACKGROUND]
OEM economics:
- The Big 4 (Otis, Schindler, KONE, TK Elevator) earn most of their profit from service.
- Otis 2024-25: service is ~60%+ of revenue with segment operating margins ~24-25%, versus new equipment at single digits. It has ~2.4M units under maintenance.
- New-equipment installs are effectively loss-leaders to capture the decades-long maintenance annuity.

Deal value:
- Advent/Cinven bought TK Elevator for ~€17.2B in 2020, one of the largest European LBOs, priced on the service annuity.

Independents:
- Independent service providers (ISPs) such as Oracle Elevator, Champion and other PE-backed platforms compete on price (often 20-40% cheaper contracts).
- They complain of proprietary diagnostic tools, controller lockouts and restricted parts. This is a longstanding "right to repair" grievance, and some building owners and ISPs have raised it with regulators.

Regulators:
- The EU Commission fined Otis, KONE, Schindler and ThyssenKrupp €992M in 2007 for cartel conduct in installation and maintenance.
- The Belgian, Dutch, German and Luxembourg markets were affected.

Interpretation:
- Strong support for C2: proprietary tools and code access lock the maintenance annuity.
- C3 is only partially supported. AI can help ISPs with diagnosis, but proprietary controller access (software locks, service tools) is a legal and technical barrier, not a knowledge one.
- C6: elevator inspection is state/municipal and code-driven (ASME A17.1), another gate.

### 11. Return mechanics across all segments: multiple arbitrage plus leverage [BACKGROUND, investor_view]
Practitioner and PE writing (Bain Global PE reports, Capstone/Harris Williams sector notes) consistently decomposes services roll-up returns into:
- buying small at low multiples (3-8x);
- selling large at high multiples (12-20x+);
- modest organic margin expansion (200-500 bps from procurement, pricing and back-office consolidation);
- leverage.

Risks that showed up in 2023-25:
- Floating-rate debt hurt highly levered platforms; some HVAC and home-services platforms delayed exits.
- The FTC (2023, the U.S. Anesthesia Partners/Welsh Carson case) signalled scrutiny of serial roll-ups. That case concerns healthcare but is relevant precedent for local service markets.

Interpretation:
- The capital flowing into repair is mostly underwriting fragmentation and financial engineering, not productivity technology.
- That supports a narrow reading of C7 (incumbents are not built for "smart"). But it also shows the market rewards control of scarce layers (licences, referrals, inspection routes, labour), not removal of knowledge friction.

### 12. Where the technology lever actually shows up in PE playbooks [BACKGROUND]
Software is cited mainly as an enabler of roll-up margin expansion, not a disruptor of it:
- field service management (ServiceTitan, which IPO'd December 2024)
- CCC Intelligent Solutions in collision (AI estimating, photo-based estimates)
- route optimisation

CCC's AI photo-estimating:
- It has cut estimate cycle time for insurers, the most direct evidence for C4's "diagnose/understand" step dropping in cost.
- The gains accrued to insurers and the data platform (CCC is a ~$6-7B-market-cap software business with ~40%+ adj. EBITDA margins), not to shops.

So when diagnosis gets cheaper, value migrates to whoever owns the data platform and the referral flow.

## Synthesis on the founder's claims

- **C1 (universal chain):** mixed. The chain describes the work, but the commercial chain often starts with mandated inspection (fire, elevator, aviation) or insurer triage (collision), not "notice". In fire safety, inspection comes first and generates the repair. In HVAC, a repair call is often converted into a replacement sale, so the chain branches into "replace" rather than "repair".
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly):** supports, with nuance. The most profitable repair franchises (elevator OEM service ~25% margins, TransDigm ~50%, StandardAero via OEM licences, HEICO/PMA) are monopolies on technical data, tools, approvals and parts. The monopoly is legal and regulatory permission, not just know-how.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap):** contradicts. The gates are licences, certifications, proprietary software locks, insurer referral control and labour. Collision already has largely open repair procedures, and it still consolidated around labour and referral.
- **C4 (order-of-magnitude cost drop in four steps):** contradicts. In HVAC and collision, labour, parts and customer acquisition dominate cost. In aviation, approved parts and turnaround/capacity dominate. Where AI cut diagnosis cost (CCC estimating), value migrated to the platform owner.
- **C5 (AI parts factory):** contradicts in regulated sectors. PMA/DER parts already do alternative sourcing in aviation; the cost is certification data and approval, not fabrication.
- **C6 (inspection essential):** strongly supports. Fire safety, elevators and aviation make inspection both the non-discretionary revenue anchor and the commercial trigger for repair.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient):** mixed. The industry is rationally structured to capture rents at scarce, regulated layers. Fragmented local service is being rationalised by PE, mostly through financial and sales engineering rather than productivity.
