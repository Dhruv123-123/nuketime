# h08: Software analog: open source, Stack Overflow, and now AI coding; did abundant software knowledge lower maintenance cost and who captured value (Red Hat, cloud)? Map the analogy to physical repair.

Research date: 2026-10-20. Note on method: the shared WebSearch budget for this workflow run was used up before this agent began, so no searches were run. All findings below come from direct WebFetch of known primary or near-primary URLs (papers, vendor reports, survey pages, regulator pages, Wikipedia for corporate facts), plus local text extraction of PDFs that were fetched. Gaps that more searching would fill are listed at the end.

## Why software is the right analog

Software is the one domain where the "knowledge monopoly" was broken decades ago, and broken hard. Source code became free (open source, ~1998 onward). How-to and diagnostic knowledge became free and searchable (Stack Overflow, 2008 onward). Since 2022, AI coding assistants have made that knowledge interactive and able to act on it. On top of that, software "parts" (libraries) have zero marginal cost and are delivered instantly (package managers). So software is the most extreme natural experiment we have for founder claims C2-C4: what happens to repair (maintenance) cost and value capture once knowledge and parts are close to free?

## Findings

### F1. Open source made "parts" (code) nearly free on a huge scale [data/study]
- Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou, "The Value of Open Source Software", HBS Working Paper 24-038 (2024). Supply-side value (the cost to rewrite once) is $4.15B. Demand-side value (the cost if every firm had to rebuild what it uses) is about $8.8T, with a range of $2.59T-$13.18T. Without OSS, "firms would need to spend 3.5 times more on software than they currently do." Also: "96% of the demand-side value is created by only 5% of OSS developers." OSS sits in 96% of codebases (Synopsys 2023).
- Source: https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/24-038_51f8444f-502c-4139-8bf2-56eb4b65c58a.pdf (SSRN 4693148)
- Implication: knowledge and parts abundance creates enormous value. But that value is produced by a tiny, concentrated group of experts. The "monopoly" did not disappear; it turned into a small, mostly unpaid elite.

### F2. Even after 20 years of free code plus Stack Overflow, maintenance still eats about 40% of developer time [data, survey]
- Stripe / Harris Poll, "The Developer Coefficient" (Sept 2018). On average developers work 41.1 hours a week, spend 17.3 hours a week on maintenance (debugging, refactoring, "bad code / errors"), and spend 13.5 hours a week on technical debt. About 4 hours a week on "bad code" works out to about $85B a year. The report puts the global GDP opportunity at $3T over ten years.
- Source: https://stripe.com/files/reports/the-developer-coefficient.pdf
- Implication: free knowledge and free parts did NOT cut maintenance by an order of magnitude. Maintenance stayed the largest single use of developer time. Abundance raised the quantity and complexity of software (Jevons-style), so total maintenance load kept growing.

### F3. The total cost of poor software quality keeps rising [data / industry consortium]
- CISQ, "Cost of Poor Software Quality in the US: 2022 Report". At least $2.41 trillion in total, and accumulated technical debt of about $1.52 trillion, described as the "biggest obstacle to making any changes to existing code bases". Supply-chain problems with third-party and OSS components "have risen significantly".
- Source: https://www.it-cisq.org/the-cost-of-poor-quality-software-in-the-us-a-2022-report/
- Implication: once knowledge is abundant, the bottleneck moves to integration, dependency complexity and verification. It does not go away.

### F4. Free parts create a maintenance and obsolescence burden of their own [data / vendor report]
- Black Duck (formerly Synopsys) OSSRA 2026. 98% of codebases contain open source. 87% have at least one vulnerability. 92% contain components 4+ years out of date. 93% contain components with no development activity in 2+ years.
- Source: https://www.blackduck.com/resources/analyst-reports/open-source-security-risk-analysis.html
- Implication: this is the direct analog of "the supplier is out of business". In software, the "part" is still freely available forever and the full design (source) is open, yet 93% of codebases still carry abandoned parts. Having the design and the ability to "manufacture" it (copy or fork) does not solve sustainment. Someone has to own it, keep it up and vouch for it. That cuts against the C5 idea that "bespoke manufacture of a discontinued part is solved".

### F5. The people who produce the knowledge are mostly unpaid [survey]
- Tidelift 2024 Maintainer Impact Report: "Sixty percent of maintainers report they are unpaid hobbyists, and only 12% of maintainers consider themselves professionals who earn most or all of their income from their maintenance work." The report stresses that open source is provided "as is", so the user organization carries responsibility.
- Source: https://www.sonarsource.com/the-2024-tidelift-maintainer-impact-report.pdf (redirected from tidelift.com)
- Implication: the knowledge layer did not capture the value. Knowledge became a commons, and the commons is underfunded.

### F6. A free knowledge commons is fragile, and a single unpaid maintainer is a systemic risk [news/history]
- XZ Utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094, CVSS 10.0). Over two-plus years an attacker social-engineered the original lone maintainer into handing over maintainer rights. A Microsoft engineer, Andres Freund, caught it by chance while chasing an SSH CPU anomaly. Alex Stamos said it "could have been the most widespread and effective backdoor ever planted in any software product."
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XZ_Utils_backdoor
- Implication for physical repair: when knowledge and parts come from anonymous, abundant sources, provenance and trust (inspection) become the critical layer. This is strong support for C6.

### F7. Red Hat: value captured by selling accountability, certification and support for free code [history / corporate]
- Red Hat sold subscriptions for support, certification and updates to software anyone could download. It became "the first one-billion dollar open-source company" (FY2012, $1.13B), passed $2B in 2015 and was near $3B by 2018. IBM bought it for $34B (announced Oct 28, 2018; closed July 9, 2019).
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Hat
- Implication: when the knowledge is free, the paid product is a warranty: certified configurations, a named party to call, lifecycle support, and ISV/hardware certification. That maps to physical repair as "certified repair + parts pedigree + liability", not "knowledge". (Not verified here: Red Hat's June 2023 restriction of RHEL source to customers, which was aimed at rebuilders. It shows that even an open-source champion builds a moat once knowledge is free.)

### F8. The cloud captured the largest share by running the free software as an operated service [data / corporate]
- AWS 2025: revenue about $128.7B and operating income about $45.6B. AWS held about 31% of cloud infrastructure in Q1 2023 (Synergy), against Azure about 25% and GCP about 11%.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Web_Services (citing Amazon Q4 results)
- Licence wars as evidence: MongoDB moved to SSPL in Oct 2018 and Elastic to SSPL/Elastic License in Jan 2021, to stop cloud providers offering their OSS as a service. AWS answered by forking OpenSearch (Apr 2021). Redis moved to SSPL in Mar 2024, which prompted the Linux Foundation's Valkey fork. Redis 8 returned to AGPLv3 on May 1, 2025.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_Side_Public_License
- Implication: the biggest winners were not the knowledge owners or the "parts makers" (OSS authors). They were the operators who took on the whole maintenance job as a managed service: patching, uptime, scaling, compliance. Customers paid to make maintenance someone else's problem. The physical analog is "power-by-the-hour" / fleet-as-a-service / managed uptime, where the operator absorbs the entire notice-to-return chain.

### F9. Stack Overflow: the free diagnostic-knowledge commons was hollowed out by AI, and its corpus was sold to AI [study + data + corporate]
- del Rio-Chanona, Laurentsyeva, Wachs, PNAS Nexus (Sept 2024). Within 6 months of ChatGPT's release, Stack Overflow posting fell about 25% relative to comparison platforms, which the authors call a lower bound. Weekly posts fell from about 60k to about 40k and then to about 30k by spring 2024. Experienced users cut back answers the most. The authors warn of a shift "from public forums to privately owned AI systems" and of degrading training data.
- Source: https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/3/9/pgae400/7754871
- Wikipedia (citing Stack Overflow data): new questions peaked at about 200k a month in 2014. Questions submitted fell 78% from Dec 2024 to Dec 2025.
- Prosus bought Stack Overflow for $1.8B (June 2021). In May 2024 Stack Overflow signed an OverflowAPI data deal with OpenAI.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_Overflow ; https://stackoverflow.co/company/press/archive/openai-partnership
- Implication: AI does not erase the knowledge monopoly; it moves it. Public, human-maintained diagnostic knowledge shrinks while model owners centralize it. For physical repair, AI diagnostics will be only as good as the field-failure data behind them. That data sits with OEMs, MROs and fleet operators, and AI may concentrate it further.

### F10. AI coding: measured productivity gains are real but modest, task-dependent and mixed on maintenance work [RCT studies]
- Peng et al. (2023), arXiv 2302.06590: developers with Copilot finished a greenfield task (an HTTP server in JS) 55.8% faster.
- METR RCT (July 2025): 16 experienced OSS developers, 246 real issues in their own large, mature repos (22k+ stars, 1M+ LOC). AI use made them 19% SLOWER. They had predicted a 24% speedup and afterwards still believed AI had sped them up about 20%.
  - Source: https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/
- METR update (Feb 2026), late-2025 tools: original developers about 18% faster (CI -38% to +9%), new recruits about 4% faster (CI -15% to +9%). METR says this is only weak evidence because of selection effects and that the true speedup is plausibly larger.
  - Source: https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/
- Implication: AI's gains are largest where the knowledge gap is largest (novices, greenfield work). They are smallest for experts maintaining complex legacy systems, which is exactly where "repair" happens. That is real but nowhere near 10x, and it argues against C3 and C4 as stated.

### F11. AI-era code shows quality and maintainability regressions [data / vendor research]
- GitClear 2025 (211M changed lines, 2020-2024). Copy/pasted lines rose from 8.3% to 12.3% of changes. Refactored/"moved" lines fell from 25% (2021) to under 10% (2024). For the first time, copy/paste exceeded moved code. Short-term churn is rising.
  - Source: https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research
- Google DORA 2024: each 25% increase in AI adoption goes with +7.5% documentation quality, +3.4% code quality and +3.1% review speed, but -1.5% delivery throughput and -7.2% delivery stability. 39% report little or no trust in AI code.
  - Source: https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/announcing-the-2024-dora-report
- Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025: 84% use or plan to use AI tools. 46% distrust AI accuracy and only 33% trust it. 66% say their biggest frustration is "almost right, but not quite". 45.2% say debugging AI code takes more time.
  - Source: https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai
- Implication: cheap AI "understanding" and "fixing" moves cost into verification and stability (inspection). The "almost right" problem is the software version of a mis-diagnosed or non-conforming part. In aviation, energy or medical hardware, an "almost right" fix is a safety event. Strong support for C6.

### F12. Regulation is now pushing liability and lifecycle maintenance onto the "manufacturer", including for software built on free parts [regulator]
- EU Cyber Resilience Act: in force 10 Dec 2024. Reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities from 11 Sept 2026, main obligations from 11 Dec 2027. Lifecycle vulnerability handling, third-party (notified body) assessment for some products, CE marking, and a special regime ("open-source steward") for FOSS.
- Source: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cyber-resilience-act
- Implication: as knowledge and parts become abundant, regulators answer by codifying who is accountable, which is a certification and inspection layer. This is the software version of airworthiness, and it supports C6 directly. It also shows that the "rebuild" is driven partly by regulation, not only by technology.

### F13. Legacy systems: even with complete documentation and open specs, scarce hands-on expertise binds [history]
- COBOL ran background processes behind 95% of card swipes as of 2020. In 2020, US state unemployment systems ran short of COBOL programmers. The IRS rushed CARES Act patches to its COBOL Individual Master File and in 2024 announced a move to Java.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COBOL
- Implication: the binding constraint was not "knowledge exists". It was qualified, accountable people available at the moment of failure, with system-specific context. Physical repair is similar: an A&P licence, type ratings, NDT certification.

## Mapping the analogy to physical repair

| Software layer | What abundance did | Who captured value | Physical repair analog |
|---|---|---|---|
| Code / parts (OSS) | Near-zero cost, $8.8T demand-side value | Not the authors (60% unpaid) | Open designs, 3D-printable spares, reverse-engineered parts |
| Diagnostic Q&A (Stack Overflow) | Free, then hollowed out by AI (-78% questions) | Model owners (via data licensing) | Forums, YouTube, iFixit, then AI diagnostic assistants |
| AI coding | Big gains on greenfield/novice work; small or negative for experts on legacy code; quality regressions | Model vendors + tool vendors (Cursor, Copilot) | AI troubleshooting copilots for techs |
| Accountability / certification (Red Hat) | Became THE product once code was free | Red Hat ($34B exit) | Certified MRO, PMA parts, DER approvals, warranties |
| Operations-as-a-service (cloud) | Took over the whole maintenance chain | AWS ($45.6B op income, 2025) | Power-by-the-hour, uptime contracts, fleet service |
| Regulation (CRA) | Assigns lifecycle liability | Notified bodies, compliance tooling | FAA/EASA airworthiness, NRC, UL, FDA |

Key differences that make physical repair HARDER than the software analog:
1. In physical repair, parts have nonzero marginal cost, so "copy" is not free. Each physical part needs materials, a process, and qualification for that specific part (materials allowables, process certs).
2. Delivery takes time and logistics. In software it is instant.
3. A physical failure can kill people, so the verification bar is far higher. Software's tolerance for "almost right" is not available.
4. The physical "parts commons" has no equivalent of GitHub. Design data is proprietary, and the OEM's moat is legal (IP, type certificates), not only epistemic.

Key lessons that DO transfer:
1. When knowledge becomes abundant, total maintenance volume can rise (Jevons) while unit cost falls. Maintenance still took about 40% of developer time (2018) despite free code and Q&A.
2. Value moves to (a) whoever takes responsibility and certifies (Red Hat), (b) whoever operates the whole lifecycle as a service (cloud), and (c) whoever owns the data and models (OpenAI and others, who licensed Stack Overflow content). It does not go to the knowledge producers.
3. AI shifts cost from "understanding" to "verification". Inspection and assurance become the bottleneck and the moat.
4. Abandonment ("supplier gone") persists even when the design is fully open (93% of codebases hold inactive components). Making the part is not the hard bit; sustaining and qualifying it is.

## Stance reasoning
- C1 (universal chain): mixed. Software maintenance fits notice-diagnose-fix-test-deploy, but cloud/SaaS collapsed the chain into continuous operations, and the best value capture comes from making the chain invisible to the customer (managed service), not from optimizing its steps.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly causes inefficiency): mixed. Before OSS, proprietary vendors did hold knowledge monopolies, and breaking them created about $8.8T of value. But inefficiency (CISQ $2.41T, 17 hrs/week maintenance) persisted after the monopoly broke. Complexity, integration and accountability were the larger drivers.
- C3 (AI removes the knowledge gap): contradicts. METR shows experts on mature codebases were not reliably faster. 46% distrust AI output. Stack Overflow data suggest AI re-centralizes knowledge in model owners rather than dissolving the gap.
- C4 (10x cost drop in the four middle steps): contradicts as stated. Even in software, where parts and delivery cost close to zero, measured AI gains run from -19% to about +56%, not 10x, and stability falls (DORA -7.2%).
- C5 (bespoke part making solved): contradicts by analogy. In software, "fabricating" a copy of an abandoned part is free and the design is open, yet 92-93% of codebases still carry outdated or abandoned components. Sustaining, qualifying and owning the part is the hard problem.
- C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports. XZ, Log4j-style supply-chain risk, the EU CRA, DORA's stability drop and the "almost right" frustration all point to verification/attestation as the bottleneck that grows with AI.
- C7 (structurally inefficient, needs rebuild): mixed. Software was "rebuilt" (OSS + cloud + DevOps), and that delivered big gains, but through new structures (managed operations, certification businesses), not by removing intermediaries. New intermediaries (AWS, Red Hat, model vendors) captured the value.
