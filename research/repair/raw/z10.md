## 1. The formal framework already exists (and has for decades)

**[VERIFIED] Wikipedia, "Mean time to repair"** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_time_to_repair)
- MTTR = total corrective maintenance time / number of corrective actions. It "generally excludes lead time for unavailable parts and other Administrative or Logistic Downtime (ALDT)" — examples: part lead times, administrative delays, technician transport.
- Distinguishes mean time to *recovery* (from discovery of failure) vs *repair* (from start of repair). Also "latent fault time" = failure occurrence to detection.

**[VERIFIED] Wikipedia, "Availability"** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability)
- Inherent availability Ai = MTBF/(MTBF+MTTR): excludes logistics and admin delay.
- Achieved availability Aa: assumes personnel, tools, spares "instantly available".
- Operational availability Ao = MTBF/(MTBF+MDT), where MDT includes logistics time, waiting/administrative downtime, preventive and corrective maintenance.

**[FROM MEMORY – verify] DoD RAM-C Rationale Report Manual (2009) / MIL-HDBK-338B / Blanchard, *Logistics Engineering and Management***
- Standard military decomposition: Mean Down Time = Mct (mean active corrective maintenance time) + MLDT (mean logistics delay time: waiting for spares, transport) + MADT (mean administrative delay time: paperwork, waiting for technician/approval, priorities).
- MIL-HDBK-472 / MIL-HDBK-470A further split *active* corrective time into: preparation, fault localization, fault isolation, disassembly, interchange, reassembly, alignment/adjustment, checkout (test). Textbook guidance consistently notes that in electronic systems fault localization/isolation (i.e., diagnosis) is the largest share of *active* repair time, and that ALDT+MLDT routinely dwarfs active repair time in fielded systems.
- Implication: the founder's chain (C1) is essentially the military's existing MDT decomposition. It is not a new insight; it is the standard RAM framework. The interesting question is which buckets dominate in which domain.

**[FROM MEMORY – verify] SEMI E10 (semiconductor equipment)** — splits unscheduled downtime into "maintenance delay" (waiting for technician or parts), repair time, change of consumables, out-of-spec input, facilities-related, and verification/qualification (post-repair test). Fabs measure each separately, which is evidence that the decomposition is measurable and routinely managed in high-value industries.

## 2. Hard data: F-35 (the clearest case of knowledge monopoly + parts delay)

**[VERIFIED] GAO-23-105341, "F-35 Aircraft: DOD and the Military Services Need to Reassess the Future Sustainment Strategy" (Sept 2023)** — https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341 (PDF: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-105341.pdf)
- Mission capable rate ~55% (March 2023), far below goals.
- **NMCS (not mission capable for supply / waiting for parts): "during fiscal year 2022, about 27 percent of the time F-35 aircraft at U.S. units were unable to operate due to the lack of a spare part."** NMCS rose from ~17% (FY2020) to ~27% (March 2023). JPO funded spares only to ~15% NMCS "because funding to anything lower than 15 percent was not affordable." => Parts waiting is the single largest identifiable downtime bucket and is a deliberate budget trade-off, not ignorance.
- **Depot repair cycle time: 172 days (2017) -> 131 days (Aug 2020) -> 141 days (Feb 2023)** vs 60–90 day goal. "Primary impediment ... was a lack of repair material."
- Backlog >10,000 components awaiting repair. DOD sent 73% of components back to the OEM for repair.
- Base-level parts fill: 75% of requisitions filled from on-site stock; of off-installation requisitions, 14% of priority-one and 24% of priority-two still unfilled after 10 days.
- **Knowledge monopoly, explicitly:** "The contractor and the original equipment manufacturer of specific components control the technical data necessary to understand, troubleshoot and repair many of the F-35's components." The "principal reason why an aircraft would be classified as non-mission capable maintenance is that some repairs are very complex and require intensive troubleshooting that maintainers are not able to do because the procedures are not covered in technical data." Units must request contractor field service reps for "routine troubleshooting." **Technical data requests take up to 60 days; complex problems require multiple requests extending 60+ days.** Ejection-seat example: contractor flown to a ship by helicopter.
- Depot manuals "ambiguous and rarely are detailed enough ... depot personnel not only cannot fix the part, but they cannot learn and understand how to fix the part." Data-rights disputes delayed depot workload activation to ~2028. Subcontractor reluctant to share data even with the prime.
- DOD decided at program outset not to buy the technical data package.
- Reading: this is a direct real-world example where (a) "understand/diagnose" delays are measured in weeks and caused by *legal/contractual* withholding of information, not by its non-existence, and (b) parts waiting is the dominant bucket. AI does not obviously fix (a) if the underlying data is proprietary and unavailable for training/grounding; it is a data-rights problem.

## 3. Repair vs replace: time collapses when organic repair capability exists

**[VERIFIED] GAO-20-116 (depot maintenance, DOD continuous process improvement)** — https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-116
- F/A-18 generator repair cut from **90 days to 30 days**, with time between failures quadrupled.
- A gearbox: **95-week replacement lead time vs 4-week repair** once an organic repair process was developed.
- DOD spent ~$19B on maintenance in FY2018; ~84,000 depot personnel.
- Reading: supports C4/C5 direction — when the "source new part from OEM" path is replaced by in-house knowledge plus repair/manufacture, cycle times fall by 3x–24x. But these gains came from process engineering and authority to repair, before AI.

## 4. Navy ships: delays dominated by capacity, workforce and unplanned work, not diagnosis

**[VERIFIED] GAO-20-257T (testimony, Navy maintenance)** — https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-257t
- Navy ships spent **>33,700 more days in maintenance than planned, FY2014–2019**; ~**75%** of maintenance periods finished late; more than half of FY2019 delays exceeded 90 days. Attack-submarine idle time cost >$1.5B in support costs FY2008–2018.
- Causes cited: insufficient shipyard capacity, shortage of skilled personnel, deferred maintenance (growth work discovered once opened).

**[VERIFIED] GAO-20-370 (Navy ship maintenance pilot)** — https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-370
- When work extends into a new fiscal year needing >$4M prior-year funding, approval took **26 to 189 days** — a pure administrative delay (MADT) bucket.
- Delays partly caused by coordinating funding for repairs expected but not quantifiable at contract award.
- Reading: CONTRADICTS a pure knowledge-gap story for heavy assets. The big buckets are physical capacity (dry docks, skilled labor), funding/contract administration, and discovered work. AI diagnosis can help scope "growth work" earlier (e.g., better pre-inspection), but cannot create dry-dock capacity or welders.

**[VERIFIED] GAO-23-106217 (aircraft mission capable rates FY2011–2021)** — https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106217
- Only 4 of 49 aircraft types met mission capable goals in a majority of years; 26 never met them. Causes cited: aging aircraft, maintenance challenges, difficulty obtaining parts. (Product page gives no NMCS/NMCM split.)

## 5. Wind turbines: a few long-logistics failures dominate downtime

**[FROM MEMORY – verify] Faulstich, Hahn & Tavner (2011), "Wind turbine downtime and its importance for offshore deployment," *Wind Energy* 14:327–337** (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/we.421 — 403 in this session)
- From ~ISET/WMEP German onshore data (~1,500 turbines): roughly **75% of failures cause only ~5% of downtime**, while **~25% of failures cause ~95% of downtime** — the latter being major component failures (gearbox, generator, blades) needing cranes, vessels and parts with long lead times.
- Offshore, weather/access windows and vessel availability add a large logistics delay (waiting for technician/vessel), which is neither diagnosis nor understanding.
- Reading: downtime is heavy-tailed; the tail is dominated by logistics of heavy parts and lift equipment, not knowledge. Supports C6/C7 partially, contradicts C3/C4 as the main lever.

**[FROM MEMORY – verify] Carroll, McDonald & McMillan (2016), "Failure rate, repair time and unscheduled O&M cost analysis of offshore wind turbines," *Wind Energy*** — ~350 offshore turbines; average repair *time* (technician hours) for major replacements is on the order of days, but total downtime is much longer because of vessel and parts lead times; failure rate ~10 per turbine-year, mostly minor.

## 6. Avionics: diagnosis is genuinely bad — No Fault Found

**[FROM MEMORY – verify] No Fault Found (NFF) literature (e.g., Söderholm 2007, *Journal of Quality in Maintenance Engineering*; Khan et al. 2014, *Proc IMechE*)**
- Commonly reported that **~30–50% (some studies up to 60%+)** of avionics line-replaceable units removed as faulty test "no fault found" at the shop. Each false removal costs a spare-pipeline slot, shipping, shop test time.
- Reading: SUPPORTS C2/C3/C4 — diagnosis accuracy at the line is poor and causes downstream parts and logistics waste. Better diagnosis (AI over fault logs, fleet data) has a measurable target here. But note the cause is often intermittent faults and test-coverage gaps, i.e., observability/instrumentation, not just knowledge.

## 7. Trucks / commercial vehicles

**[VERIFIED, vendor claim] Decisiv home page** (https://www.decisiv.com/)
- Claims 33% downtime reduction across >14,000 daily service events; multi-day repairs averaging 3.36 days; "minimum 3-day reduction in days out of service for multi-day repair events"; 90% fixed first time; "30% improved efficiency" from technicians starting jobs with needed information.
- Reading: vendor-reported, but consistent with the idea that *communication/approval/information-routing* (MADT) is a big bucket in truck repair. Decisiv's whole product is workflow, not diagnosis.

**[FROM MEMORY – verify] TMC (ATA Technology & Maintenance Council) "Service Event Dwell" / S.2 study group** — industry practitioners repeatedly state that the majority of dwell time is NOT wrench time; it is check-in queue, diagnosis, estimate/authorization, and parts. I could not retrieve a primary number.

## 8. Army logistics: order-ship time halved by process, pre-AI

**[FROM MEMORY – verify] RAND Arroyo Center, "Velocity Management" (Dumond, Eden, Folkeson et al., MR-1108-A, 2001)** (https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1108.html — 403)
- Army repair-parts order-ship time cut from roughly **22 days (1994) to ~8 days (~1999)** — about a 60% reduction — through process mapping (Define-Measure-Improve) of the requisition pipeline segments (requisition submission, depot processing, transportation, receipt). Repair cycle time also cut.
- Reading: the "deliver parts" bucket shrank by ~2.5–3x with process discipline and measurement, not AI. Order-of-magnitude claims need more than knowledge.

## 9. Synthesis

- **Where the time goes:** Across domains with data, the largest buckets of total downtime are (1) waiting for parts / repair material (F-35: NMCS ~27% of all aircraft time; depot cycle 141 days driven by "lack of repair material"), (2) administrative / approval / funding delay (Navy 26–189 days funding approval; truck authorization), and (3) physical capacity (dry docks, skilled labor, cranes, vessels). Active repair ("wrench time") is usually the smallest bucket. Diagnosis is a meaningful bucket in electronics/avionics (NFF 30–50%) and where data is withheld (F-35: 60-day tech data requests).
- **Knowledge monopoly is real but is often legal, not epistemic:** F-35 shows the information exists; it is withheld by contract. An AI model can't reason over a manual it doesn't have. The fix is data rights / right-to-repair plus AI.
- **Order of magnitude:** Documented step-changes are 2.5–3x (Army OST), 3x (F/A-18 generator), up to ~24x for a single part when repair replaces a long-lead new-buy (gearbox 95 -> 4 weeks). 10x across all four middle steps is plausible only for specific parts or classes, not for total downtime, because physical logistics and capacity are a floor.
- **C1 chain:** fully consistent with the long-standing DoD MDT = Mct + MLDT + MADT decomposition; standards bodies (MIL-HDBK-472, SEMI E10) already add post-repair checkout/qualification as a separate state. The founder's chain omits "administrative / approval / funding" as a stage, which the data suggest is one of the largest.
