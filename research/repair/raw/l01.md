## Findings

1. **[VERIFIED] Supply chain disruption costs airlines more than $11bn in 2025 (IATA).** IATA's TechOps program page says: "supply chain challenges will cost the airline industry more than $11 billion in 2025." It also cites 1,254 deliveries in 2024 ("about 30% below pre-COVID peaks") and a record 17,000-jet backlog. https://www.iata.org/en/programs/ops-infra/techops/
   - [RECALL, unverified] The IATA/Oliver Wyman Dec-2024 breakdown is roughly: excess fuel from older aircraft ~$4.2bn, extra maintenance ~$3.1bn, engine leasing ~$2.6bn, and **excess spare-parts inventory holding ~$1.4bn**. Airlines are deliberately overstocking parts as a hedge, which is itself a logistics cost.

2. **[VERIFIED] Parked fleet and engine groundings (IATA, Dec 2024).** About 5,000 of 35,166 aircraft (14%) were parked, 4 percentage points (~1,600 aircraft) above pre-pandemic. About 700 aircraft (2%) were parked specifically for engine inspections. Narrow-body lease rates are 20-30% above 2019. Average fleet age is a record 14.8 years. https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2024-releases/2024-12-10-02/

3. **[VERIFIED] IATA, June 2025.** More than 1,100 aircraft younger than 10 years are in storage (3.8% of the fleet, vs 1.3% in 2015-2018). Nearly 70% of these have PW1000G engines. IATA expects supply chain issues to "persist in 2025 and possibly to the end of the decade." https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2025-releases/2025-06-02-01/

4. **[VERIFIED] PW1000G powder-metal recall: the bottleneck is shop capacity, not knowledge.** The September 2023 recall covered all ~3,000 PW1100G engines. P&W expected an average of ~350 aircraft on the ground through 2026. **Inspections take 250-300 days.** Cost is $6-7bn, about 80% of it customer compensation. The diagnosis (the defect mode) was known; the delay comes from MRO slots, inspection throughput and new parts production. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_%26_Whitney_PW1000G

5. **[VERIFIED] Cost of AOG.** Boeing is cited as estimating that a 1-2 hour AOG costs $10,000-20,000, and up to $150,000 per hour depending on aircraft type and route. AOGs often occur at stations that "lack either inventory, capabilities or facilities." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_on_ground. A high hourly cost is what makes premium expedite (next-flight-out, on-board couriers, charters) economically rational. The logistics premium is small compared with downtime.

6. **[VERIFIED] Oliver Wyman MRO forecast 2025-2035.** MRO spend is $119bn in 2025 (12% above the 2019 record), reaching $156bn by 2035 at a 2.7% CAGR. Fewer than 1,300 aircraft were built in 2024, vs 1,800+ in 2018, because of "supply chain delays and labor and raw material shortages." Engine (GTF/LEAP) inspections continue into 2027. https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2025/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2025-2035.html

7. **[VERIFIED] Military analog (GAO F-35, 2023): the repair pipeline is the binding constraint.** The F-35 mission-capable rate was ~55% in March 2023, "far below program goals." More than 10,000 components were waiting for repair, and component repair times "remained slow." Lifecycle cost is over $1.7tn, of which $1.3tn is operations and sustainment. GAO says DOD must "identify and obtain the technical data needed," which is a direct case of the OEM holding the knowledge and data rights. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341

8. **[VERIFIED] GAO-23-106217 (49 aircraft fleets, FY2011-2021).** Only 4 of 49 fleets met their mission-capable goal in a majority of years, and only 2 of 49 met it in FY2021. Program officials cited "issues with getting parts and supplies." Maintenance costs rose ~$1.2bn even as total O&S costs fell. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106217

9. **[VERIFIED] GAO-24-106703 F-35 sustainment.** Projected sustainment cost rose 44%, to $1.58tn. Of 43 GAO recommendations, 30 (~70%) are unimplemented, including "improving spare parts management." https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106703

10. **[VERIFIED] The structural design of repair logistics: LRU / tiered maintenance (O/I/D levels).** Aviation repair is deliberately split. At the flight line, a sealed line-replaceable unit is swapped from distributed spares near the operating site, and the failed unit then travels to an intermediate shop or depot/OEM for diagnosis and repair. This means "diagnose -> source -> deliver -> repair" is **not one linear chain**. It is two loops: a fast remove-and-replace loop (minutes to hours, gated by whether a spare is positioned nearby) and a slow repair loop for the rotable (weeks to months, gated by shop capacity and OEM data). Level-of-repair analysis trades spares inventory against skill, test equipment and facilities. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-replaceable_unit ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_of_repair_analysis

11. **[VERIFIED] Spares economics: obsolescence.** Up to 23% of service-parts inventory may become obsolete each year (Cohen et al. 2006, cited). After-sales is a small share of sales but a "relatively high share in total profits." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_parts_pricing ; HBR "Winning in the Aftermarket" (2006) https://hbr.org/2006/05/winning-in-the-aftermarket (body paywalled). [RECALL, unverified] The HBR article reports the aftermarket at roughly a quarter of revenue but a much larger share of profit for many manufacturers, and says parts fill rates in many firms are poor despite large inventories.

12. **[VERIFIED] Regulatory gate on alternative parts (PMA).** In the US, non-OEM replacement parts need FAA Parts Manufacturer Approval: identicality, a licence from the OEM, a prior STC, or test and computation. Design approval goes through an ACO and production approval through a MIDO. Since 2011, PMA quality systems must meet 14 CFR 21.137. Any "AI parts factory" in aviation enters through this certification gate, not around it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval

13. **[RECALL, unverified] AOG Technics (2023).** A UK distributor supplied CFM56 parts with forged airworthiness documentation. Parts were found on dozens of engines across multiple airlines, and EASA, the FAA and the UK CAA issued alerts. This shows that the parts-sourcing and traceability step is a **trust/certification** problem: the parts existed and moved fast, but their provenance could not be verified. That supports C6 and argues against a "just source it faster" framing.

14. **[RECALL, unverified] Service parts logistics benchmarks (field service, non-aviation).** Aberdeen and The Service Council surveys typically put average first-time-fix rates around 75-80%. "Part not available on the van/at site" is a leading cause of repeat visits, and best-in-class firms are near 90%. 3PLs (UPS Supply Chain Solutions, DHL Service Logistics, Kuehne+Nagel, CEVA) run networks of hundreds of forward stocking locations to support 2-hour and 4-hour metro delivery SLAs for IT, medical and semiconductor equipment. Aviation AOG desks use next-flight-out, on-board couriers and charters. These are priced at large multiples of standard freight but remain small relative to AOG cost.

15. **[RECALL, unverified] Engine shop visit turnaround times.** These roughly doubled post-2020 for CFM56/LEAP/V2500, from ~60-90 days to well over 100 days at many shops. The causes are long-lead parts (castings, forgings, LLPs) and labour shortages, not diagnostic knowledge.

## Reasoning / synthesis
- **Where time goes in aviation:** Physical delivery is the cheap, solved part. Integrators can move a part almost anywhere in under 24-48 hours, and charter/on-board courier closes most of the remaining gap at a price. The long poles are:
  - (a) whether the part **exists** anywhere: new-build constraints on castings and forgings, rotables stuck in repair queues;
  - (b) **shop capacity** and labour for the repair loop: 250-300-day GTF inspections, 10,000+ F-35 components waiting for repair;
  - (c) **certification and traceability**: PMA, 8130-3/EASA Form 1 paperwork, the AOG Technics case.
- **Knowledge monopoly is real but narrower than C2 states.** In aviation it shows up as data rights and repair-licence control (GAO on F-35 technical data; OEM DER/PMA licensing). It is a legal and IP control. Diagnostic know-how is not the main bottleneck: in the GTF case the defect was fully understood and aircraft still sat for most of a year.
- **Expedite cost is not the inefficiency to attack.** Given AOG at $10k-150k/hour, the industry rationally overspends on speed. The waste sits in inventory: overstock to hedge supply ($1.4bn excess holding, up to 23%/year obsolescence) combined with stockouts of the specific long-lead items.
- **C4 critique:** An order-of-magnitude drop in diagnose plus understand plus source plus deliver would not move aviation repair much, because the dominant cost and time sit in parts manufacture lead times, shop capacity and certification. "Source" is helped more by visibility into who holds a serviceable unit (pooling, marketplaces) than by AI knowledge.
- **C1 critique:** The LRU model shows the chain is really two coupled loops: swap from positioned stock, then repair the rotable. Real systems optimise where in the chain to hold inventory, rather than running the steps in sequence.
