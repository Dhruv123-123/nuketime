## 1. The legal structure of sign-off (hard data: regulation text)

### F1. Return to service is a legal monopoly of named certificate classes (14 CFR 43.7)
- Source: 14 CFR 43.7, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/43.7
- "no person, other than the Administrator, may approve" an aircraft or component for return to service after maintenance, except: mechanic/IA holders (per Part 65), repair stations (Part 145), manufacturers (on their own products, using FAA-approved data except for minor alterations), Part 121/135 air carriers, private pilots (only after preventive maintenance), light-sport repairmen, sport pilots (own aircraft, preventive maintenance).
- Reasoning: the gate is not knowledge, it is a certificate. Someone with perfect knowledge but no certificate cannot release the aircraft. This is the core counterpoint to C2/C3: the "monopoly" is legal-and-liability, not informational.

### F2. Doing the work is far more open than signing it off (14 CFR 43.3)
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/43.3
- Uncertificated "supervised persons" may do work the supervising mechanic is authorized to do, if the supervisor "personally observes the work" and is "readily available in person". Pilots may do preventive maintenance on aircraft they own/operate (not 121/129/135). Pilots may do flight-deck database updates.
- Reasoning: the regulation already separates "hands" from "signature". Labor can be distributed; accountability cannot. AI that helps a supervised person or owner do the work does not change who may sign. This suggests the scarce asset is the signature holder's time and willingness to accept liability, not the know-how.

### F3. The standard of work is "the current manufacturer's maintenance manual" (14 CFR 43.13)
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/43.13
- 43.13(a): persons "shall use the methods, techniques, and practices prescribed in the current manufacturer's maintenance manual" or Instructions for Continued Airworthiness, or other methods acceptable to the Administrator. Must use manufacturer-recommended special tools or FAA-accepted equivalents. 43.13(b): result must be "at least equal to its original or properly altered condition".
- Reasoning: SUPPORTS C2 in a specific way: OEM-authored data is written into law as the default standard. The knowledge monopoly is codified: deviation requires "acceptable to the Administrator" data (e.g., DER-approved repair, STC, Field Approval Form 337). An AI-generated repair procedure is not approved data; it would need an engineering approval path. CONTRADICTS C3: AI knowing the answer does not make the answer legally usable.

### F4. Repair stations must hold current OEM data on premises (14 CFR 145.109)
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/145.109
- Equipment "must be located on the premises and under the repair station's control"; tools "must be those recommended by the manufacturer of the article" or FAA-accepted equivalents; calibrated to FAA-acceptable standards. Data (maintenance manuals, overhaul manuals, service bulletins, ADs, ICAs) "must be current and accessible when the relevant work is being done."
- Reasoning: OEM data access is a certification prerequisite, so OEM data licensing (CMM/overhaul manual subscriptions) is a toll gate for independent MROs. [BACKGROUND, unverified this run] ARSA and independent MROs have long complained that OEMs restrict or price ICAs/CMMs; FAA 21.50(b) requires holders of design approvals to make ICAs available, but CMMs for components are often contested as not "ICA". This is the closest regulatory analog of "right to repair" in aviation and SUPPORTS C2.

### F5. Who signs at a repair station (14 CFR 145.157)
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/145.157
- US repair stations: person approving return to service must be certificated under Part 65 (mechanic or repairman). Foreign repair stations: 18 months practical experience or training, familiar with regs. All must read/write English.
- Reasoning: the repairman certificate is tied to an employer (site-specific), so the certificate system binds expertise to organizations. Signature authority is organizational, not portable.

### F6. Mechanic certificate: 18/30 months or a Part 147 school (14 CFR 65.77)
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/65.77
- Experience route: 18 months on one rating, 30 months concurrent for A&P; or graduation from a certificated AMT school (Part 147). Plus written, oral and practical tests.

### F7. Inspection Authorization (IA) is a second, scarcer gate (14 CFR 65.91)
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/65.91
- Requires A&P held at least 3 years, actively engaged for the 2 years before applying, a fixed base of operations, available equipment/facilities/inspection data, and a written test (90-day wait on failure). IA is needed to sign major repair/alteration return to service (Form 337) and annual inspections on GA aircraft.
- Reasoning: the inspection/sign-off layer is the narrowest human bottleneck in GA. SUPPORTS C6 (inspection essential) and shows it is a credentialed human role.

### F8. EASA: certifying staff licensed by category (Part-66) inside approved organisations (Part-145)
- Source: EASA FAQ Part-66, https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/the-agency/faqs/part-66
- Categories: A (minor scheduled line maintenance, simple defect rectification), B1 (mechanical/structures/powerplant, no avionics troubleshooting), B2/B2L (avionics), B3 (small non-pressurised aeroplanes <=2,000 kg), L (light aircraft), C (base maintenance release). Licences exist for "certifying the release to service of work performed on aircraft." Type ratings needed for complex aircraft.
- Reasoning: EASA splits sign-off into finer slices than the FAA (A&P is broad; EASA is category + type). Even more credential-gated, more labor-intensive. [BACKGROUND, unverified this run] EASA Part-145 requires an accountable manager, a quality/compliance system, a Maintenance Organisation Exposition, and since 2022-2023 a Safety Management System per Reg. (EU) 2021/1963.

### F9. Form 1 / 8130-3: the part's passport [BACKGROUND, unverified this run; fetch of faa.gov page returned 403]
- FAA Form 8130-3 "Authorized Release Certificate, Airworthiness Approval Tag" serves both as an export/conformity approval for new parts (issued by production approval holders or designees) and as a maintenance release for used parts (issued by Part 145 stations). EASA Form 1 is the European equivalent. Under the US-EU bilateral (BASA/MIP and Maintenance Annex Guidance), a US Part 145 station holding EASA approval can issue a dual-release 8130-3 accepted in the EU.
- Reasoning: a part without trace paperwork is essentially worthless in commercial aviation, regardless of its physical condition. Value is in the paper trail, which makes "sourcing" (C4) really a documentation/provenance problem, and makes "make it yourself" (C5) blocked unless a production approval (PMA, TSO, PC) or an owner-produced-part route (14 CFR 21.9(a)(5)) exists.

## 2. What happens when the paper trail fails (hard data: cases)

### F10. AOG Technics: ~60,000 CFM56 parts with forged release paperwork
- Source: Wikipedia, Bogus aircraft parts, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogus_aircraft_parts
- Between 2019 and 2023 the UK company supplied an estimated 60,000 parts for CFM56 engines with forged documents (forged EASA Form 1s). Director pleaded guilty to fraudulent trading and was sentenced February 2026 to 56 months. [BACKGROUND] Airlines including Southwest, United, American, Delta, TAP and Virgin Australia found affected parts, and several engines were taken off wing in 2023.
- Reasoning: SUPPORTS C6 and C7 jointly. The industry trusts paper, not parts, so a single forger defeated the system for four years. This is an argument that inspection/provenance must evolve (digital, cryptographic, physical-verification). It CONTRADICTS a naive C4: making sourcing cheaper and faster increases forgery risk unless provenance verification also scales.

### F11. Unapproved parts history: FAA linked them to 174 accidents/incidents (1973-1996), about 24 crashes 2010-2016
- Source: same Wikipedia page, citing FAA study. 17 fatalities (1973-96); ~24 crashes, 7 deaths, 18 injuries (2010-16). Partnair 394 (1989) tail separation from counterfeit bolts.
- Reasoning: low counts relative to flights; the sign-off system is "inefficient" by design to keep this tail small. Context against C7: the inefficiency buys a safety record.

### F12. Alaska 1282 (Jan 2024): the failure was a missing removal record, not missing knowledge
- Source: NTSB DCA24MA063, https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/DCA24MA063.aspx
- Probable cause: Boeing's "failure to provide adequate training, guidance, and oversight" so manufacturing staff consistently followed its parts removal process. The four bolts holding the door plug were never found. The FAA's ineffective compliance surveillance was a contributing factor. 19 new recommendations, including clarifying "when a removal record is required", tracking recurring nonconformances, retaining audit records over 5 years, and an independent safety-culture review.
- Reasoning: even at the OEM, with all the knowledge in the world, the failure was documentation and process discipline. Strongly CONTRADICTS C3 as a sufficient fix. Knowledge was never the gap; procedural traceability was. SUPPORTS C6: the inspection/record layer is where catastrophic risk sits, and it needs better tooling.

## 3. Workforce and the sign-off bottleneck (hard data)

### F13. ~330,000 certificated mechanics/repairmen (Dec 2018); over half aged 50-70; FAA curriculum outdated
- Source: GAO-20-206, https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-206
- FAA acknowledged AMT school curriculum and testing standards were outdated (later addressed by the Part 147 rewrite, 2022, and the mechanic ACS).

### F14. ATEC 2026 Pipeline Report: 44% of certificated mechanics over 60; record 11,103 new certificates in 2025
- Source: ATEC, https://www.atec-amt.org/pipeline-report
- 44% over 60 (+3 pts vs 2025 report); >39,000 retirements expected in 10 years. Oliver Wyman: North American shortfall ~25,000 technical personnel in 2026 incl. 9,100 certificated mechanics; certificated mechanic deficit ~20,000 by 2030 (12% of demand). FAA issued record 11,103 certificates in 2025 (+23.2%); school route 7,496 (+27%); military 1,375. AMTS enrollment 27,740 (+13.5%), 62% load factor. Women 3.0%.
- Reasoning: the scarce input is licensed human signatures, and it is aging out. AI that raises the throughput of each certificate holder (documentation, task cards, troubleshooting) attacks the real constraint; AI that "replaces knowledge" does not remove the need for the signature.

### F15. Boeing 2026 Pilot and Technician Outlook: 728,000 new maintenance technicians needed globally 2026-2045
- Source: https://www.boeing.com/commercial/market/pilot-technician-outlook

### F16. ARSA surveys: technician shortage costs ~$100M/month (2019); skills gap could cost repair stations $1.95B in revenue (2017)
- Source: ARSA economic data page, https://arsa.org/news-media/economic-data/ (headlines only, methodology not visible). Opinion/industry-advocacy data.

## 4. Liability shaping the chain

### F17. GARA (1994): an 18-year statute of repose for GA manufacturers, with a rolling clock for replaced parts
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Aviation_Revitalization_Act
- US GA production fell from ~18,000 units (1978) to 928 (1994) under liability pressure; roughly doubled within five years of GARA. The repose clock restarts for any part on its installation date.
- Reasoning: liability law visibly shapes the industry's output, and it pushes liability downstream onto the last person who touched or replaced something, i.e., the mechanic/repair station that signed. This is a structural reason why signers are conservative, prefer OEM parts and data, and why independent makers of replacement parts carry fresh liability. Relevant to C5: an "AI parts factory" making a part restarts a liability clock that it alone carries.

### F18. [BACKGROUND, unverified this run] Signature = personal liability and certificate action
- The mechanic or repair station signing a 43.9 maintenance record entry or 8130-3 is subject to FAA certificate action (suspension/revocation under 49 USC 44709) and civil penalties; falsification is a criminal matter (14 CFR 43.12, 145.12; 18 USC 38 on fraud involving aircraft parts). Insurers price repair stations largely on their quality system and the data they use. This means signers rationally refuse non-approved data even when it is technically correct.

## Synthesis

- The chain: notice -> diagnose -> data -> parts -> work -> inspect -> sign off is real and codified (43.9 records, 43.11 inspection records, 8130-3 for parts). But C1 misses that every step generates a record, and the record, not the physical step, is what makes the aircraft legal. The chain is closer to "a chain of signatures attached to a chain of tasks".
- The "knowledge monopoly" exists, but it is mostly a legally codified data monopoly (43.13 references OEM manuals; 145.109 requires them on site; deviation needs approved data) plus a credential monopoly (43.7). AI dissolves neither by itself. A model that knows the repair is not "approved data", and a model cannot hold a certificate.
- Where cost and time actually go at sign-off: certified-human time (IA/B1/C signatures, scarce and aging), approved-data access (OEM licensing, DER/engineering approvals for deviations), and part provenance (8130-3/Form 1 trace). Failures (AOG Technics, Alaska 1282) are record-and-process failures.
- Inspection/certification must evolve (C6 strongly supported): digital trace, tamper-evident release certificates, automated record checking, AI-assisted conformity inspection. That evolution has to come through the regulator (FAA/EASA acceptance), so it is slow.
- C7: the system is deliberately slow and conservative because liability and safety push that way; "built to be inefficient" is partly wrong. It is built to make accountability legible. There is real rent-seeking at the edges (OEM data licensing, OEM-controlled aftermarket), but the core structure is a safety design choice.

## Company shapes the evidence points at
1. Signature-throughput tools: AI copilots for certificated staff that pre-draft 43.9 entries, task cards, 337s, and check records against ADs/SBs, so one IA or B1 signs more, faster, with less risk. Targets the real scarcity (F13-F15).
2. Provenance / back-to-birth trace: digital, cryptographically verifiable 8130-3/Form 1 and records, plus automated forgery detection across brokers (F10, F12). Buyers: airlines, lessors, insurers.
3. Approved-data factory: engineering-approval services (DER/DOA-backed) that use AI to produce repair data and get it FAA/EASA-approved faster: a route from AI knowledge to legally usable data (F3).
4. PMA / AM replacement parts with the approvals included: the C5 idea only works if it includes design and production approval and accepts the liability (F17).
5. Records auditing for lessor transitions (lease returns are dominated by records review) [BACKGROUND].
