## Findings (hard data unless marked)

### 1. Öko-Institut / Uni Bonn for Umweltbundesamt, "Einfluss der Nutzungsdauer von Produkten auf ihre Umweltwirkung: Strategien gegen Obsoleszenz", UBA Texte 11/2016 (English summary pp. 40-50)
URL: https://www.umweltbundesamt.de/system/files/medien/378/publikationen/texte_11_2016_einfluss_der_nutzungsdauer_von_produkten_obsoleszenz.pdf
- Large household appliances (GfK data, Germany): average **first useful service life fell from 14.1 years (2004) to 13.0 years (2012/13)**. Average age at replacement due to a defect fell by one year, to 12.5 years.
- Defects were still the main replacement reason: **57.6% of replacements in 2004, 55.6% in 2012**.
- **30.5% of large appliances replaced in 2012/13 still worked**. The owner wanted a better device.
- **Replacements within less than 5 years due to a defect rose from 3.5% to 8.3% of all replacements (2004 to 2012)**. The study calls this the "critical" finding.
- Washing machines at recycling centres (capacitor date codes): **average age at disposal fell from 16 years (2004) to 13.7 years (2013)**. **More than 10% were 5 years old or less in 2013, against 6% in 2004.** The reasons for replacement were unknown.
- Uni Bonn online survey 2013/14: washing machines disposed of at 11.6 years on average, 69% because of a defect.
- Flat-screen TVs: **over 60% of working flat screens were replaced in 2012 because the owner wanted a better device**. Defects accounted for only 25-28% of TV replacements.
- Notebooks: **"A clear trend that notebooks break sooner over the time cannot be confirmed from the data."** Age at defect-driven replacement actually rose, from 4.8 years (2004) to 6.2 years (2012), which suggests failure rates were going down. This contradicts a simple "everything breaks sooner" story.
- Smartphones (Stiftung Warentest): only 9% of people who changed phones within 3 years cited the battery. 40% wanted a better device and 28% got a new phone through their contract. Conclusion: **"psychological obsolescence plays the leading role."**
- Planned obsolescence: the study did not set out to prove or refute it. It found "very diverse reasons" for replacement, but that "devices more and more are replaced ... after shorter periods of use."
- Economic obsolescence (TVs): repairing the display or power board "run[s] into the hundreds of euro", while new-set prices keep falling. The barrier table lists: repair costs high relative to new prices; excessive component integration; no spare parts or only original parts; excessive call-out fees.
- Environment: a washing machine that lasts 5 years has about **40% higher cumulative energy demand and global warming potential** than one that lasts 20 years. Over 20 years that is about 1,100 kg more CO2e.

### 2. UN/ITU/UNITAR Global E-waste Monitor 2024
URL: https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024/
- **62 Mt of e-waste in 2022, up 82% since 2010**, growing by about 2.6 Mt per year. Generation is growing **5 times faster than documented recycling**.
- **Documented collection and recycling: 22.3%.** Projected to reach 82 Mt by 2030, with the recycling rate falling to 20%.
- **US$62 billion of recoverable resources are not accounted for.** Reaching 60% collection by 2030 would give net benefits above US$38 billion.
- Recycling meets only 1% of rare-earth demand.
- The GEM 2020 (quoted in the FTC report) attributes the growth to "higher consumption rates of EEE, short life cycles, and few repair options." That is the UN's own causal claim, not a measurement.

### 3. European Commission impact assessment, Right to Repair Directive, SWD(2023) 59/60, 22 March 2023
Council docs 7767/23 ADD 2/3. Executive summary: https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-7767-2023-ADD-3/en/pdf ; press release: https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_23_1794
- Every year in the EU, premature disposal of repairable goods causes **261 Mt CO2e, 35 Mt of waste and 30 Mt of resources**. Consumers lose **almost EUR 12 billion a year** by replacing instead of repairing.
- Narrow market-failure estimate: **EUR 5.1 billion a year**. This counts only "reluctant replacers" (9.2% of consumers), who tried to repair, failed and then replaced. The upper range is EUR 5-25 billion a year, or EUR 62-307 billion NPV over 15 years. The value of repairable consumer durables is EUR 792 billion a year.
- Consumer segments: enthusiastic repairers 13.4%, reluctant repairers 37.8%, reluctant replacers 9.2%, enthusiastic replacers 39.5%. **Close to 40% of consumers do not want to repair at all.**
- Gap between actual use and designed lifetime, in years: **phones 1.7 vs 7; TVs 7 vs 22; refrigerators 10 vs 20; laptops 4 vs 7; cars 11.5 vs 18.**
- **Consumers will pay at most about 20% of the purchase price for a repair** (17% for cars, 27% for smartphones). Of consumers who did not repair, between 25% (clothing) and 50% (dishwashers) said it was too expensive.
- Diagnosis is a gate: "Finding out how much repair will cost ... often requires the repairer to carry out a diagnostic ... consumers need to first pay the diagnostic". The cost of diagnosis is 7% (shoes) to 22% (cars) of the cost of common repairs. "Due to uncertainty about these upfront costs many consumers abandon the option of repair." **This is direct evidence that the cost of the diagnose step suppresses repair.**
- Information gap: 19% of smartphone owners did not repair because they could not find out how, or no service was available. Hassle and time also matter: 14% said repair would take too long, and 14% did not repair fridges or TVs because of the effort of transporting them.
- Most defects occur outside the legal guarantee. Example: phones 5.5% defect rate a year, of which 1.9% fell under the guarantee.
- 27% did not repair a phone because they wanted a new model.
- Cars are the outlier: **52% of consumers repair rather than replace a defective car.** The product with the deepest independent repair ecosystem and the most open information shows the highest repair rate.
- Benefits of the preferred option: 18.4 Mt CO2 saved over 15 years, EUR 176.5 billion in consumer savings over 15 years, about 8,000 repair jobs, and +EUR 4.9 billion GVA in repair. 82% of repair employees in the EU work for SMEs.
- The Repair Café International top barriers for 2019 (1,582 observations) were "no spare parts available", "irreparable" and "unable to open". These are design and parts problems, not knowledge problems.
- An older study (Bayus 1988) linked advertising for new TVs to falling employment of TV service technicians.

### 4. EEB, "Coolproducts don't cost the earth", September 2019
URL: https://eeb.org/library/coolproducts-report/ (PDF https://eeb.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Coolproducts-report.pdf)
- Extending the life of all EU washing machines, notebooks, vacuum cleaners and smartphones by one year would save **about 4 Mt CO2 a year by 2030, equal to more than 2 million cars**. By product: smartphones 2.1, notebooks 1.6, washing machines 0.25, vacuum cleaners 0.1 Mt.
- Typical lifetimes: washing machines 11 years; laptops 4-5; vacuum cleaners 5-8; smartphones 3. To offset their manufacturing emissions they would need to last: washing machines 17-23 years, notebooks 20-44, vacuum cleaners 11-18, smartphones 25-232.
- Eurobarometer: **77% of EU citizens would rather repair than buy new**, but are put off by the cost of repair and the level of service.
- Causes of shorter lifetimes, citing Prakash et al.: robustness, software, **high cost of repair**, and fashion. Manufacturing accounts for 40-64% of a notebook's lifecycle global warming potential.
- This is a campaigner source, but the arithmetic draws on peer-reviewed LCAs.

### 5. FTC, "Nixing the Fix: An FTC Report to Congress on Repair Restrictions", May 2021
URL: https://www.ftc.gov/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions
- **"There is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions."** The record "contains no empirical evidence" that independent shops are more likely to cause cyber or safety harm.
- The record "does not establish that repairs conducted by independent repair shops would be inferior ... if [they] were provided with greater access to service manuals, diagnostic software and tools, and replacement parts." This is regulator support for C2 in its access-restriction form.
- **About 70-75% of car owners use independent shops** after the warranty ends, mostly for trust, convenience and price. Alternative collision parts are 15-50% cheaper than OEM parts.
- "Basically, 100% of manufacturers have restrictions on repair in every one of their [EULAs]."
- E-waste dispute: CTA and CompTIA argued that e-waste peaked in 2013-14 and is now falling. The FTC found that its cited sources "[do not] appear to support" that claim. The US generates 6.9 million tons of e-waste a year, or 176 lb per household (PIRG).
- US PIRG: repair could cut household spending on electronics and appliances by 22%, about **$330 per family per year**. This is an advocacy estimate.
- Low-income households: only 48% of adults earning under $40k could cover a $400 emergency expense in cash.
- Apple's battery programme: it planned to replace 1-2 million iPhone batteries and ended up replacing more than 11 million. **Cheap, easy repair brought out large latent demand.**

### 6. PROMPT project (EU H2020) consumer survey, 2021: lifecycle and replacement reasons in BE, FR, IT, PT and ES
URL: https://prompt-project.eu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/2021_PROMPT-lifecycle-and-replacement-reasons-report.pdf
- Share of replacements for reliability reasons (out of use, not working well, repair costs too high, no spare parts): **washing machines 75%, vacuum cleaners 64%, smartphones 36%, TVs 34%.**
- Share replaced while still working, cited by the EC IA: washing machines 26.3%, smartphones 63.4%, TVs 64.2%, vacuum cleaners 36.5%.
- Implication: for phones and TVs, most replacement is driven by preference or fashion, not by failure or repair friction. Better repair knowledge would not change that. For workhorse appliances, failure and repair cost drive replacement.

### 7. BLS CPI, televisions (CUUR0000SERA01, annual average index)
Source: BLS public API, https://api.bls.gov/publicAPI/v2/timeseries/data/CUUR0000SERA01
- Index **1831 (2006), 708 (2010), 347 (2014)**: TV quality-adjusted prices fell about 81% in 8 years.
- [UNVERIFIED, from memory] The widely cited AEI/BLS "chart" (Mark Perry) shows TV prices down about 97% since 2000 while CPI labour-intensive services rose. This BLS TED headline was not fetched (404 on the guessed URL).
- Mechanism: manufactured goods fall in price because of automation and offshoring, while repair is local, skilled labour whose cost tracks wages (Baumol cost disease). Combined with the ~20% acceptable-price threshold, repair stops making economic sense for low-value goods. This is structural economics, not a knowledge monopoly.

## Reasoning / synthesis

1. **Products are being kept for shorter periods, but less dramatically than the rhetoric suggests.** For large appliances in Germany, first use fell from 14.1 to 13.0 years over about 8 years, roughly 8%. Early failures doubled from 3.5% to 8.3% of replacements, but from a small base. Notebooks did not get less reliable. The big gaps between designed and used lifetime are in phones (1.7 vs 7 years) and TVs (7 vs 22), and those are mostly driven by preference.
2. **E-waste is clearly growing** (62 Mt, +82% since 2010, 22% recycled). The UN attributes this partly to "few repair options", but most of the growth comes from consumption volume and a growing installed base.
3. **The cost to the economy is large but moderate in GDP terms.** EU consumer detriment is about EUR 12 billion a year (EUR 5-25 billion range), set against EUR 792 billion of repairable durables bought each year. Environmental externality: 261 Mt CO2e a year.
4. **Why consumers do not repair**, ranked by the EC survey and PROMPT: (a) price relative to new, with a ~20% threshold; (b) preference for new or fashion (~40% "enthusiastic replacers", ~60% of working TVs and phones replaced); (c) hassle and time (downtime, transport); (d) parts and design (no parts, cannot open); (e) information (where to repair, what it will cost, the paid diagnostic gate). Knowledge or information is a real factor, but it is one of five and not the dominant one in consumer goods.
5. **Where AI plausibly helps:** remote or self-diagnosis that removes the paid diagnostic gate and the uncertainty about price; matchmaking and transparency (exactly what the EC directive tries to legislate); and identifying parts. These map to C4's "diagnose" and "understand" steps, and the EU evidence says uncertainty at these steps causes consumers to abandon repair.
6. **Where AI does not help:** the labour cost of hands-on repair relative to falling new-goods prices; parts that do not exist or are design-locked (glued batteries, serialisation); consumer preference for new; and downtime. The cars case (52% repair, 70-75% at independents) shows that even with open information and a deep aftermarket, replacement decisions still follow economics.
7. **The knowledge-monopoly framing (C2)** is better supported in its narrow form, "OEMs restrict access to manuals, diagnostics and parts" (FTC: scant justification), than in the form "people lack knowledge". Most consumers never try to repair, and when they do, the barriers are mostly price, parts and hassle.

## Stances (summary)
- C1: silent or mixed. The EC IA describes a repair process (find a repairer, transport, diagnose and quote, wait for parts, repair), which roughly matches C1. It adds a decision gate that C1 omits: repair vs replace vs abandon, based on the price relative to new (~20%) and on preference. Many failures never enter the chain.
- C2: mixed. The FTC supports restricted access to manuals, diagnostics and parts. EU surveys show information is one barrier among several, and cost, preference and hassle dominate.
- C3: contradicts or silent. Nothing in the lifetime data suggests closing the knowledge gap solves replacement. Psychological obsolescence and economic obsolescence (labour vs cheap new goods) are untouched by knowledge.
- C4: mixed. Diagnosis costs are 7-22% of repair costs, and uncertainty about the quote drives abandonment, so cheaper diagnosis matters. But labour, downtime and parts availability set the ~20% threshold, so an order-of-magnitude drop in only these four steps would not flip most low-value goods.
- C5: contradicts or silent. Top barriers are "no spare parts available" and "unable to open", so demand for parts is real. Nothing here shows bespoke manufacturing solves it at consumer price points.
- C6: silent (consumer goods). Trust in repair quality scores 8.2/10 as a decision factor. That supports some role for quality assurance, but not certification specifically.
- C7: mixed. There is evidence of structural incentives against repair: 100% of EULAs restrict repair, OEMs profit from "high turnover", advertising works against repair. But much of the decline in repair is rational economics (Baumol) plus consumer preference, not just industry design.
