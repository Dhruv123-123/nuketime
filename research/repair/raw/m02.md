## Findings

### 1. Cost per hour of AOG [VERIFIED via secondary source]
- Wikipedia "Aircraft on ground" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_on_ground) cites a Boeing Commercial Airplanes Operations Center page archived in 2008. Boeing's estimate: "a 1-2 hour AOG situation can cost $10,000 to $20,000, and possibly up to $150,000 per hour", depending on aircraft type and route.
- AOG is defined as the aircraft being "temporarily unairworthy". Causes range from an inoperative light bulb to damage to an engine or airframe structure. AOG events are "largely unscheduled and often occur where inventory, capabilities or facilities are lacking."
- Note: this is the figure the industry quotes everywhere, and it is an OEM marketing number from 2008. There is no audited, recent cost-per-hour dataset in the public domain. The cost is driven by cancellations, passenger compensation (EU261), crew timeouts and network knock-on effects, not by the repair itself.

### 2. Scale of the supply-chain problem: grounded fleet [VERIFIED]
- IATA press release, 10 Dec 2024 (https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2024-releases/2024-12-10-02/):
  - About 5,000 aircraft (14% of the 35,166-aircraft global fleet) were parked. That is 4 percentage points above pre-pandemic levels, about 1,600 extra aircraft.
  - About **700 aircraft (2% of the global fleet) were parked for engine inspections**. This is mostly the Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal issue, plus LEAP durability problems. IATA expected this to persist into 2025.
  - Average fleet age is a record 14.8 years. Narrowbody lease rates are 20-30% above 2019.
  - Deliveries were about 1,254 in 2024, a 30% shortfall. The backlog is 17,000 aircraft, or 14 years at current rates.
- Interpretation: today, the biggest grounding is not caused by knowledge gaps. It comes from limited engine shop-visit capacity and parts (castings, forgings, LLPs), a fleet-wide engineering defect, and OEM production shortfalls. Every party knows what the problem is and how to fix it. What they lack is shop slots and certified hardware.

### 3. Supply-chain cost to airlines [BACKGROUND, unverified this session]
- In Oct 2025, IATA and Oliver Wyman published an estimate that aerospace supply-chain problems cost airlines more than $11bn in 2025. My recollection of the split: about $4.2bn excess fuel (older, less efficient aircraft kept flying), about $3.1bn extra maintenance (older fleets), about $2.6bn engine leasing (spares needed to cover groundings), and about $1.4bn extra inventory holding. Verify the exact figures.
- Over 2023-2025, the Oliver Wyman MRO surveys repeatedly ranked material/parts availability and lengthened component turnaround times (TAT) as the top MRO pain point, ahead of labor shortages. Verify.

### 4. Military analog: depot backlog and OEM dependence [VERIFIED]
- GAO-23-105341 (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341):
  - The F-35 mission-capable rate was about 55% in March 2023, well below goals.
  - **More than 10,000 components were awaiting repair**, above desired levels. Component repair times "remained slow."
  - DOD "relies heavily" on the contractor (Lockheed) for sustainment. Intellectual property and technical data are explicitly flagged as gaps that stop the government from doing its own repairs.
  - The services must take over sustainment management by Oct 2027.
  - Life-cycle cost is over $1.7T, of which $1.3T is operations and sustainment.
- GAO-24-106703 (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106703):
  - Availability has "trended downward considerably over the past 5 years". No variant meets its goals.
  - Projected sustainment cost rose 44%, from $1.1T (2018) to $1.58T (2023).
  - Air Force cost is $6.6M per aircraft per year against a $4.1M target.
  - 30 of GAO's 43 recommendations (about 70%) remain unimplemented. They include spare-parts management.
- Interpretation: this is the closest documented case of a **knowledge/data-rights monopoly** causing downtime. It supports C2 in a specific form: the restriction is legal and contractual (IP, tech data), not cognitive. AI cannot read data the operator has no rights to. The binding constraint is a backlog of physical repairs, which suggests repair capacity and parts, not diagnosis.

### 5. MEL / deferral reduces how many faults become AOG [VERIFIED]
- Wikipedia "Minimum equipment list" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_equipment_list): the manufacturer's MMEL, approved and tailored into the operator's MEL, lets an aircraft dispatch with listed equipment inoperative. A technician confirms the fault, checks MEL conditions, and placards the item. Each deferral is limited to a set number of hours, cycles or calendar days. The CDL and NEF lists cover structure and cabin items.
- Implication for C1: most real faults do not follow the full chain before return to service. The usual sequence is notice, then a quick confirm, then defer, then fly, with diagnosis and repair scheduled later at a hub where parts and people are. So AOG is mostly the subset of faults that are not deferrable or occur away from base, plus structural damage (bird strike, ground-handling damage, lightning) and engine events.

### 6. How AOG support works [BACKGROUND, unverified this session]
- **Airline maintenance control centre (MCC)**: receives the fault, often before landing, from ACARS/aircraft health monitoring such as Boeing AHM and Airbus Skywise Health Monitoring. It works out MEL dispatch options and troubleshooting. It dispatches line technicians or contract line maintenance at outstations.
- **OEM 24/7 desks**: the Boeing Operations Center in Seattle connects airlines to engineering and parts. Airbus AIRTAC is the technical AOG centre, with a 24/7 spares AOG desk. Engine OEMs (GE, CFM, P&W, RR) run their own desks. Their roles include engineering dispositions for repairs outside the Structural Repair Manual (SRM) and approving repair schemes. That approval is often the long pole in structural AOGs, because it requires design-authority (DOA/ODA) sign-off.
- **Field service representatives (FSRs)**: OEMs station FSRs at major customers. For heavy damage, OEMs send AOG repair teams. Boeing AOG teams and Airbus repair teams go out for structural repairs, sometimes taking days to weeks.
- **Parts logistics**: there are ATA Spec 2000 priority codes (AOG, critical, routine). AOG parts move "next flight out" by on-board courier or charter. Pool and power-by-the-hour programs exist, such as Lufthansa Technik TCS, AFI KLM E&M, and OEM pools. Brokers and the used serviceable material (USM) market use ILS/PartsBase to find a part with valid release paperwork (FAA 8130-3 / EASA Form 1). Without the paperwork, a part is unusable.
- **Sign-off**: a licensed engineer issues the certificate of release to service (CRS), or an A&P/IA makes the return-to-service entry. Where a required inspection item (RII) applies, there is also an independent inspection.

### 7. Time breakdown: diagnosis vs parts wait vs repair vs sign-off [NO HARD PUBLIC DATA FOUND]
- I found no public, recent, quantified split of AOG elapsed time by stage. Airlines and MROs treat it as internal KPI data. Without search I could not reach practitioner surveys that may exist (Aviation Week MRO, ICF, AeroDynamic Advisory).
- Indirect evidence and widely held practitioner views [BACKGROUND]:
  - **Waiting for parts (and paperwork) dominates long AOGs.** Line repair of a line-replaceable unit (LRU) swap takes minutes to hours once the part is on site. Getting it to an outstation takes hours to days. This is why airlines hold costly rotable pools and fly-away kits, and why AOG brokers and couriers exist. The 2024-25 supply crunch, with component TATs and shop-visit TATs reportedly up 30-100%+ versus pre-COVID, made parts wait the dominant stage.
  - **Diagnosis matters mainly through misdiagnosis.** Industry literature often cites no-fault-found rates on unscheduled LRU removals of about 30-50% for some avionics. Technicians "shotgun" parts to clear a fault quickly, which turns a diagnosis problem into a parts/rotable-pool cost problem. This is the one place where better diagnosis (AI-assisted troubleshooting on fault isolation manual + fleet data) has clear, measurable value.
  - **Engineering disposition / approval** of a non-standard repair, plus sign-off, is the long pole in structural damage AOGs (ground-equipment strikes, bird strikes). The OEM must approve the repair scheme. Some OEMs quote turnaround targets of hours to a few days.
  - Sign-off for a simple swap is minutes. Compliance paperwork for parts (traceability, 8130-3) is a real hidden delay.
- Rough working estimate (my synthesis, NOT data): for a typical outstation AOG lasting hours to a day, diagnosis is about 10-20%, parts sourcing and delivery about 50-70%, repair labor about 10-20%, and inspection/release about 5-10%. For engine-driven groundings (the GTF case), parts and shop capacity are about 90%+ of the downtime, measured in months.

### 8. Where AI/data already sits in AOG [BACKGROUND]
- OEM predictive-maintenance platforms (Airbus Skywise, Boeing Insight Accelerator/AHM, GE/CFM engine health monitoring) have existed for about a decade. They are sold back to airlines, so the OEMs hold the fleet data. This is a data moat, not just a knowledge moat.
- That runs against a strong C3: the knowledge is not "gone". It is moving into OEM-controlled data platforms, and it is gated by maintenance-manual licensing (AMM, FIM, IPC, SRM are OEM copyright, licensed per tail).

## Reasoning against the founder's claims

- **C1 (universal chain)**: mixed. The stages exist, but aviation inserts a "defer under MEL" branch. Most faults get deferred rather than fixed immediately, and engineering approval and parts-paperwork stages fall outside the simple chain. The chain is also not linear: parts swapping is used as a diagnostic tool.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly)**: mixed. It holds in the form of IP/data rights and design-authority approval: F-35 tech data, OEM manual licensing, repair schemes that need OEM sign-off. But the biggest current downtime (700 aircraft parked for engines) is a capacity and hardware problem where knowledge is not the gap.
- **C3 (AI erases the gap)**: contradicts. The regulatory monopoly (design authority, PMA/DER approval, 8130-3 traceability) and contractual monopoly (data rights) are not cognitive, so AI does not remove them.
- **C4 (10x on the 4 middle steps)**: mixed. Diagnosis could improve a lot (NFF, faster troubleshooting), but it is a small share of AOG time. Sourcing and delivery are limited by physical inventory location and by the global supply of certified parts. Better search lowers broker search cost; it does not create a part at the outstation. A 10x drop in cost per AOG is implausible. A 10x drop in diagnostic labor is plausible.
- **C5 (AI parts factory)**: contradicts in aviation. Any part needs approved design data (PMA, STC, or OEM) plus a production approval and conformity. Making a replacement part on demand is a certification problem lasting months to years, not a manufacturing problem lasting hours. Possible exceptions are non-critical cabin parts, tooling, and military sustainment (DoD additive-manufacturing programs).
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve)**: supports. Release-to-service, RII, design-authority approval and parts traceability are the gates. AI-assisted inspection (drone/borescope image analysis) is an active area, but regulators still require a licensed human signature.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient)**: mixed. The redundancy (rotable pools, MEL, stationed FSRs) is a deliberate, safety-driven response to unpredictability. It is costly but rational. Aftermarket profit for OEMs (parts margins, data platforms) does create incentives to keep repair OEM-centric, which supports a weaker form of C7.
