## Findings

### F1. Google: repair is batched, cheap, and designed around software fault tolerance (hard data plus design doctrine)
Source: Barroso, Hoelzle, Ranganathan, "The Datacenter as a Computer", 3rd ed. (2019), ch. 7 "Dealing with Failures and Repairs". PDF: https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~shivaram/cs744-readings/dc-computer-v3.pdf
- "An efficient repair process is critical to the overall cost efficiency of WSCs. A machine in repair is effectively out of operation ... repair actions are costly in terms of both replacement parts and the skilled labor involved. Last, repair quality—how likely a repair action will actually fix a problem while accurately determining which (if any) component is at fault—affects both component expenses and average machine reliability."
- Because of the software fault-tolerance layer, "quickly responding to individual repair cases is not as critical ... a data center can implement a schedule that makes the most efficient use of a technician's time by making a daily sweep of all machines that need repairs attention. The philosophy is to increase the rate of repairs while keeping the repair latency within acceptable levels."
- Cost comparison: on-site IT service contracts with 24-hr response cost 5-15% of equipment value per year; 4-hr response roughly doubles that. Google's worked example: 1 hr per repair, 5% AFR, $100/hr tech, parts 10% of a $2,000 server gives $15 per server per year, i.e. 0.75% of capex per year. "Keeping large clusters healthy can be quite affordable." Book's TCO model elsewhere assumes repair and maintenance at 5% of capex per year.
- So the urgency premium (paying for fast response) is 7-30x the cost of batched repair. The lever was not cheaper knowledge but removing urgency through redundancy (N+2 costs 2% overhead at 100 replicas).
- Sparing: crash rate of mature servers is 1.2-2 crashes per year; for a 2,000-server service, ~10 machines/day crash; since 99% of restarts finish within 2 days, "as few as 20 spare machines" (1%) keep the service fully provisioned.
- Availability: ~55% of restarts under 3 min, 25% 3-30 min, ~1% over a day (those are going into repairs). Average machine availability 99.93%.
- Mean time to hardware repair in Google's fleet is more than six years, versus mean time to machine crash of six months or less: most crashes are software, not hardware.
- Memory and disk faults were the two most common diagnoses for servers sent to hardware repair at Google in 2018.
- Homogeneity: "homogeneity can allow more efficient supply chains and more efficient repair processes because automatic and manual repairs benefit from having more experience with fewer types of systems."
- Diagnosis is a data product: Google's System Health Infrastructure "constantly monitors every server for configuration, activity, environmental, and error data ... an automated machine failure diagnostics tool that uses machine learning methods to suggest the most appropriate repairs action." It also "helped pinpoint a specific batch of defective components across a large fleet."
- Prediction is economically hard: "a false component failure prediction incurs all of the overhead of the regular hardware repair process (parts, technician time, machine downtime, etc.)"; where software tolerates faults, "prediction models must have much greater accuracy to be economically competitive."

### F2. Google: SMART-based failure prediction mostly fails at the individual-unit level (hard data, contradicts "AI fixes diagnosis")
Source: Pinheiro, Weber, Barroso, "Failure Trends in a Large Disk Drive Population", FAST 2007. https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/disk_failures.pdf
- "Out of all failed drives, over 56% of them have no count in any of the four strong SMART signals"; even adding all signals, "over 36% of all failed drives had zero counts on all variables."
- Conclusion: SMART models are "unlikely" to predict individual drive failures accurately, but are useful "in reasoning about the aggregate reliability of large disk populations ... for logistics and supply-chain planning."
- Implication: telemetry plus statistics improves spares provisioning (the sourcing step) more than it improves per-unit diagnosis. The signal has to exist before any model can use it.

### F3. Disk field replacement rates far exceed datasheets; 43% of returned drives are No Trouble Found (hard data)
Source: Schroeder and Gibson, "Disk failures in the real world: What does an MTTF of 1,000,000 hours mean to you?", FAST 2007. https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/fast07/tech/schroeder/schroeder_html/index.html
- Datasheet MTTF implies AFR <= 0.88%; field annual replacement rates were typically 2-4% and up to 13.5%; weighted average ~3.4x datasheet; up to ~15x; for 5-8 year old drives ~30x.
- "One disk vendor reported that for 43% of drives returned by customers, it found no problem with the disk."
- Wear-out begins earlier than vendor models assume; replacement is not exponential (Weibull fits better).
- Implication for C2/C4: the OEM's spec sheet is itself wrong knowledge; the knowledge that mattered came from the operator's fleet data, not the OEM. And diagnosis error (NTF) is a large waste line: nearly half of RMAs ship working parts back and forth.

### F4. Open Compute's fault-management work exists explicitly to cut No Trouble Found RMAs and standardise error reporting (practitioner/standards)
Source: OCP Hardware Fault Management Sub-Project. https://www.opencompute.org/projects/hardware-fault-management
- Objectives: "minimize performance degradation, reduce No Trouble Found (NTF) returns in RMA, and scale the solution across heterogeneous fleets."
- Activities: standardise hardware error reporting formats and error classification; GPU/accelerator RAS requirements; Fleet Memory Fault Management (FMFM) framework; white paper on data poisoning for uncorrected errors.
- Implication: the hyperscaler fix to the knowledge problem was to force vendors to expose standardised, machine-readable fault data (an interface/standards fix), not to "know more" on the operator side. The diagnose step depends on what the hardware is designed to report.

### F5. Even Google lacks the chip maker's diagnostic access: "Cores that don't count" (hard data plus practitioner view; supports C2 in a twist, contradicts C3)
Source: Hochschild et al. (Google), "Cores that don't count", HotOS 2021. https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s01-hochschild.pdf
- Rate: "on the order of a few mercurial cores per several thousand machines - similar to the rate reported by Facebook." "The problem is serious enough for us to have applied many engineer-decades to it."
- Knowledge asymmetry: "because we have limited knowledge of the detailed underlying hardware, and no access to the hardware-supported test structures available to chip makers, we cannot infer much about root causes." Conversely, "Chip makers do not have easy access to diverse large-scale workloads ... Without such a feedback loop, their testing is at best 'self consistent'." The knowledge gap runs both ways.
- Human triage still matters: "roughly half of these human-identified suspects are actually proven, on deeper investigation, to be mercurial cores ... The other half is a mix of false accusations and limited reproducibility." Novel failure modes "will have to be root-caused by humans."
- Fleet-wide testing "has expanded to new classes of CEEs as we and our CPU vendors discover them, still a few times per year."
- Remedy is quarantine (don't schedule on the bad core) rather than repair: an RPC service lets applications report suspect cores; clustering of reports leads to quarantine and deeper checking.
- Cause: smaller feature sizes and design complexity: "The problem will continually get harder."

### F6. Meta: silent data corruption is now ~1 per 1,000 devices in AI silicon; detection is continuous and in-production (hard data)
Source: Meta Engineering, "How Meta keeps its AI hardware reliable", July 2025. https://engineering.fb.com/2025/07/22/data-infrastructure/how-meta-keeps-its-ai-hardware-reliable/
- Historically soft-error bitflips ~1 per million devices; with denser accelerator silicon, SDCs ~1 per thousand devices.
- Over 66% of Llama 3 training interruptions came from hardware failures (SRAM, HBM, processing grids, network switches).
- Tools: Fleetscanner (targeted micro-benchmarks during maintenance windows; full fleet coverage every 45-60 days), Ripple (co-located with production; fleet coverage in days), Hardware Sentinel (analyses kernel-space application exceptions, no test allocation; outperforms test-based methods by 41%).
- Training remediation: "reductive triage" (binary-search with mini training runs on shrinking clusters, quarantine, resume from checkpoint), hyper-checkpointing, shadow-node re-execution.
- Gradient corruption "can go unnoticed for weeks or months." Timeline: work started 2016, SDC detection scaled 2018, frameworks in place 2019, AI SDC detection in production 2024; 2023 cross-industry effort with Google, Microsoft, ARM, AMD, NVIDIA, Intel.
- Also: Dixit et al. (Meta), "Detecting silent data corruptions in the wild", arXiv 2203.08989 (3+ years of production experience comparing Fleetscanner and Ripple). https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08989

### F7. Meta Llama 3: 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days; 78% hardware; only 3 needed significant manual intervention (hard data)
Source: Llama Team, "The Llama 3 Herd of Models", arXiv 2407.21783 (2024), section 3.3.4 and Table 5. https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783
- 16K H100 GPUs. 54-day snapshot: 466 job interruptions, 47 planned (automated firmware/kernel maintenance), 419 unexpected. ~78% confirmed or suspected hardware. GPU issues 58.7% of unexpected.
- Table 5: faulty GPU 148 (30.1%), GPU HBM3 72 (17.2%), software bug 54 (12.9%), network switch/cable 35 (8.4%), host maintenance 32 (7.6%), GPU SRAM 19 (4.5%), GPU system processor 17 (4.1%), NIC 7, NCCL watchdog timeouts 7, SDC 6, thermal interface 6, SSD 3, PSU 3, chassis 2, IO board 2, dependency 2, CPU 2, system memory 2.
- "Despite the large number of failures, significant manual intervention was required only three times during this period, with the rest of issues handled by automation." >90% effective training time.
- Diagnosis tools: PyTorch NCCL flight recorder; NCCLX timeouts and state snapshots; straggler tools ("By investigating just a few top suspects, we were usually able to effectively identify the stragglers").
- Note: "handled by automation" means detect, isolate, swap in spare capacity and restart from checkpoint. The physical repair of the failed GPU/HBM still happens later, off the critical path.

### F8. Meta research clusters: MTTF collapses with scale; lemon-node removal is a major lever (hard data)
Source: Kokolis et al. (Meta), "Revisiting Reliability in Large-Scale Machine Learning Research Clusters", arXiv 2410.21680 (v2 Feb 2025). https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21680v2
- 11 months, ~4M jobs, 150M+ A100 GPU-hours, two clusters (RSC-1, RSC-2).
- Failure rate 6.50 (RSC-1) and 2.34 (RSC-2) per thousand node-days; RSC-1 spiked from ~2.5 to ~17.5.
- MTTF: 8-GPU jobs 47.7 days; 1,024-GPU jobs 7.9 hours; projected 16,384 GPUs 1.8 hours; 131,072 GPUs 0.23 hours (14 minutes).
- Health checks every five minutes per node; high-severity failures remove node immediately and reschedule; lower severity drains after current job; node stays out until fixed and all checks pass.
- Lemon nodes: 40 nodes (1.2% of RSC-1, 1.7% of RSC-2) were in 13% of daily jobs; removing them cut large-job (512+ GPU) failure rate from 14% to 4%. Root causes: GPU 28.2%, DIMM 20.5%, PCIe 15.4%.
- Hardware failures hit under 1% of jobs but ~19% of GPU runtime.
- Implication: at scale, the value is in the notice/isolate step (minutes), and in identifying repeat offenders; repair itself is decoupled.

### F9. ByteDance ByteRobust: 97% effective training time on 9,600 GPUs over 3 months via routine automated detect/demarcate/recover (hard data, abstract-level)
Source: "Robust LLM Training Infrastructure at ByteDance", arXiv 2509.16293 (v4 Oct 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16293
- Treats failure as routine: "detecting and recovering failures in a routine manner", fault demarcation and localization via data-driven approach. 97% ETTR.
- Shows the playbook generalised across hyperscalers (Meta, Google, ByteDance) by 2025.

### F10. Meta maintenance orchestration: ~1 million operations per day, buffer-managed (practitioner, hard numbers)
Source: Meta Engineering, "Maintaining large-scale AI capacity at Meta", June 2024. https://engineering.fb.com/2024/06/12/production-engineering/maintaining-large-scale-ai-capacity-meta/
- "Maintenance trains" cycle small maintenance domains out of production; all capacity except one domain stays live.
- OpsPlanner orchestrates disruptive work, owns planned-maintenance and failure buffers, ~1 million operations/day; 30+ maintenance operations, ~50 components updated, 3 host verification tasks, thousands of disruptive AI host tasks daily.
- Safeguards: autostop when buffers exhausted; auto-offboarding of failing upgrades.
- Implication: return-to-service (post-repair verification) is automated and gated: a host does not return until upgrades and checks are complete. That is the hyperscaler version of C6's inspection step: inspection became software, not a human sign-off.

### F11. Circular sparing: hyperscalers became their own parts suppliers (hard data)
Source: Google 2024 Environmental Report. https://www.gstatic.com/gumdrop/sustainability/google-2024-environmental-report.pdf
- "As of the end of 2023, 29% of components used for server deployment, maintenance, and upgrades were refurbished inventory." Since 2015, 44M+ hardware components resold into secondary market, 7M+ in 2023.
- Microsoft runs "Circular Centers" with similar reuse goals (90% reuse/recycle of servers and components by 2025 target, per Microsoft announcements; primary URL not retrieved in this run, so treat as unverified).
- Implication: hyperscalers solved "sourcing parts" largely by owning a homogeneous fleet and harvesting decommissioned machines, not by fabricating bespoke parts. This is a scale/standardisation solution.

### F12. Microsoft Project Natick: no-repair design; 1/8 the failure rate of land (hard data, small sample)
Source: Microsoft, "Microsoft finds underwater datacenters are reliable, practical and use energy sustainably", 2020. https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/sustainability/project-natick-underwater-datacenter/
- "Our failure rate in the water is one-eighth of what we see on land." Concept: no on-site repair; failed servers simply taken offline; fleet replaced every ~5 years. Hypothesised causes: nitrogen atmosphere and no people to "bump and jostle" components.
- Implication: humans in the repair loop cause failures. The extreme hyperscaler answer to repair inefficiency is "don't repair; overprovision and fail in place." Strongly contradicts C1's universal chain (diagnose/source/repair steps disappear entirely).

### F13. Backblaze (independent cloud storage operator): 1.57% AFR across ~300k drives; 53,337 drives installed by techs in 2024 (hard data)
Source: Backblaze Drive Stats for 2024. https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-2024/
- 298,954 drives in annual table; AFR 1.57% (2024) vs 1.70% (2023). 305,180 drives monitored at year end.
- Backblaze publishes per-model failure data publicly since 2013: an example of breaking the vendor's reliability-knowledge monopoly via open data, making buyers better at sourcing. Shows the knowledge was obtainable without AI once someone aggregated the fleet data.

### F14. OCP / Meta hardware design: integration and modularity for serviceability (practitioner, weak numbers)
Sources: https://engineering.fb.com/2024/03/12/data-center-engineering/building-metas-genai-infrastructure/ ; https://engineering.fb.com/2024/10/15/data-infrastructure/metas-open-ai-hardware-vision/
- Grand Teton integrates power, control, compute, fabric into one chassis, "simplifies system deployment"; Catalina (GB200, ORv3, up to 140 kW, liquid cooled) modular rack. Meta notes: "Identifying a problematic GPU that is stalling an entire training job becomes very difficult at a large scale."
- No hard repair-time numbers found in these posts. Trend worth noting (from general knowledge, not verified here): rack-scale GPU systems (NVL72) shift the field-replaceable unit up to trays/racks and liquid-cooled assemblies, which makes physical repair harder and more vendor-dependent, not easier.

## Synthesis: how hyperscalers made repair efficient

1. Decouple repair from service. Redundancy (N+2 at ~2% overhead; ~1% spares) turns every failure from urgent to batched. Urgent on-site service costs 5-15%/yr of equipment value (x2 for 4-hr); batched in-house repair ~0.75%/yr. This, not knowledge, is the biggest cost lever.
2. Make "notice" continuous and automatic. 5-minute health checks; in-production SDC screening; flight recorders. Notice is cheap and fast; the remaining problem is subtle failures (SDC, stragglers, lemons).
3. Isolate, don't diagnose, on the critical path. Quarantine the node/core, restart from checkpoint, diagnose later. Binary-search triage. Only 3 manual interventions out of 419 failures at Meta.
4. Standardise and homogenise. Few SKUs, OCP specs, standard error formats; this makes diagnosis learnable and spares poolable.
5. Own the data, push vendors. Fleet data contradicted vendor datasheets (3-30x); OCP pushes vendors to expose fault data and reduce NTF (43% NTF on drives).
6. Be your own parts supplier. 29% of Google's maintenance components are refurbished from its own fleet.
7. Inspection as software gate. Hosts return only after automated verification.
8. Limits: individual failure prediction is weak (36-56% of failed disks give no SMART signal); novel failure modes need humans; chip-internal knowledge stays with chip makers; failures get harder as silicon shrinks (SDC 1e-6 to 1e-3 per device).

## What generalises vs what does not
- Generalises to fleets with many identical units and software-defined redundancy: wind turbines, EV/robotaxi fleets, battery storage, telecom, robot fleets, airline fleets (partially).
- Does not generalise easily to one-off assets (a single plant, a single washing machine owner): no redundancy to batch against, no fleet data to learn from. The hyperscaler trick is scale-dependent.
- Key point for the founder: hyperscalers did not "close the knowledge gap" by knowing more; they built the instrumentation, standard interfaces and redundancy that made precise knowledge unnecessary on the critical path. For the founder's thesis, the transferable product is probably the fleet-level telemetry, isolation and repair-orchestration layer (a "Borg/OpsPlanner for physical assets"), plus standards that force OEMs to expose fault data.

## Stances
- C1 (universal chain): mixed. The steps exist, but hyperscalers re-order and decouple them (notice to isolate to return to service in minutes using spares; diagnose/repair later in batch) or delete repair entirely (Natick fail-in-place, decommission and harvest).
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. Real two-way asymmetry (Google lacks chip test structures; vendors lack fleet workloads; vendor datasheets 3-30x off; 43% NTF). But the dominant hyperscaler savings came from redundancy, batching and automation, not knowledge.
- C3 (AI closes gap): contradicts. ML diagnosis has been used at Google since ~2007 yet individual prediction remains weak; novel failures still need humans; SDCs are getting more frequent; the needed signals are often not exposed by hardware.
- C4 (10x drop in four steps): mixed. Hyperscalers did achieve roughly 10x-plus cost reductions vs OEM service contracts (0.75% vs 5-15%/yr), but via scale, redundancy and batching; physical parts logistics was solved by owning spares, not by AI.
- C5 (AI parts factory): contradicts/silent. No hyperscaler fabricates bespoke replacement parts; components (GPUs, HBM, DIMMs) cannot be fabricated outside fabs. Their answer is standardisation and harvested spares.
- C6 (inspection essential and evolving): supports. Return-to-service gating by automated health checks and SDC screening is central, and evolving (Fleetscanner, Ripple, Hardware Sentinel); inspection became continuous rather than one-off.
- C7 (structurally inefficient, needs rebuild): mixed. Hyperscalers rebuilt their own repair stack and show conventional OEM service is 7-30x more expensive, which supports the claim for the vendor-service model; but the rebuild was enabled by redesigning the asset and the system around failure, which is a design choice, not just a repair-industry fix.
