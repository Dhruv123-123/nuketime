## Findings

### 1. Wartime Britain: the Civilian Repair Organisation (CRO) put about 79,000 aircraft back into service, 1940-45 [data]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Civilian Repair Organisation", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilian_Repair_Organisation
- 79,000 aircraft returned to service, May 1940 to July 1945. 36.5% (3,285) of the RAF's heavy aircraft then flying had passed through the CRO.
- About 100 participating firms. Most were existing aircraft makers, plus engineering and woodworking firms on contracts. Lord Nuffield ran it from Morris Motors at Cowley, with the No.1 Civilian Repair Unit set up on 11 Sep 1939.
- The logistics were a system of their own. No.50 Maintenance Unit moved wrecks and parts to repair sites. No.1 Metal and Produce Recovery Depot reclaimed materials. Airspares Ltd stripped crashed aircraft for reusable parts. Minor jobs were done "Fly In": the aircraft flew in one day and the same pilot flew it out the next.
- Reading: in a war emergency, the scarce things were organisation, logistics, the right to authorise (Air Ministry and later Ministry of Aircraft Production supervision) and the capacity of skilled firms. The answer was a state-run network with recovery of parts and materials built in, not more knowledge. Each repaired aircraft was still test-flown before it went back to its unit, so inspection stayed in the chain (C6).

### 2. Wartime Britain: "Make Do and Mend" was a state programme to spread repair knowledge [history]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Make Do and Mend", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Make_Do_and_Mend
- Board of Trade paper "Extension of the Life of Clothing" (2 Jul 1941). Clothes rationing started in 1941. The scheme launched in 1942 with the Women's Institute, the WVS and others. First booklet in 1943, a Ministry of Information newsreel the same year, the mascot "Mrs Sew and Sew", evening classes, and clothing exchanges.
- Reading: when materials were rationed, the state treated repair knowledge as something it could spread cheaply through pamphlets and classes. This is a historical precedent for C2/C3: knowledge is the cheap lever and spreads fast. But this was a low-complexity area (textiles) with no certification step.

### 3. Cuba: about 60,000 pre-1960 US cars kept running for 60 years without OEM parts [data, dated]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Yank tank", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yank_tank (citing Schweid 2004; the page is flagged as outdated)
- About 60,000 classic American cars still in use, out of roughly 173,000 cars in Cuba. The 1962 embargo ended new OEM parts.
- How they were kept going: household products and Soviet parts adapted in. Engine swaps, mostly to Soviet diesels, especially in almendrón taxis. In 2002 gasoline cost about $0.75/L against $0.15-0.20/L for diesel. Cars that could not be fixed were "parked" or "parted out", which made them a parts pool for the rest.
- Average distance driven per car fell from nearly 3,000 km/yr (mid-1980s) to under 800 km/yr (2000-01). Usage was rationed, not only parts.
- Legal limits: only pre-revolution cars could be privately traded, and a sale needed a *traspaso* document. No private dealerships for European or Asian cars.
- Reading: Cuban mechanics did not lack knowledge. They had a great deal of it, spread across many people. What bound them was parts, materials, fuel and law. Their answer was substitution: engine swaps, cannibalisation, and making or adapting parts. The result was a car that runs but is no longer the original design: worse fuel economy, worse safety, and wear from overloading.

### 4. Cuba's Special Period: the binding limit was physical inputs, not know-how [data]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Special Period", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Period
- GDP fell 35% to 1995. Imports and exports fell more than 80%. Oil imports fell to about 10% of pre-1990 levels. Manufacturing capacity fell 90%, construction 75%, agriculture 47%.
- Tractors and harvesters were "largely paralyzed without petroleum". Blackouts lasted up to 16 h. Bus waits ran up to 3 h.
- Adaptations: about 1.2 million bicycles bought from China plus about 500,000 made in Cuba. Trucks converted into "camel" buses (reportedly up to 300 passengers). Shared taxis carrying 6-8 people. Horse carts licensed as taxis.
- Reading: a whole country of skilled improvisers still could not repair its way out of missing fuel and parts. Know-how was plentiful. Energy and materials were the hard ceiling. This is direct evidence against C4: cheap knowledge does not create inputs.

### 5. Cuba: Oroza's "technological disobedience" [practitioner and design-research view]
- Sources: Walker Art Center magazine on Ernesto Oroza, https://walkerart.org/magazine/ernesto-oroza-technological-disobedience-cuba ; Wikipedia "Ernesto Oroza", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernesto_Oroza (book *RIKIMBILI*, 2009; *Objets Réinventés*, 2002)
- Examples: a Soviet Orbit fan repaired with telephone parts and an LP record as blades (2007). A guillotine mousetrap made from a beer can (1991). A charger for non-rechargeable batteries (2012). Rubble dust mixed with cement as building material.
- Oroza's idea of the "object of necessity": once people stop treating the brand and the manufacturer's design as binding, the object becomes a kit of parts.
- [BACKGROUND, unverified this session] In 1991 the Cuban armed forces published *Con nuestros propios esfuerzos* ("With our own efforts"), a state manual of improvised fixes for household and industrial items. It shows the state spreading repair knowledge as a commons, much like Make Do and Mend.
- Reading: on the knowledge side, the "monopoly" was broken by attitude and by sharing between peers, not by technology. The trade-off is that the repaired object is a different, lower-spec object (a function-preserving substitution).

### 6. Soviet Union: chronic shortage made households do their own repairs [study, partly BACKGROUND]
- Kornai's shortage economy. Source: Wikipedia, "Shortage economy", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortage_economy . Under chronic shortage buyers face "forced substitution" between imperfect substitutes, queues of hours a day, and waiting lists (10-15 years for apartments in the 1980s USSR). Shortage is systemic, not a planning mistake. Buyers stockpile goods to trade later.
- [BACKGROUND, unverified this session] Gerasimova & Chuikina, "The Repair Society", Russian Studies in History 48(1), 2009. They argue Soviet everyday life was a "repair society": state repair services ("byt" services) were scarce, slow and poor, so people repaired at home, kept stocks of parts and junk "just in case", and traded favours (blat). Knowledge sat with households, especially men in garages. The magazine *Sdelai Sam* (Do It Yourself) and *Za Rulem* repair columns spread know-how.
- Lada/Zhiguli. Source: Wikipedia, "Lada Riva", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lada_Riva . Fiat 124-derived, simple mechanics, a 21-piece toolkit as standard. About 3 million VAZ-2105s and over 14 million Fiat 124 derivatives built at Togliatti over about 41 years. Still "the most popular second-hand car in Russia". Reading: a design simple enough for owners to fix, plus one platform kept for decades (parts interchangeable at huge volume), did more for repairability than any knowledge product. Parts commonality across a large fleet is what makes a parts market liquid.

### 7. Russia after 2022: aviation under sanctions shows parts, not knowledge, are what bind; certification breaks first [data/news]
- Sources: Wikipedia, "International sanctions during the Russian invasion of Ukraine", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_sanctions_during_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine ; Wikipedia, "Aeroflot", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroflot
- About 515 leased aircraft (about $10B) moved onto the Russian registry. Airbus and Boeing stopped support on 2 Mar 2022. Bermuda suspended airworthiness approvals on 14 Mar 2022. Russian carriers went onto the EU banned-carrier list because they had lost foreign airworthiness certificates.
- Jun 2022: the government advised airlines to use some aircraft as parts donors. Reuters (Aug 2022): A320s, A350s, a 737 and a Superjet stripped for parts, with about 15% of Aeroflot's fleet apparently grounded.
- From Apr 2023 Aeroflot sent aircraft to Iran for maintenance. Reports (2023) of crews told not to log faults that would ground an aircraft, and of flights with disabled brakes.
- June 2025 Finnish study: about 4,000 shipments of Boeing and Airbus parts (about €1B) still reaching Russia through the UAE, Turkey, China and Gabon.
- Import substitution is slow: MC-21 deliveries to Aeroflot are now expected from 2027.
- Reading: Russia has deep aerospace engineering knowledge, so this is no knowledge gap. What binds is certified parts and legal airworthiness. Workarounds were cannibalisation, grey imports and lower standards. Safety oversight is the first casualty when parts run short. This supports C6 strongly. Against C3/C4: with knowledge in hand, the sourcing and delivery steps took years and a black market.

### 8. Iran: decades of aviation sanctions [data]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Iran Air", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran_Air
- 2014: Iran's deputy minister said more than 100 Iranian aircraft were grounded for "lack of access to new parts and technical expertise". Average fleet age was about 24.1 years (Mar 2017, citation-needed). Aircraft "had to be parted out". The safety record fell sharply.
- Six second-hand A300s bought in 2001 were grounded within two years over engine problems.
- After 2016 sanctions relief, Iran Air placed big orders, but only 3 Airbus and 16 ATR aircraft were delivered. The US revoked licences in 2018.
- Reading: the official reason names both parts and expertise. The expertise in question is OEM data, engineering support and authorisations, which is closer to licensed knowledge than to general know-how. That partly supports C2. In aviation the "knowledge monopoly" is enforced through law and certification (type certificate data, approved parts), not just through who knows what.

### 9. Jugaad (India/Pakistan): the safety cost of uncertified improvisation [study/regulator]
- Source: Wikipedia, "Jugaad", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jugaad (cites Thomas Birtchnell's Aligarh hospital study)
- Improvised vehicles built from salvaged parts, such as irrigation-pump diesels on wooden-plank chassis. Poor brakes, top speed about 60 km/h, often more than 20 riders.
- In the Aligarh study, 13.88% of pedestrian road-casualty cases out of 2,139 road casualties (72 h) were linked to jugaad. The vehicles are not legal under the Motor Vehicles Act 1988 and are subject to bans and seizures (Jaipur, 2010).
- Reading: when people improvise or make parts without inspection, risk is shifted onto third parties. This contradicts the easy version of C5 (making bespoke parts is solved) and supports C6.

### 10. [BACKGROUND, unverified this session] Other scarcity analogues to check
- Cuba allowed private car imports in 2014, with dealer prices that made them unaffordable (reports of a Peugeot 508 at about $262,000). Under state rules, demand for scarce goods can stay unmet even when supply is legally allowed.
- WWII Lend-Lease and US "field expedient" repair in the Pacific; the Liberty ship programme's standardisation. These show that standardisation made field repair possible.
- North Korean and Venezuelan grounded fleets, and Venezuelan oil-industry cannibalisation after 2017: further cases where parts, not know-how, were the binding limit.
- East Germany (GDR): Trabant owners waited 10-15 years for a car, while DIY repair and *Bastler* culture flourished.

## Synthesis: knowledge vs parts in scarcity economies

1. Every scarcity case studied shows a huge spread of knowledge: Cuban mechanics, Soviet garage men, British WI classes, Russian aerospace engineers. Knowledge became a commons through peer networks, state pamphlets (Make Do and Mend, *Con nuestros propios esfuerzos*), magazines (*Sdelai Sam*) and simple, long-lived platforms (Zhiguli). Yet the shortages stayed. The binding limits were **physical parts and materials, energy, and legal or certification authority**.
2. When parts are missing, the common workarounds are cannibalisation (a car "parted out", aircraft used as parts donors), substitution (Soviet diesels in US cars, LP-record fan blades), grey imports (Russia, about €1B), and lower standards (unlogged faults, jugaad vehicles). Each keeps the item running by eating into safety, efficiency or legality.
3. Made-to-fit local parts do happen at scale (Cuba), but only where no certification applies. In certified fields (aviation), self-made parts end in groundings, lost airworthiness and bans. The bottleneck is approving the part, not making it.
4. Organisation and logistics were the lever that scaled in wartime Britain. The CRO's 79,000 aircraft came from a state network of about 100 firms, salvage depots, transport units and test flights. It cut lead times by running a system end to end, not just by knowing more.
5. The chain the founder describes (C1) is visible in these cases, but scarcity systems reorder it. Scarce parts lead to rationed use and to cannibalising one asset to keep others running. "Parking" an asset to wait for parts becomes a normal state, not a failure. Inspection is the step most likely to be quietly skipped.

## Implications for a company
- Under scarcity, the lasting value sits in: (a) liquid, cross-referenced parts supply (what fits what across platforms); (b) authorising non-OEM or recovered parts (PMA/DER-type routes, certified reverse engineering); (c) certified parts recovery and teardown (the modern Airspares Ltd / MPRD); (d) inspection and test that makes improvised or substituted repairs safe to sign off.
- AI diagnosis or knowledge alone would mostly have been redundant in Cuba or the USSR: their people already knew what to do. It could matter more where knowledge is locked up legally (OEM data in aviation), but there the lock is legal, not cognitive.
