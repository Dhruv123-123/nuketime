## Verified findings (fetched this session)

### 1. Regulatory lineage: PMA is a 1947-1965 creation, and enforcement in the 1990s turned it into an industry
Source: Wikipedia, "Parts Manufacturer Approval" (cites the Federal Register directly), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval
- 1947: CAR 3.31 let the Administrator approve replacement parts (12 Fed. Reg. 7898).
- 1952: authority moved to the ".18" sections (effective Mar 5, 1952).
- 1955/56: parts authority separated into general rule CAR 1.55 (adopted Apr 13, 1956).
- Feb 1, 1965: CAR 1.55 became FAR 21.303, with a Fabrication Inspection System requirement.
- 1972 (Amdt 21-38): PMA-specific data submission requirements.
- 1980s: the aftermarket grew as airlines looked for cheaper spares, **but many makers did not get PMAs**.
- 1995: FAA "Enhanced Enforcement" (60 Fed. Reg. 10480) pushed unapproved makers to apply for PMAs.
- 2009 rule (74 FR 53368, effective Apr 16, 2011): PMA holders must meet the same production quality system as OEMs (21.137).
Reading: the legal channel for non-OEM parts has existed for about 60-75 years. The industry did not take off when the rule was written. It grew once enforcement and quality-system rules made PMA parts trusted enough for airlines to use. The constraint was certification and trust, not knowledge.

### 2. What a PMA applicant has to prove (14 CFR 21.303)
Source: Cornell LII, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/21.303
- There are three routes: (1) test reports and computations showing the design meets airworthiness requirements for the specific product it goes on; (2) identicality to a type-certificated design; (3) a licence from the design holder. The applicant must also run an approved quality system.
- Reading: reverse-engineering the part (the "knowledge" step) is necessary but not enough. The costly part is showing compliance per product: testing, analysis, FAA/DER review, and a production quality system. This is the stage AI does not remove.

### 3. HEICO, the largest PMA maker, about doubled its approved catalogue in 7 years
Sources: HEICO 10-K FY2018, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/000004661918000117/hei1031201810k.htm ; HEICO 10-K FY2025, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/000004661925000082/hei-20251031.htm
- FY2018: "approximately 11,000 parts for which PMAs have been received from the FAA"; adding "approximately 300 to 500" PMAs a year.
- FY2025: "approximately 20,000 parts"; adding "approximately 400 to 550" PMAs a year.
- Even the leader adds only about 400-550 new approvals a year. Engineering and certification throughput is the rate limiter.

### 4. HEICO Flight Support Group scale and margins (FY2025)
Source: HEICO 10-K FY2025 (same URL)
- FSG: net sales $3,117.3M, operating income $750.4M, operating margin 24.1% (22.5% in FY2024). FSG was 70% of HEICO net sales in FY2025 (68% FY2024, 60% FY2023). Total HEICO net sales were $4,485.0M.
- Note: FSG revenue covers more than PMA (it also includes repair/overhaul and distribution), so FSG sales overstate PMA sales.

### 5. HEICO's own framing: the OEMs have held near-total control of engine spares
Source: HEICO 10-K FY2025
- The three main jet engine OEMs "have been the sole source of substantially all jet engine replacement parts for their jet engines."
- Success depends on the ability to "re-design sophisticated products of OEMs in order to sell competing replacement parts at substantially lower prices."
- HEICO believes it is "the largest independent supplier of non-OEM jet engine and aircraft component replacement parts."
- Competition is "principally based on price and service to the extent that our parts are interchangeable."
- FY2018 10-K: "the PMA process creates a significant barrier to entry in this market niche". HEICO calls certification a moat. It has "no material patents" and relies "primarily on trade secret protection". So the knowledge is proprietary on both sides.
- Risk factor: "we may not be able to re-design and manufacture replacement parts that perform as well as those offered by OEMs."

### 6. An airline (MRO arm) took equity in a PMA maker, a structural airline counter-move
Source: HEICO 10-K FY2025
- "the 20% noncontrolling interest held by Lufthansa Technik AG in HEICO Aerospace Holdings Corp." (80% HEICO-owned). [UNVERIFIED-memory: the stake dates from 1997, and the deal included Lufthansa committing to buy and help approve parts.]
- Reading: the airline side backed an alternative supplier with capital and engineering acceptance. That says the barrier was the buyer's willingness to take engineering risk, not only information.

### 7. The OEM business model: sell engines below cost, recover on spares
Source: Wikipedia, "Aircraft maintenance" and "Power by the Hour" (citing industry forecasts), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_maintenance , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_by_the_Hour
- Engine makers "deeply discount their sales, up to 90%, to win the multi-year stream of spares and services". Life-limited part (LLP) prices then rise to recover the discount. This is a razor-and-blades model.
- Power-by-the-hour started with Bristol Siddeley in 1962 and was revived by Rolls-Royce in the 1980s. GE and Pratt & Whitney have equivalents. Under these flight-hour agreements the OEM controls which parts go in, so PMA is effectively locked out of engines covered by them.
- Engines were 31% of the $70B airlines spent on MRO in 2017.

### 8. MRO market size and current stress
Source: Oliver Wyman Global Fleet & MRO Market Forecast 2024-2034, https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2024/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2024-2034.html
- MRO was about $104B in 2024, projected at $124B by 2034. GTF, LEAP and Trent XWB durability problems and supply-chain shortages are straining capacity. This is the setting for the 2022-2026 rise in PMA demand.

### 9. Counterfeit and unapproved parts: why certification and traceability stay central
Source: Wikipedia, "Bogus aircraft parts", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogus_aircraft_parts
- AOG Technics (UK) supplied an estimated 60,000 CFM56 parts with forged documents (2019-2023). Its director was sentenced in Feb 2026 to 56 months.
- An FAA study of May 1973 to Apr 1996 counted 174 accidents and incidents and 17 fatalities linked to unapproved parts.
- Reading: in aviation the scarce thing is a trusted pedigree and paperwork, not parts geometry. Faster or AI-generated sourcing makes fraud easier, so it raises the value of provenance and inspection.

## Background knowledge, NOT verified this session [UNVERIFIED-memory]
- Market share: PMA is commonly put at roughly 2-5% of the commercial aviation spare-parts market. Older estimates (around 2010-2015) put PMA at about $1-2B a year against a parts aftermarket of $40B or more. Growth estimates run about 6-10% a year. Engine PMA penetration is lower than airframe/component PMA because of OEM service agreements and LLP sensitivity.
- Airline adoption: large US carriers (Delta TechOps, United, American, Southwest) and Lufthansa are heavy users. Many Asian and Middle East operators and most lessors have been more cautious. Airline surveys (Aviation Week/MRO, AeroDynamic Advisory, ICF) show majorities saying they would use more PMA. Actual spend share stays low.
- OEM countermeasures: (a) flight-hour agreements (TotalCare, GE TrueChoice, P&W EngineWise) that exclude PMA; (b) warranty and guarantee clauses voided or diluted when PMA is installed; (c) lessor return conditions that ban or discount PMA in engines and LLPs, which makes PMA a residual-value risk; (d) restricted access to instructions for continued airworthiness (ICA) and component maintenance manuals, and restrictive licensing of repair data; (e) bundling parts with service and data analytics; (f) IP litigation. [Lead to verify: a late-1990s Pratt & Whitney suit against HEICO, possibly settled around 2001.] (g) selective price cuts on parts targeted by PMA; (h) OEMs buying aftermarket capacity.
- Counter-countermeasures: MARPA (the PMA trade association) lobbying, IATA "Guidance Material and Best Practices for Alternate Parts (PMA) and Approved (non-OEM) Repairs (DER)" (cited in the Wikipedia PMA article). IATA also complained to EU regulators about OEM aftermarket practices (around 2019-2022).
- Recent tailwind: during the 2022-2025 supply crunch and the GTF/LEAP problems, OEM parts lead times and price escalation (often 5-10% or more a year in list prices) pushed airlines toward PMA and used serviceable material. HEICO FSG organic growth was in the high teens in some quarters.

## Reasoning against the hypotheses
- C2 (knowledge monopoly) is **partly supported**. The OEMs really did hold "substantially all" engine spares, and HEICO's model is to re-design OEM parts. But the lock is not only knowledge. It is also contracts (flight-hour agreements, warranties, lease return conditions), certification of each part number for each product, and buyer risk aversion. PMA knowledge existed legally from 1956/1965, yet penetration stayed in the single digits for decades.
- C3 (AI closes the gap) is **contradicted for aviation**. Reverse-engineering geometry was never the main barrier. The costly steps are compliance testing, the quality system, FAA acceptance and contractual acceptance. HEICO, with decades of reverse-engineering skill, still adds only about 500 approvals a year.
- C4 (10x cost drop on the four steps) is **mixed or contradicted**. The PMA discount versus OEM is meaningful (often quoted at 30-50% [UNVERIFIED]), but it is a percentage cut, not 10x. Certification overhead stays.
- C5 (bespoke parts on demand) is **contradicted** for safety-critical aviation. A part with no PMA, licence or approved repair cannot legally be installed, however well it is made. Obsolescence is handled through PMA, DER repairs and USM, all slow and certification-bound.
- C6 (inspection/certification essential) is **strongly supported**. The 2009 quality-system rule, the AOG Technics fraud and HEICO calling PMA "a significant barrier to entry" all point the same way.
- C7 (built to be inefficient) is **partly supported**. The razor-and-blades model (engines discounted up to 90%) is a deliberate economic structure that makes money from aftermarket lock-in. Some of the "inefficiency" is intended rent extraction, and some is a rational price of safety.
- C1 (the universal chain) is **mixed**. PMA history adds stages the chain leaves out: design approval or certification of the part itself (before sourcing) and contractual or lessor acceptance (before installation).

## Company-shape implications
- "Certification throughput" tooling: AI that speeds up PMA/DER data packages (test-plan generation, similarity analysis, compliance documentation) for PMA makers and DERs. This attacks the real bottleneck of about 500 approvals a year even at the leader.
- Provenance and traceability for parts (anti-AOG-Technics): digital back-to-birth records, document-forgery detection.
- Data or analytics that let lessors and financiers price PMA and repair content into residual value. This weakens one of the main OEM countermeasures.
- Being an AI-native PMA house is possible, but it competes with HEICO on certification capacity, not on knowledge.
