## Findings (hard data first)

### 1. NIST AMS 100-34 (Thomas & Weiss, 2020). Economics of manufacturing machinery maintenance, US discrete manufacturing, 2016 data. HARD DATA (survey, n is small, wide CIs)
URL: https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ams/NIST.AMS.100-34.pdf (doi 10.6028/NIST.AMS.100-34)
- Machinery maintenance spend was $57.3B. Fault/failure costs added $16.3B and buffer inventory $0.9B, for a total of **$74.5B**.
- Losses from preventable maintenance issues were **$119.1B**: $18.1B downtime, $0.8B defects, **$100.2B lost sales** from delays and defects. That is 16.03 injuries and 0.05 deaths per million employees.
- **"On average, 45.7 % of machinery maintenance was reactive maintenance."**
- The top quartile by reliance on reactive maintenance had **3.3x more downtime, 16x more defects**, 2.8x more lost sales from defects, 2.4x more lost sales from delays and 4.9x more inventory increases than the bottom quartile.
- Firms that invested more in preventive or predictive maintenance had 44% less downtime, a 54% lower defect rate, 35% fewer lost sales from defects and 29% fewer from delays.
- Among firms with less than 50% reactive maintenance, the top half in PdM had 15% less downtime, an 87% lower defect rate and 66% less inventory increase.
- The 90% CI on maintenance cost is about $42-72B, so the estimates are noisy.
- PdM requires workforce investment: personnel must be trained on what to monitor, how to analyse the data and how to respond.
- Reasoning: **the losses ($119B) are 1.6x the direct maintenance spend ($74.5B), and most of the losses are lost sales from delays.** The economic pain sits in *time-to-restore and unplanned-ness*, not in the repair labour or parts themselves.

### 2. US DOE / FEMP O&M Best Practices Guide, Release 3.0 (PNNL, 2010). HARD-ish (cites a ~2000 study and "independent surveys", the classic source of the numbers everyone repeats)
URL: https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2020/04/f74/omguide_complete_w-eo-disclaimer.pdf
- Average US facility maintenance mix (study from about 2000): **>55% reactive, 31% preventive, 12% predictive, 2% other**. "More than 55% of maintenance resources and activities of an average facility are still reactive."
- Preventive saves an estimated **12-18% vs reactive**. Predictive saves **8-12% over preventive**, and total savings can exceed 30-40% depending on how reactive the starting point is.
- "Independent surveys" of PdM programmes report: ROI 10x, maintenance cost down 25-30%, breakdowns eliminated 70-75%, downtime down 35-45%, production up 20-25%. These figures are widely recycled and the original surveys are not cited, so treat them as soft.
- Top-performing facilities run **<10% reactive, 25-35% preventive, 45-55% predictive**.
- Disadvantages of reactive work: overtime labour, secondary damage, "inefficient use of staff resources". PdM is "not inexpensive": equipment often costs more than $50k, and training needs "considerable funding".
- RCM explicitly says cheap, non-critical equipment is best left to reactive maintenance (run-to-failure is rational for some assets).

### 3. Siemens/Senseye "True Cost of Downtime 2022" (pub. 2023). VENDOR SURVEY (PdM vendor, so incentive to inflate)
URL: https://assets.new.siemens.com/siemens/assets/api/uuid:3d606495-dbe0-43e4-80b1-d04e27ada920/dics-b10153-00-7600truecostofdowntime2022-144.pdf
- Unplanned downtime costs Fortune Global 500 companies about **$1.5T a year, or 11% of turnover**, up from $864B (8%) in 2019-20. That works out to $129M a year per facility.
- The average large plant has **20 downtime incidents a month and loses 25 hours a month**.
- Cost per hour: automotive over $2M, oil and gas about $500k, FMCG about $39k. Hourly costs are up at least 50% in two years because of inflation and plants running at full capacity.
- Cost components: lost revenue, idle wages, wages of the people fixing the fault, **emergency spare parts**, and penalties.
- Full CM/PdM adoption could save 1.6M hours, $734B from 6% higher productivity, and **$236B from a 40% cut in maintenance costs**.
- More than 75% of respondents do some condition monitoring, and a third have an in-house PdM team.
- Reasoning: this source frames the problem as downtime, not knowledge. Its claimed 40% maintenance-cost cut from full PdM adoption is a vendor's upper bound, and it is still well short of 10x.

### 4. Wrench time: typical 25-35%. PRACTITIONER CONSENSUS
- Fiix glossary (Goncalves, Jan 2022): wrench time is "in the 25% to 35% range", so 65-75% of technician time is spent without tools in hand. Self-reported figures run to 70% or more, but about 30% is realistic. URL: https://www.fiixsoftware.com/glossary/wrench-time/
- Limble blog, citing Doc Palmer (*McGraw-Hill Maintenance Planning and Scheduling Handbook*; Plant Services 2017 "Palmer's Planning Corner: Wrench Time"): sampling uses about 160 observations with a margin of error of about ±7%. Palmer's example takes a plant from 35% to 50% through planning and scheduling. URL: https://limble.com/blog/wrench-time
  - Non-wrench categories (unquantified on the page): getting tools and spare parts, reading the work order, travel to the job, breaks and idle time, and receiving instructions.
  - Stated causes of low wrench time: poor planning and scheduling (missing parts or tools, extra travel, job site not ready), reactive interruptions, and waiting on spare parts and tools.
- **[unverified here]** Typical breakdowns from published wrench-time studies (Palmer; Life Cycle Engineering; various consultancies) run roughly as follows:
  - direct work 25-35%
  - travel 10-20%
  - obtaining parts/materials/tools 10-15%
  - waiting/coordination/permits/instructions 10-20%
  - late start, early quit and breaks 10-15%
  - The exact split varies a lot by site; I could not fetch a primary breakdown table.
- Palmer's core argument **[background, consistent with Limble's summary]** is that planning raises wrench time mainly by having parts kitted, tools staged, job plans and permits ready before the job, i.e. *logistics and coordination*, not technician knowledge.

### 5. IDCON "Let's Kill Wrench Time Studies". PRACTITIONER VIEW (contradicts naive reading of the metric)
URL: https://www.idcon.com/resource-library/work-management-planning-scheduling/let-s-kill-wrench-time-studies/
- The result is already known (25-35%), so IDCON says to go straight to fixing planning, scheduling and materials management.
- In good plants, craftspeople do partial planning, CMMS entry, root cause analysis and maintainability work, which a study scores as zero. **Craftspeople may spend 20-30% of their time on "pure thinking"** (diagnosis and understanding), and that time is valuable.
- Maintenance's product is equipment reliability, not time sold. High wrench time matters mainly during shutdowns, and slow work can inflate the metric.
- Reasoning: some non-wrench time *is* the diagnose and understand step, but it is not waste and it is a minority share. Most non-wrench time is logistical.

### 6. IDCON "Get big rewards by improving work management". PRACTITIONER VIEW
URL: https://www.idcon.com/resource-library/work-management-planning-scheduling/get-big-rewards-by-improving-work-management/
- Better work management cuts maintenance cost by about 20% and brings emergency work below 10%. Motivated clients get there in 1-2 years.
- **"90% of the effort" in reliability improvement is people and changing workplace behaviour.** This contradicts a pure technology or knowledge thesis: the bottleneck is organisational discipline.

### 7. IDCON "Planning and Scheduling Mistakes to Avoid" (Owe Forsberg). PRACTITIONER VIEW
URL: https://www.idcon.com/resource-library/work-management-planning-scheduling/common-mistakes-in-planning-and-scheduling/
- The failure modes are organisational:
  - work not identified, because operators aren't trained to write requests
  - break-in work bypasses screening
  - no priority system ("When you don't have priorities, everything is a priority")
  - planners pulled into purchasing and supervision
  - backlog not managed (rule-of-thumb target is 4-6 weeks)
- Poor operations-maintenance teamwork is "the most widespread problem".

### 8. Maintenance cost as % of replacement asset value (RAV)
- Fiix glossary: world-class target is **2-3% of RAV**. Below 2% may mean under-maintenance or deferred work; above 3% may mean inefficiency or ageing assets. URL: https://www.fiixsoftware.com/glossary/replacement-asset-value/
- SMRP Best Practices (7th ed.) holds 70+ metrics with best-in-class targets, but it is behind a member login. URL: https://smrp.org/learning-resources/smrp-library/best-practices-metrics-guidelines/
- **[unverified here]** Typical industrial plants commonly run at 3-6% of RAV, and refinery benchmarks (Solomon) are similar.
- Reasoning: a typical-to-world-class gap of about 1.5-2x is a meaningful efficiency prize, but it is not an order of magnitude.

### 9. Planned maintenance percentage
- Fiix: best-in-class planned maintenance percentage is **85% or higher**. Top performers' mix is about 30% usage-based, 50% condition-based and 5% run-to-failure. URL: https://www.fiixsoftware.com/glossary/planned-maintenance-percentage/
- MaintainX *State of Industrial Maintenance 2026* (survey of 2,234 US/Canada leaders): 64% have a PM programme, yet **50% of teams spend less than 40% of their time on planned work**. URL: https://www.getmaintainx.com/state-of-industrial-maintenance
  - Downtime drivers: **36% cite labour shortages and 28% a lack of necessary skills**.
  - Unplanned downtime rose at 34% of facilities and fell at 21%.
  - **58% already use AI** and 75% report ROI within 6 months. This is a vendor survey, and it does not say what the AI is used for.

### 10. Plant Engineering maintenance software survey. TRADE SURVEY
URL: https://www.plantengineering.com/maintenance-survey
- Respondents put average unplanned downtime cost at about $108k. 59% use a CMMS, 39% an EAM. 48% are on-premise and about 30% cloud or hybrid.
- Reasoning: the system-of-record layer (CMMS/EAM) is already widespread, though data quality is the usual complaint [unverified here].

### 11. BLS Occupational Outlook: industrial machinery mechanics, maintenance workers and millwrights. HARD DATA
URL: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/industrial-machinery-mechanics-and-maintenance-workers-and-millwrights.htm
- About **547,300 jobs (2025)**, of which 446,900 are industrial machinery mechanics. Median pay is $64,100 ($30.82/hr).
- Growth is projected at **+14% for 2025-35 (+18% for mechanics)**, with about 51,900 openings a year, mostly replacement for retirements and transfers.
- The work is described as "read technical manuals, observe equipment, use diagnostic and vibration analysis tools to find the cause", then disassemble, repair and test.
- Reasoning: the labour pool is about $35B a year in wages for this occupation group. Demand for hands is *growing*, which is consistent with knowledge not being the binding constraint: physical labour and availability are.

### 12. Nowlan & Heap, *Reliability-Centered Maintenance* (United Airlines / DoD, 1978). HISTORY
URL (403 from here): https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA066579.pdf. Wikipedia RCM page: "the vast majority of failures are not necessarily linked to the age of the asset".
- **[unverified here, widely cited]** The six age-reliability patterns are A 4%, B 2%, C 5%, D 7%, E 14%, F 68%. About 89% of components show no wear-out zone, so scheduled overhaul does little for them.
- Reasoning: this is the original "knowledge" revolution in maintenance. Aviation solved a big part of the "understand failure" problem 45+ years ago through data analysis and MSG-3. The gains came from *deciding what not to do*, not from making each repair faster.

## Synthesis: where does maintenance labour time go?
- **About 25-35% hands-on wrench time.** The other 65-75% is mostly logistics and coordination: travel, getting parts, tools and permits, waiting for equipment release or operations, getting instructions, and breaks/late starts.
- A smaller but real slice (IDCON: up to 20-30% in good plants) is thinking work: diagnosis, root cause, planning. That is the slice AI most directly addresses.
- Around 45-55% of maintenance work in the average US plant is still reactive (NIST 45.7%; DOE >55%; MaintainX: half of teams spend under 40% of time on planned work).
- Reactive work drives low wrench time, because unplanned jobs have no parts kit, permits or job plan and so cause more travel and waiting. It also drives the large indirect costs (NIST: losses of $119B against $74.5B spend; Siemens: 11% of FG500 turnover).
- **The proven lever is organisational: planning, scheduling, kitting and prioritisation** (Palmer: 35% to 50% wrench time; IDCON: -20% cost, "90% people"). Moving from reactive to PM/PdM is worth 12-18% plus 8-12% of maintenance cost (DOE), and at most about 25-40% (DOE surveys, Siemens). None of these sources shows an order-of-magnitude (10x) reduction in direct maintenance cost.
- Benchmarks put world class at 2-3% of RAV, against roughly 3-6% typical. That is about a 2x gap.

## Stance reasoning
- **C1** (universal chain): mixed. Industrial practice adds steps the chain omits: identify/request, screen, prioritise, plan, kit, schedule, permit/LOTO, coordinate with operations, execute, test, close out and record. Much of the time sits in *scheduling/coordination/waiting*, which the chain doesn't name. Industry also chooses run-to-failure deliberately (RCM).
- **C2** (knowledge monopoly): mixed, mostly contradicts for in-plant work. Inefficiency comes mainly from planning, logistics and reactive culture. Skills shortages are real (MaintainX 28% skills, 36% labour), but they are about people as much as knowledge. Plants own their own maintenance; OEM lock-in is not the dominant story here.
- **C3** (AI closes the gap): mixed/contradicts. AI adoption is reported at 58%, yet the reactive share and labour constraints persist. PdM needs sensors (>$50k), training and behaviour change, and the "90% people" point stands.
- **C4** (10x on four steps): contradicts at the industrial scale. The evidence gives 12-40% savings and a roughly 1.5x wrench-time gain. Parts logistics are part of the waiting time but do not dominate total cost; lost production does. A 10x cut in diagnosis would remove only a minority of time.
- **C5** (bespoke parts): silent here. Emergency spares appear only as a downtime cost component, and buffer inventory is small ($0.9B, NIST).
- **C6** (inspection essential): mostly silent, mildly supportive (testing and inspection are part of every job). Condition monitoring is a kind of continuous inspection, and AI is already entering there.
- **C7** (built to be inefficient): mixed. Reactive practice is widespread and wrench time is low, which supports C7. But the fix (planning, RCM, PdM) has been known for decades and best-in-class plants already reach under 10% reactive, 85%+ planned and 2-3% RAV. The inefficiency is mainly management discipline and incentives, not structural design. Run-to-failure is sometimes optimal.
