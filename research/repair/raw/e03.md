## Findings

### 1. Catastrophic repairs: a bad repair, not missing knowledge, killed plants [VERIFIED-FETCH]
**San Onofre (SONGS).** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Onofre_Nuclear_Generating_Station
- Edison and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) replaced the steam generators over a 10-year, $671M project finished in 2011. Within about a year the new generators showed wear at about 15,000 places across more than 3,000 tubes. The cause was fluid-elastic vibration and inadequate U-bend support, a design error by the OEM.
- Unit 3 leaked in January 2012. Neither unit restarted, and both were retired in June 2013.
- SCE claimed $7.57B. In 2017 the ICC tribunal awarded $125M, which was the contract's liability cap, and SCE had to pay MHI's $58M in costs.
- Insurance paid $400M. A ratepayer settlement of about $3.3B is reported. Decommissioning is estimated at $4.4B.
- Lesson: the OEM, the holder of the "knowledge monopoly", got the design wrong. Liability caps meant the owner and ratepayers carried almost all of the cost.

**Crystal River 3.** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_River_Nuclear_Plant
- In 2009 the plant cut an access opening in containment to replace its steam generators. The engineering plan called for detensioning 97 tendons. Progress Energy reduced that to 65, and only 27 were actually detensioned.
- The concrete then delaminated, and repairs kept triggering new cracks. Repair estimates were $0.9-1.3B, and an independent review in 2012 put them at $1.5B, worst case $3.4B. Insurance recovery was expected at about $850M.
- The plant was retired in 2013.
- Former NRC chairman Jaczko blamed "improper work planning, improper understanding of how to properly do this containment retrofit."
- Lesson: execution and work planning failed. The knowledge existed (the engineering plan was right) and was overridden.

**Davis-Besse.** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis-Besse_Nuclear_Power_Station
- Boric-acid corrosion of the reactor head was found in 2002. The plant was down until March 2004, and repairs and upgrades cost $600M.
- Penalties: an NRC fine of over $5M (its largest) plus a $28M DOJ settlement.
- The replacement head was **sourced by buying one from the mothballed Midland plant**. That shows how thin the supply of large nuclear components is: the fix was salvage, not fabrication.
- Most of the two years went to "corrective reviews and engineering changes" (regulatory and organisational recovery), not to physical repair.

### 2. Nearly every US PWR has replaced its steam generators [VERIFIED-FETCH]
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_generator_(nuclear_power)
- "Most U.S. PWR plants have had steam generators replaced", moving from Alloy 600 to Alloy 690 tubing.
- Tube inspection by eddy current and plugging of bad tubes need a reactor shutdown, so inspection sets the outage schedule.

### 3. Fleet performance: capacity factor rose from under 60% to about 90% or more [VERIFIED-FETCH]
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_the_United_States
- Capacity factor was 64% in 1978 and below 60% through much of the 1970s and 80s. It got back to the 1978 level in 1990, has been about 90% since 2001, was 92% in 2007, and 93% in 2019.
- No new knowledge technology drove this. The causes were consolidation of operators, INPO peer benchmarking, shared outage best practice, pre-staged parts and much shorter refuelling outages.
- Closures for economic reasons: SONGS, Crystal River, Vermont Yankee and Kewaunee (2013-14) cited high maintenance and repair costs against cheap gas. FirstEnergy (2018) and Exelon (Byron and Dresden, 2020, later reversed with subsidies) announced closures on economics.

### 4. EDF 2022 stress-corrosion crisis: inspection and specialist welding capacity were the bottleneck [VERIFIED-FETCH, partly BACKGROUND]
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_France
- Up to 32 of 56 reactors were offline in September 2022. 2022 output was 279 TWh, against a normal level of about 360-400 TWh.
- The EBITDA impact was about -€18.5B, and market power purchases were estimated at €29B.
- The Grand Carénage life-extension programme is costed at €49.4B.
- [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED] EDF lacked qualified pipe welders and brought in welders from Westinghouse in the US and Canada. A large part of the downtime was precautionary inspection (cutting out pipe sections for lab metallurgy), because the non-destructive testing methods could not reliably size the cracks.
- Lesson: the scarce layers were **inspection capability and certified hands**. The diagnosis was not limited by knowing what to look for. It was limited by the ability to measure it without cutting pipe.

### 5. Davis-Besse and Midland: large parts are bespoke and come from salvage [VERIFIED-FETCH]
- See finding 1. Reactor heads, steam generators and large forgings come from very few forging houses (historically Japan Steel Works, Doosan, Le Creusot). Lead times run to years. [BACKGROUND] The 2016 Le Creusot forging-records falsification scandal at Areva put about 400 components under review and kept several French reactors down in 2016-17.

### 6. Operating cost structure [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED; NEI page returned 403]
Source: NEI "Nuclear Costs in Context", https://www.nei.org/resources/reports-briefs/nuclear-costs-in-context
- The US average total generating cost fell from about $44-46/MWh in 2012 to about $30-32/MWh in 2022-23, a drop of roughly 30-35%.
- Approximate split: operations about $19-20 (60%), capital (sustaining capex, much of it obsolescence-driven replacement) about $6-7, fuel about $5-6.
- The fall was driven by the NEI "Delivering the Nuclear Promise" programme (2015 onward): headcount cuts, standardised engineering, combined purchasing, fewer and shorter outages, and work-scope reduction. Of the efficiency programmes, these process and organisational changes did more than any diagnostic technology.
- Implication: the cost is labour plus compliance plus downtime. The parts themselves are a small share.

### 7. Refuelling outage duration [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED]
- The average US refuelling outage was about 100+ days around 1990. It is about 30-35 days today; EIA has reported fleet averages in the low 30s. Best operators (Constellation, formerly Exelon) report fleet averages of about 20-25 days, and record outages at single units are about 15-18 days.
- Outages run on 18- or 24-month cycles. During one, 1,000-2,000 extra contract workers (mostly itinerant craft) arrive on site.
- Derived arithmetic: a 1 GW unit makes about 24,000 MWh a day. At $30-60/MWh, every day of outage is worth **about $0.7-1.5M of lost revenue** before outage labour, so critical-path days dominate the economics.
- The critical path is typically refuelling itself, reactor vessel and steam generator inspections (ISI, eddy current), valve and pump overhauls, and emergent work found during inspection. Industry practice is to treat "discovery work" as the main source of overrun.

### 8. Emergent repairs and forced outages [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED]
- The US fleet forced loss rate is about 1-2%. Emergent work found in outages (for example boric acid leaks, cracked baffle bolts, valve problems) is the main cause of outages running long.
- Typical root causes are aging degradation such as stress corrosion cracking, flow-accelerated corrosion and vibration fatigue. Diagnosis itself is often fast. The time goes into engineering disposition (Operability Determination, 10 CFR 50.59 screening), ASME Section XI repair or replacement rules, procuring a qualified part and NRC relief requests where needed.

### 9. Obsolete analog I&C: the regulator and licensing are the bottleneck, not technical knowledge [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED; NRC page partly fetched]
Source: https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/digital.html [VERIFIED-FETCH] says only that the NRC applies the same requirements to analog and digital, focuses on "diversity and defense-in-depth", and notes that the fleet is "adding digital upgrades to address obsolescence in analog equipment."

[BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED]:
- Most of the US fleet still runs 1970s analog safety I&C. Spare cards and modules come from cannibalising retired plants, reverse engineering, or EPRI-coordinated obsolescence databases and shared spare pools.
- Only a handful of safety-related digital I&C upgrades have been licensed in the US: Oconee RPS/ESPS (approved about 2010 after a multi-year review), Diablo Canyon PPS, and Constellation's Limerick full digital safety I&C upgrade (approved about 2024, presented as the first under the modernised alternate review process).
- The common-cause-failure (CCF) software issue and the ISG-06 licensing process made projects expensive and uncertain. Several utilities abandoned projects in the 2010s; there were reports of more than $100M budgets and licence reviews lasting many years.
- INL's LWRS programme argues that digital transformation (online monitoring, automated surveillances, digital work packages) could cut O&M by tens of percent. The main barriers it names are regulatory uncertainty and utility business cases, not missing knowledge.

### 10. Additive manufacturing of qualified parts works, but qualification is the bottleneck [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED]
- 2020: Westinghouse installed a 3D-printed thimble plugging device at Exelon's Byron Unit 1.
- 2021: TVA Browns Ferry Unit 2 loaded four 3D-printed fuel-channel fasteners made by ORNL and Framatome, the first safety-related additively manufactured parts in a US reactor.
- ASME Code Case work and NRC AM guidance (for example on laser powder bed fusion of 316L) are in progress. Each new material and process needs years of qualification data.
- Commercial Grade Dedication (CGD) is the route for using non-nuclear parts in safety-related service. It needs critical-characteristic testing, and that procedure often costs several times the part.
- Lesson for C5: being able to make a part is not the binding constraint. **Proving equivalence to a regulator** is.

### 11. Counterfeit and fraudulent items: provenance and paperwork are a core risk [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED; Wikipedia page 404]
- South Korea, 2012-13: forged quality certificates for cables and parts at KHNP plants led to shutdowns of several reactors (Shin Kori 2, Shin Wolsong 1 and others). Dozens of people were indicted. It caused summer power shortages.
- France, 2016: falsified forging records at Areva's Le Creusot.
- US: the NRC has issued long-running counterfeit, fraudulent and suspect item (CFSI) guidance.
- Implication: cheaper or faster sourcing increases the burden of verifying provenance. Any "AI parts factory" adds to that burden.

### 12. Palisades restart: getting back to service is a long chain [VERIFIED-FETCH, partial]
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palisades_Nuclear_Generating_Station
- Holtec bought the plant in 2022. Funding includes a $1.5B DOE conditional loan (2024) and $300M from the state.
- The licence status returned to operational in August 2025. Restart in late 2025 needed operators requalified, a deep clean of systems, fuel receipt, and fuel-handling incidents resolved, including a bent assembly that needed a one-time NRC licence amendment.
- [BACKGROUND] Extensive steam generator tube degradation was found, and sleeving repairs were done. That again shows that inspection-driven discovery sets the timeline.

## Reasoning against the hypotheses

- **C1 (universal chain): supported, with caveats.** Nuclear follows the chain explicitly, but two features break the neat ordering. (a) "Noticing" is mostly scheduled inspection during planned outages, not failure-driven. (b) Engineering disposition, regulatory screening (50.59, ASME XI, relief requests) and dedication or qualification sit between "understand" and "source" and are often the longest steps. Return to service is itself a regulated step with hold points.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed.** OEMs (Westinghouse, Framatome, MHI, GE-Hitachi) do hold design-basis knowledge and proprietary codes, and utilities depend on them for large components and I&C. But the costliest failures (SONGS, Crystal River) were OEM or contractor errors or bad execution, not owners lacking knowledge. The industry also already pools knowledge heavily through EPRI, INPO, owners' groups and operating-experience databases. The scarce thing is legally qualified knowledge and credentialed people, not information.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicted for this domain.** The binding constraints are regulatory acceptance of evidence, qualified welders and NDE technicians, forgings capacity and liability. AI can speed literature search, work-package drafting, operating-experience retrieval and diagnostics: INL and EPRI are piloting LLMs and online monitoring. But the NRC still needs a licensee-owned, quality-assured (10 CFR 50 Appendix B) basis. Software CCF concerns make AI in safety functions especially slow.
- **C4 (10x cost drop in four steps): contradicted.** Cost sits in labour (about 60% of generating cost is operations), downtime (about $1M a day per unit) and compliance. Diagnosis and parts logistics are a small part. The 30-35% cost cut of 2012-2023 came from process and organisation (Delivering the Nuclear Promise), not from cheaper diagnosis. Order-of-magnitude savings in those four steps would move total repair cost by perhaps 10-20%.
- **C5 (bespoke parts solved): contradicted.** Additive manufacturing is real (Browns Ferry, Byron) but qualification takes years for each material and process. CGD costs more than fabrication. Large forgings remain a world bottleneck that is solved by salvage (Midland head). Counterfeiting history makes regulators suspicious of new supply routes.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): strongly supported.** Inspection (eddy current, ultrasonic, ISI) sets outage critical paths. EDF 2022 showed that weak NDE crack sizing forced destructive cut-outs. Automated and AI-assisted NDE analysis (eddy-current data auto-analysis is already used) and online monitoring that replaces time-based surveillances are where the leverage is.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.** Inefficiency is partly deliberate: defence-in-depth, conservatism, independent verification, liability allocation. Partly it is regulatory ossification, as in the decade-long digital I&C stalemate. But the US fleet has already been radically optimised (outages cut from about 100 to about 30 days, capacity factor from about 60% to about 93%) without a "fundamental rebuild". That is evidence that incremental process discipline can extract large gains.

## Where the money and time go
1. Downtime: about $1M a day per GW unit, plus replacement power. EDF's €29B of market purchases is the extreme case.
2. Labour: operations is about 60% of generating cost, and outages bring in 1,000-2,000 contractors.
3. Discovery or emergent work on the critical path during inspection.
4. Engineering disposition and regulatory paperwork.
5. Rare catastrophic repairs costing $0.6-3.4B, which ended the life of whole plants.

Parts cost and shipping are small, except for large forgings, where the issue is lead time measured in years, not shipping.

## Real bottleneck
Qualified evidence and qualified people:
- NRC-acceptable proof that a part, software or repair meets the design basis (Appendix B QA, ASME, CGD, digital CCF).
- Certified NDE technicians and welders, plus inspection methods that can size defects without destructive cut-outs.
- Scarce heavy forging capacity.

## Company shapes suggested
- AI-assisted NDE data analysis (eddy current, UT, phased array) that cuts critical-path inspection days and gives confident crack sizing. This is regulator-facing.
- Qualification and dedication-as-a-service: an AI-assembled, Appendix-B-compliant evidence package for commercial grade dedication or reverse-engineered obsolete parts, combined with additive manufacturing. The product is the dossier, not the part.
- Obsolete I&C replacement modules with form-fit-function equivalence and pre-built licensing basis (an FPGA or analog-equivalent approach that avoids software CCF).
- Outage critical-path optimisation and emergent-work prediction from operating-experience data.
- Provenance and anti-counterfeit tracking for nuclear parts.
