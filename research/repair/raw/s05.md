## Findings

### F1 [FETCHED, data] CCC Crash Course 2025 (Q1 2025), https://www.cccis.com/crash-course
- Total-loss share hit a record 22.3% of all loss categories in 2024 (22.9% non-comprehensive). Over 70% of 2024 total-loss valuations were on vehicles 7 or more years old.
- Average labor rates rose +4.5% in 2024 (after +7.5% in 2023). EV labor rates run about 30% above ICE.
- Parts replaced per estimate: 13.5 industry-wide, with EV 22, hybrid 18.5 and ICE 15.9.
- Scans appeared on 86.3% of repairable DRP appraisals in Q4 2024 (avg fee about $157), and 91.7% of scans were on the first estimate.
- ADAS calibration appeared on 29.9% of repairable DRP appraisals (avg about $547 with diagnostic op), and 56.3% of calibrations were on a SUPPLEMENT, meaning they were not caught at first estimate.
- Total repair days are down about 2 days from the peak but still 4-5 days above 2020. EV repairs average 37.5 total days.
- Average total cost of repair (TCOR) change vs 2020 was +$1,285 in 2024.
- Reading: cost growth comes from vehicle complexity (sensors, calibrations, more parts, higher labor), not from a lack of estimating knowledge. The supplement rate on calibrations is a real "diagnose/understand" failure: the first estimate misses work that only shows up after teardown. Photo AI cannot see hidden damage, so it cannot close that gap on its own.

### F2 [FETCHED, data] III / ISO collision claim severity 2015-2024, https://www.iii.org/fact-statistic/facts-statistics-auto-insurance
- Collision severity went from $3,377 (2015) to $5,489 (2024), up 63%. Frequency went from 6.01 to 4.16 claims per 100 car-years, down 31%.
- Big jumps came in 2021 (+19%) and 2022 (+17%): supply chain, parts and used-car prices.
- HLDI 2022-2024 model years: average collision severity $10,267.
- Reading: fewer crashes (partly ADAS) but each repair costs more. Software and estimating have digitised for 30 years while severity rose, so digitising information flow has not bent the cost curve. Parts, labor and complexity dominate.

### F3 [FETCHED, practitioner/vendor data] Tractable x Admiral Seguros case study, https://tractable.ai/en/case-studies/admiral-seguros
- 12,000 claims in 2021 handled "touchless". 90% of estimates were produced without a human appraiser and 98% of claims were completed in under 15 minutes. 70-75% of customers who received the link finished the flow, often in about 2 minutes.
- Reading: AI can collapse the *appraisal/estimate* step (days down to minutes) for simple, mostly cosmetic, low-severity claims. This is vendor-reported, from a selected customer, and does not cover the full repair cycle. The Aioi Nissay Dowa case study gave no numbers ([FETCHED] https://tractable.ai/en/case-studies/aioi-nissay-dowa). The Tractable homepage claims "thousands of claims daily" but publishes no cycle-time or cost metric ([FETCHED] https://tractable.ai/en/).

### F8 [BACKGROUND, regulator] FTC v. CCC Holdings / Mitchell (2013-2014)
- The FTC sued to block CCC's acquisition of Mitchell in Nov 2013, arguing the two were 2 of only 3 meaningful US providers (with Audatex) of estimating software and total-loss valuation systems. CCC abandoned the deal in March 2014.
- Reading: there is a three-firm oligopoly in the information layer, and the US government treats it as concentrated. That is a "data/platform monopoly", which is close to but not identical to C2's "knowledge monopoly". VERIFY URL on ftc.gov.

### F9 [BACKGROUND, court] In re Auto Body Shop Antitrust Litigation (MDL 2557, M.D. Fla., A&E Auto Body v. 21st Century et al.)
- Hundreds of body shops sued State Farm and dozens of insurers alleging labor-rate suppression and DRP steering/boycott. The cases were dismissed repeatedly for failure to plead conspiracy, and the 11th Circuit largely affirmed (around 2019-2021).
- Reading: shops' core complaint is *payment power* (insurer-set "prevailing rates", steering to DRPs that accept discounts and concessions), not knowledge. The courts did not find illegal conspiracy, so the structure is lawful market power. That supports C7's "structurally built" claim, but the mechanism is payer power, not knowledge. VERIFY.

### F10 [BACKGROUND, court/history] Avery v. State Farm (Illinois, 1999)
- A jury verdict of about $1.18B found State Farm breached contracts by specifying non-OEM (aftermarket) crash parts. The Illinois Supreme Court reversed in 2005.
- Reading: the parts-sourcing step in collision is shaped by insurer cost control (aftermarket, LKQ recycled, reconditioned) versus OEM repair procedures and position statements. It is a fight over *who pays and which part is allowed*, not over whether a part can be made. VERIFY.

### F11 [BACKGROUND, practitioner] DRP and OEM certification economics
- DRPs give insurers a volume-for-concession deal: discounted labor and parts, CSI and cycle-time KPIs, insurer-written estimates and photo-based triage. Large MSOs (Caliber, Gerber/Boyd, Crash Champions, Classic) grew by stacking DRPs. Boyd 10-Ks have cited a majority of revenue from insurance-paid DRP work. VERIFY.
- OEM certification programs (Honda ProFirst, Tesla, Ford, GM, I-CAR Gold Class) require equipment such as aluminium rooms, rivet guns, welders and scan tools, with commonly cited costs from tens of thousands up to $100k-$250k+ per shop. They also require OEM repair procedures, which OEMs increasingly publish (often behind paid subscription). Example: Ford's procedures are free on its site, and many others charge per day or per year. Some OEMs restrict structural part sales to certified shops (Tesla), which gates parts sourcing. VERIFY figures.
- Reading: on parts gating and tooling capex, C2 is partly right: OEMs control *access* (parts, procedures, scan tools, calibration targets). But much of the "knowledge" is already published. The binding constraint is that the shop must have the equipment, training and certification that let it act on that knowledge.

### F12 [BACKGROUND, data] Technician shortage
- The TechForce Foundation and Collision Repair Education Foundation have reported large annual shortfalls of collision techs (tens of thousands of new entrants needed per year) and an ageing workforce. CCC data shows the 2022-23 cycle-time spike was driven by shop capacity and parts delays. VERIFY.
- Reading: the time bottleneck is *physical capacity (techs, bays) plus parts availability*, not estimating.

### F13 [BACKGROUND, investor/vendor] CCC AI products
- CCC launched "Estimate-STP" (straight-through photo estimate) in 2021 and says its AI is used on millions of claims. CCC (NASDAQ: CCCS, IPO via SPAC 2021) connects about 300+ insurers and about 30,000 repair facilities, with revenue around $900M-$1B per year.
- Reading: the incumbent platform already ships photo-AI at scale. A startup offering "AI estimating" in US collision competes with CCC/Mitchell/Solera distribution and data moats. VERIFY figures in CCC 10-K.

## Synthesis

**Where money and time go in collision:**
1. The physical repair: labor hours times a rising labor rate.
2. Parts: about 13-22 per repair, with high OEM part prices and sensor-laden bumpers, mirrors and windshields.
3. New scan and calibration operations, which are mandatory and often supplemented.
4. Waiting: parts delays, shop backlog and technician shortage. Repair days are still +4-5 vs 2020, and EVs average 37.5 days.

Estimating/appraisal is a small share of dollars. It used to be a big share of *calendar time* (the adjuster visit), and photo-AI does compress that piece from days to minutes. But the estimate is then frequently revised after teardown: supplements are the norm, and 56% of calibrations are supplemented. Even a 10x cheaper estimate barely moves total repair cost or keys-to-keys time.

**Real bottleneck:**
- **Payment:** a three-firm information oligopoly (CCC, Mitchell, Audatex) is the "common language" that insurers use to set prices, and DRPs channel volume to compliant shops.
- **Physical:** OEM gating of parts, procedures and scan/calibration access, plus a shortage of certified technicians and equipped bays.

Knowledge is mostly codified already (databases, OEM procedures). The binding constraints are *who pays and on what terms*, *who may buy the part*, and *who has the tooling and certification*.

**Stance summary:**
- C1 mixed: the chain fits, but collision adds a payer-negotiation and valuation/total-loss decision step, plus a teardown-supplement loop. The process is iterative, not linear.
- C2 mixed: there is an information oligopoly and OEM gating, but the dominant inefficiency is payer power and capacity.
- C3 contradicts: AI does well on visible damage, but hidden damage, calibration needs and OEM access are not knowledge problems.
- C4 contradicts: estimate time drops 10x, but those steps are a small share of cost, and parts and labor dominate.
- C5 silent/contradicts: collision parts are mass-produced. Gating is commercial and regulatory (OEM-only structural parts, insurer aftermarket rules), not "can it be made". Safety-critical parts also need certification.
- C6 supports: states are adding ADAS certification and calibration requirements, and OEM certification is growing.
- C7 mixed/supports: incentives (DRP concessions, prevailing-rate fights) are structural, but the industry is heavily digitised and consolidating (MSOs, CCC). It is "built for payer cost control", not "not built for smart".
