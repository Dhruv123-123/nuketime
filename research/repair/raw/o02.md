# o02: Consulting literature on aftermarket service (Cohen, Agrawal, Agrawal HBR 2006 "Winning in the aftermarket"; Bain, McKinsey, BCG, Deloitte service reports): what inefficiencies and margins do they report?

Researched 2026-10-20. Note: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through, so some planned follow-ups (McKinsey gen-AI field service figures, McKinsey "Why aftermarket and service are vital to OEMs", Deloitte 2024 "smart operations") were not done. McKinsey PDFs returned HTTP 503, so McKinsey numbers below come from search-result snippets, not full reads.

## Primary sources read in full

### 1. Cohen, Agrawal & Agrawal, "Winning in the Aftermarket", HBR May 2006 (full text read)
URL: https://fishmandavidson.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Winning-in-the-Aftermarket_HBR_May_06.pdf (HBR page: https://hbr.org/2006/05/winning-in-the-aftermarket)
Conflict note: Cohen chaired MCA Solutions and V. Agrawal co-founded it. MCA sold after-sales service supply-chain software, so the authors were vendors of the fix they recommend.

Hard numbers (all second-hand inside the article, mostly late 1990s to 2004):
- Aberdeen Group: US spare parts and after-sales services come to **8% of GDP, about $1 trillion a year** "on assets they already own".
- In autos, white goods, industrial machinery and IT, aftermarkets are **4-5x larger than the original-equipment business**.
- Firms at the Wharton-Stanford Service Supply Chain forum (2004) said **29-50% of revenues** come from servicing products.
- AMR Research (1999): **45% of gross profits from the aftermarket on 24% of revenues**.
- Accenture: GM earned relatively more profit on **$9B of after-sales revenue (2001)** than on **$150B of car sales**.
- One big US automaker: **about 50% of consumers with problems faced unnecessary repair delays because dealers lacked the right parts.** There was little coordination between parts warehouses and dealers.
- OEMs hold **about 10% of annual sales as spares**. Inventory turns run **1-2x a year** (versus 6-50x in manufacturing supply chains), and **23% of parts become obsolete every year**.
- The service network handles **15-20x more SKUs** than manufacturing. Demand is "always unpredictable, sporadic", and the network must handle reverse flows of failed parts.
- "Third-party vendors have become so price competitive that OEMs lose most of the aftermarket the moment the initial warranty period ends."
- Customer satisfaction with after-sales support was **10-15% below expectations** (authors' 1997 study).
- Semiconductor fabs used to accept a 2-day response in the 1980s and now expect **15 minutes**.
- Firms that adopted the authors' approach saw **service quality up 10-15%, service-asset investment down 25-50%, operating costs down 10%**. This is vendor-reported.

Diagnosis of the cause (opinion): management neglect ("Top managements the world over treat aftermarket services as a mere afterthought"). Firms apply ERP and deterministic forecasting to a probabilistic problem. They measure part-fill rate rather than customer uptime. Service is treated as a "stepchild". The authors do not blame a knowledge monopoly. They also say service knowledge is a moat: "when businesses provide aftermarket support, they gain a deep understanding of customers' technologies, processes, and plans – knowledge that rivals can't easily acquire."

### 2. Deloitte Insights, "Aftermarket services: Transforming manufacturing in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic" (2020; 2022 PDF; full text read)
URL: https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-shared/legacy/docs/insights/2022/DI_Rise-of-Aftermarket-services.pdf
- Aftermarket **operating margin is about 2.5x** the margin on new equipment, globally.
- Figure 1 (major US industrial products companies):
  - 2005: products 7.0% operating margin, services 15.5%; revenue split 78.1/21.9.
  - 2008: products 7.5%, services 18.2%; split 77.1/22.9.
  - 2018: products 9.0%, services 23.0%; split 76.4/23.6.
  - So the services share of revenue barely moved (22% to 24%) in 13 years, while service margins rose.
- "Many manufacturers generate 40–50% of their overall profits from services." Parts and services are expected to deliver over 50% of profit. For some firms the aftermarket already accounts for the entire profit, and new equipment is sold mainly "to fuel the future aftersales business."
- Service-focused manufacturers often have **80%+ of their installed base** under service contracts.
- Bobst: remote access solves **80% of electrical problems online**. This is evidence that remote diagnosis is already displacing truck rolls.
- Daimler Trucks NA uses 3D printing to deal with long spare-part lead times and "parts where suppliers are unable to find the right tooling". This is a real but limited analog for C5.
- Airbus Skywise gives customers access to **20,000 Airbus engineers** across an aircraft's life. Here the OEM is using the platform to keep hold of its knowledge.
- Executives expect the business model to move from a 75:25 products-to-outcomes mix to 50:50 within a decade.
- Eaton's aftermarket VP: "Customers primarily look at transparency and speed to resolution."

### 3. Bain, "Winning in industrial service" (June 2015, 2014 survey of 45 European manufacturers)
URL: https://www.bain.com/insights/winning-in-industrial-service/
- Service is **22% of revenue** at a **39% average gross margin**: 42% for equipment makers, 30% for systems and plant engineering, 46% for components.
- Service grew **9% a year (2010-13)**, nearly double the growth of new equipment.
- **Spare parts are 49%** of the service business.
- The average service-contract attach rate on new equipment is **only just above 20%**.
- Inefficiency spread across firms:
  - Technician utilisation is 83% at champions vs 72% at followers. The best exceed 90% and the worst fall below 50%.
  - Technician attrition is 1-2% at the best vs 10-15% at the worst.
  - On-time delivery is 89% vs 80%.
- Champions earn a 46% gross margin vs 37% for followers.

### 4. Bain, "Service Now" study (2012)
URL: https://media.bain.com/Images/Bain%20study_Service_Now.pdf
- Services are about **20-25% of revenue but about half of profits** in industrial goods. Service EBIT is about 20%, versus 5-10% in the core business. Top performers reach 60% service revenue share and >25% EBIT.
- "When considering the full-service potential of their installed base, companies typically reach only **10% to 25% of potential revenue** – and often companies don't even know where they stand." This is the key wallet-leakage number: 75-90% of service spend on OEM installed base goes elsewhere (to independents, in-house crews, or deferral).
- Case: at one OEM, competitors were capturing **80% of the service business on its installed equipment**.
- Europe's elevator big four (Otis, Schindler, Kone, Thyssenkrupp) **raised EBIT 56% in 2008-09** while industrial goods earnings fell 37%. Service annuities are countercyclical, and elevator service is a famously closed, OEM-dominated, inspection-mandated market.
- The cause given is organisational, not a knowledge gap: "OEMs' engineering roots have led them to focus their attention and investments on technical innovation".

### 5. BCG, "Aftermarket services drive growth for industrial manufacturers" (2025, about 100 machinery companies averaging >$2B revenue)
URL: https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/aftermarket-services-drive-growth-for-industrial-manufacturers
- Service revenue rose **10% in 2023**, with **8%** expected for 2024.
- Service gross margins are about **double the 15-25%** typical of equipment. **Spare parts and consumables run about 50% gross margin.** Top performers make about 42% gross margin and about 20% EBIT on services.
- Service is 33% of revenue at equipment makers with direct customer access, vs 17% at component makers.
- Top performers hold **38% fewer days of inventory**.
- Between a quarter and a half of companies struggle to capture service potential.
- **Only 4%** have generated recurring revenue from digital services, even though **28%** of new machines are now connected.
- **About one-third have moved AI past pilots** in field-technician support and guided troubleshooting. Only **5-8%** have reached pilots in spare-parts forecasting and service RFP analysis.
- AI value-based pricing lifts spare-parts EBIT by **3-4 percentage points**.
- Digital tools cut operating cost by **5-15%**.
- Takeaway: incumbents are already deploying AI for diagnosis and knowledge, and are using it to raise prices on parts, not lower them.

### 6. Bain, "Cracking the services conundrum in aerospace and defense" (Jan 2020)
URL: https://www.bain.com/insights/cracking-the-services-conundrum-in-aerospace-and-defense/
- Services are **nearly 80% of an aircraft's life-cycle value**. Service gross margins are **1.4x** those of new equipment.
- Services add about $15B to the A&D profit pool from 2017 to 2027, reaching $45B.
- Services are 35% of the A&D market, but **OEM service revenue is only 15-25% of OEM revenue**. Boeing, Airbus and Embraer target roughly 3x their 2017 service revenue within 10 years.
- Airlines increasingly do their own repairs. Services need overhead 10-20% lower than a product business.
- So for airliners, OEMs are trying to grab more of the repair chain, not less.

## Secondary sources (snippets; not fully read)
- McKinsey, "Industrial aftermarket services: Growing the core" (about 2017-18; https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/industrials-and-electronics/our-insights/industrial-aftermarket-services-growing-the-core):
  - Across 30 industries, aftermarket **EBIT averages 25% vs 10%** for new equipment.
  - Repricing 100,000 spare-parts SKUs raised one machinery maker's EBIT by **2 points in one year**.
  - Large margin gains came from pricing, not repair productivity.
- McKinsey/AEM survey of ~1,300 US farmers and contractors (https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/automotive-and-assembly/our-insights/for-oems-in-the-united-states-the-aftermarket-is-fertile-ground):
  - **Half of farmers say technicians take too long to arrive**, 29% say dealers respond too slowly, and 21% have no way to check repair availability.
  - After warranty, **46% of farmers and 64% of contractors** go outside the OEM channel, including repairing in-house. This came from search snippets and could not be re-verified because the page returned 503.
  - Pain points named: nontransparent pricing, high repair cost, unresponsive dealers and technicians. This is where the time goes: waiting for a technician (a labour and logistics constraint), not diagnosis.
- Aberdeen Group (via SDCExec): aftermarket parts and service margins are "as much as 10 times" initial product margins, at 20-30% of revenue and ~40% of profit. Vendor-adjacent. https://www.sdcexec.com/sourcing-procurement/consulting/news/10316760/
- Automotive: the dealer "facing fill rate" benchmark is about 95% (Automotive Logistics). DMEautomotive (2012): only 10% of dealer customers' service spend leaks to independents.

## Synthesis / reasoning

**Margins.** Every consulting source finds the same structure.
- Service is about 20-25% of revenue but 40-50% of profit, with margins 1.4x to 2.5x (sometimes "up to 10x") those of new equipment.
- Spare parts are the profit core, at ~50% gross margin.
- The pricing lever is spare-parts repricing, now AI-assisted.
- This is the "razor/blade" lock-in structure that a C7-style critique describes. But consultants frame it as a feature to exploit, not a bug.

**Inefficiencies reported.** These are overwhelmingly operational and managerial, not knowledge-based:
1. Parts logistics. Cohen et al.: 50% of repair delays at one automaker came from wrong or missing parts, 1-2 inventory turns, 23% annual obsolescence, 10% of sales tied up in spares, 15-20x the SKUs of manufacturing, and lumpy probabilistic demand.
2. Labour. Bain: technician utilisation ranges from <50% to >90%, attrition from 1-2% to 10-15%. McKinsey: half of farmers complain about technician arrival time.
3. Commercial capture. Bain: OEMs get only 10-25% of installed-base service potential, and attach rates are about 20%.
4. Org neglect. Service is a "stepchild" and an "afterthought", run with manufacturing ERP logic.
5. Digital monetisation is weak. BCG: 4% earn recurring digital revenue despite 28% connectivity.

**Against C2/C3/C4.**
- Nowhere does the literature say diagnosis or "understanding" is the main cost or time sink. The time sinks are parts availability and technician dispatch.
- The 75-90% of installed-base service the OEM does not capture shows that independents and owners already do most repair in many industrial categories. In those categories the knowledge monopoly is leaky, and it was leaky before AI.
- OEM knowledge moats are real (Airbus Skywise's 20,000 engineers, Cohen's "knowledge rivals can't easily acquire"). But incumbents are adopting AI themselves: a third are past pilots in guided troubleshooting.
- OEMs use AI to raise parts prices (+3-4 pts EBIT). AI on its own therefore strengthens incumbent rent capture unless parts and access are unbundled.
- No source suggests a 10x cost drop. Reported gains are single- to low-double-digit percentages: 10% opex, 5-15% from digital, 25-50% inventory investment (vendor-reported), and 2-4 pts EBIT.

**For C7, partially.** The structure is built for OEM margin, not for owner efficiency. New equipment is sold "to fuel the future aftersales business", and margins are 2.5x and rising with parts at ~50% gross. Customer satisfaction runs 10-15% below expectations, and half of repairs are delayed by parts. But consultants attribute the waste to management neglect and wrong tools, which are fixable within incumbents, rather than to a design that requires a rebuild.

**C6.** Only indirectly supported. Elevator service, a mandated-inspection category, shows the most resilient OEM economics (+56% EBIT in the crisis). Regulation and inspection anchor incumbents' service lock-in. AR/VR remote quality checks (Deloitte) hint that inspection itself is being digitised.

**C5.** Weak support. 3D printing is used at Daimler Trucks for small parts where tooling is unavailable. That is narrow, and nothing in this literature suggests bespoke part-making is "solved".

**C1.** The literature models service as a supply chain of parts, people and infrastructure, plus reverse logistics and a response-time promise. It does not use a linear 8-step chain. Cohen stresses that the defining feature is a time-bound uptime commitment under stochastic demand. The step chain fits roughly, but the real economics live in pre-positioning: inventory and people are placed before the failure. The chain therefore runs partly before "notice", and steps are often run in parallel rather than in order.
