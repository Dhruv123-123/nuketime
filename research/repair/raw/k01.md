## Bottom line

The best steelman for OEM-controlled repair is **not** "independents are dangerous". When regulators asked for that evidence, it mostly wasn't there. The strong version rests on four different things:
1. **Incentive alignment and risk transfer.** When the OEM is paid for uptime (power-by-the-hour, LTSAs), it has the incentive and the fleet-wide data to raise reliability. There's empirical support for this (Guajardo et al. 2012).
2. **Lifecycle pricing and investment recovery.** Aftermarket margins pay for cheaper or better primary goods (Chicago school, Carlton & Waldman). Forcing open repair can lead OEMs to raise upfront prices or cut durability (Jin, Yang & Zhu 2023, Management Science).
3. **Counterfeit and unapproved parts and provenance.** This is a real catastrophic tail in aviation (Partnair 394, 55 dead; AOG Technics 2023). But the fix in aviation is certification and traceability, not OEM exclusivity.
4. **Cybersecurity of remote access, not of repair as such.** NHTSA's 2023 position on the Massachusetts telematics law was about opening remote, bidirectional telematics, not about manuals or parts.

On safety, liability, security and reputation, the evidence is thin. The FTC found "scant evidence" for manufacturers' justifications. Manufacturers gave "no data" tying injuries to independent repair, apart from one 2011 phone incident, and "no empirical evidence" on liability or reputational harm. In the FDA's 2018 medical-device review, only 3 of 4,301 adverse-event reports had enough information to say servicing contributed to a death.

## Findings

### F1. FTC Nixing the Fix (May 2021): overall verdict on OEM justifications [regulator; hard record review]
- Source: FTC, "Nixing the Fix: An FTC Report to Congress on Repair Restrictions", May 2021. https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf
- Quote: "Based on a review of comments submitted and materials presented during the Workshop, there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions."
- The FTC issued a formal Call for Empirical Research and asked manufacturers for data in individual meetings. That makes this an absence of evidence despite asking, not an absence of looking.
- Caveat in favour of the steelman: Commissioners Wilson and Phillips noted "it is difficult to conduct a full weighing of the cost and benefits of repair restrictions without an analysis of the intellectual property rights of manufacturers". So the report deliberately left out the investment-recovery / IP leg.

### F2. FTC on safety: one anecdote, no data [regulator]
- "Other than citing to the mobile phone thermal runaway occurring in Australia in 2011, manufacturers provided no data to support their argument that injuries are tied to repairs performed by consumers or independent repair shops."
- The best OEM safety evidence on record:
  - PRBA cited a 2011 Australian Transport Safety Bureau case. A screw probably misplaced during a non-authorised repair punctured a battery and caused a thermal runaway on an aircraft.
  - Microsoft said off-spec power supplies caused "at least 12 serious overheating incidents".
  - The EDA/AEM 2019 dealer survey. Of dealers who had seen modified equipment come in, 45% said the modifications removed emissions controls, 54% said they disabled OEM safety features, and 62% said they could reduce reliability. The FTC called this "inapposite" because it concerns modifications, not repairs.
- Counter-evidence in the same report: Consumer Reports found consumers were more satisfied with independent shops than with factory service. Auto Care Association: 70-75% of consumers use independent repair after warranty.
- FTC counterpoint: OEMs can design for safe repair (removable rather than glued batteries). Withholding manuals and parts "may be exacerbating the very safety concerns they have raised."

### F3. FDA 2018 medical device servicing report (as quoted in FTC report) [regulator; data]
- Of 4,301 medical device reports (MDRs) the FDA identified as possibly involving third-party servicing, only 3 had enough information to conclude that servicing caused or contributed to a death. FDA: "the currently available objective evidence is not sufficient to conclude whether or not there is a widespread public health concern related to servicing of medical devices, including by third party servicers, that would justify imposing additional/different burdensome regulatory requirements."
- URL (FDA original; it returned 401 when I fetched it, so the quotes come via the FTC footnote): https://www.fda.gov/media/113431/download
- Read: medical devices are the domain where you'd most expect an OEM safety premium, and the FDA could not find one. Note the FDA also could not rule it out, because reporting is poor.

### F4. FTC on cybersecurity: no evidence either way; the risk is in the access layer [regulator]
- "The record contains no empirical evidence to suggest that independent repair shops are more or less likely than authorized repair shops to compromise or misuse customer data."
- The manufacturer argument at its best (CTA's Walter Alcorn, security consultant Earl Crane): the FTC's own IoT guidance makes manufacturers accountable for lifetime security and oversight of service providers. "Mandating design decisions runs in direct contradiction of policies that focus on manufacturer accountability." That is a coherent accountability argument: one accountable party.
- FTC rebuttal: advocates want diagnostics and firmware patches, not signing keys. A like-for-like parts swap is "unlikely to create a cybersecurity risk".

### F5. NHTSA vs Massachusetts Data Access Law (June 2023): the strongest regulator-backed security argument [regulator]
- In June 2023 NHTSA told automakers not to comply with Massachusetts' 2020 Question 1 telematics law. It argued the law conflicts with the federal Safety Act. Its reason was that requiring open, remote, bidirectional access to vehicle telematics could let malicious actors control vehicles at scale. NHTSA later (Aug 2023) clarified that compliance could be done through short-range Bluetooth-style access. **[letter fetch returned 403. Summary via Wikipedia "Right to repair" plus background; the August clarification is not re-verified this session.]** URL: https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/2023-06/NHTSA-letter-to-Manufacturers-Massachusetts-Data-Access-Law-June-13-2023_0.pdf
- Proof the threat is real: Miller and Valasek remotely took over the brakes, steering and acceleration of a 2014 Jeep Cherokee in 2015 (Wired, 21 Jul 2015, https://www.wired.com/2015/07/hackers-remotely-kill-jeep-highway/). This led to a recall of about 1.4M vehicles **[recall count not re-verified this session]**.
- Read: the valid security point is about remote fleet-scale access, not about the knowledge itself. Information can be opened while command channels stay gated. That separates C2/C3 (knowledge) from the security problem.

### F6. FTC on liability and reputation: assertions only [regulator]
- OEM argument (CTA, CompTIA, joint manufacturer comment): early press coverage of a device failure never separates OEM from third-party repair, so brand harm lands on the OEM. CompTIA: "If an OEM's brand and warranty are to stand behind repair work and assume product liability, it is only reasonable that the repair facility demonstrates competency and reliability."
- AHAM: authorised networks give traceability (repair records), technician background checks and drug screening. That helps liability and insurance claims.
- FTC: "Manufacturers provided no empirical evidence to support their concerns about reputational harm or potential liability resulting from faulty third party repairs."
- AHAM data point used against the "monopoly" framing: of 20,000 appliance servicers in member networks, over 87% are local independent firms affiliated with the brand. On this view the network is open to anyone who trains and certifies, so the gate is certification, not ownership.

### F7. Kodak (1992): the courts' reading of OEM efficiency defences [court]
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, 504 U.S. 451 (1992). https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/504/451
- Kodak's three justifications:
  1. "to promote interbrand equipment competition by allowing Kodak to stress the quality of its service"
  2. "to improve asset management by reducing Kodak's inventory costs"
  3. "to prevent ISOs from free riding on Kodak's capital investment in equipment, parts and service"
- The Court found triable fact issues on all three. On inventory: "the inventory of parts needed to repair Kodak machines turns only on breakdown rates, and those rates should be the same whether Kodak or ISOs perform the repair". On free-riding: Kodak's theory "has no support in our caselaw". Kodak held 80-95% of service on its machines.
- Steelman part: the Court itself recognises that tying can be procompetitive by "assuring quality control" and "reduc[ing] costs through economies of joint production and distribution". It condemns tying only where there is market power. Scalia's dissent (with O'Connor and Thomas): rational buyers price lifecycle service into the equipment purchase, so an OEM facing equipment competition can't extract monopoly rents. That is the Chicago "single systems market" view.
- Outcome: on remand a jury found against Kodak, about $72M after trebling, affirmed by the 9th Cir. 1997 **[not re-verified this session]**.

### F8. Chicago / systems-market economics: aftermarket profits as investment recovery [academic theory]
- Arguments (summarised in Wikipedia "Aftermarket (merchandise)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aftermarket_(merchandise), citing Carlton & Waldman and Waldman):
  - Primary and aftermarket form a single market.
  - Farsighted buyers discipline aftermarket prices.
  - Aftermarket margins cross-subsidise cheaper equipment (razor and blades).
  - Aftermarket profit funds better quality in the original equipment.
- Herbert Hovenkamp has argued lock-in theories have produced no defensible market-power findings and has suggested overruling Kodak (cited in the Wikipedia Kodak article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastman_Kodak_Co._v._Image_Technical_Services,_Inc.).
- Against: the same Wikipedia aftermarket article reports a modern consensus that aftermarket monopolisation can harm even informed consumers, partly because buyers are myopic. Borenstein, MacKie-Mason & Netz show aftermarket prices above cost even under equipment competition.
- Read: the investment-recovery argument is theoretically coherent but depends on buyers pricing in lifecycle costs, which the evidence says they mostly don't. FTC: customers "have no idea whether or not their devices are repairable".

### F9. Jin, Yang & Zhu (2023), Management Science: right to repair can backfire [academic, formal model]
- Chen Jin, Luyi Yang, Cungen Zhu, "Right to Repair: Pricing, Welfare, and Environmental Implications", Management Science 69(2):1017-1036, Feb 2023. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4401. HBR summary, Jan 2023: https://hbr.org/2023/01/research-the-unintended-consequences-of-right-to-repair-laws
- Finding (via Wikipedia's summary of the paper; full text not fetched): when repair opens up and repair costs fall, the OEM responds strategically. It may raise the new-product price, or shift toward cheaper, less durable or less repairable products. That can hurt consumers and the environment in some regimes. **[detailed conditions not re-verified this session]**
- Read: this is the most rigorous recent academic steelman. Its point is that making repair cheaper doesn't automatically improve the system once the OEM re-prices. That bears on C4: cutting the cost of diagnosis and sourcing may be captured or offset upstream.

### F10. Performance-based contracting raises reliability when the OEM owns the outcome [academic, empirical]
- Guajardo, Cohen, Kim & Netessine, "Impact of Performance-Based Contracting on Product Reliability: An Empirical Analysis", Management Science 58(5), 2012. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1474 (403 on fetch). Using Rolls-Royce aero-engine data, they found engines under performance-based (power-by-the-hour) contracts had markedly higher reliability (on the order of 25-40%) than engines under time-and-materials contracts. **[magnitude from memory, not re-verified this session]**
- Related: Kim, Cohen & Netessine (2007), "Performance contracting in after-sales service supply chains", Management Science. This is the theory of why paying for outcomes realigns the incentives that time-and-materials repair breaks.
- Read: this is the best efficiency case for OEM control, and it's a structural argument, not a knowledge argument. Once the OEM is paid for uptime, the "repair is built to be inefficient" incentive flips. It supports C7's diagnosis (time-and-materials incentives are perverse). But it suggests the fix might be OEM-led outcome contracts, not unbundling.

### F11. Counterfeit and unapproved parts: the catastrophic tail [history; news]
- Partnair Flight 394 (8 Sep 1989, Convair 580): the tail failed in flight and all 55 aboard died. Three of the four fin-attachment bolts were counterfeit, wrongly heat-treated and able to bear only about 60% of the design breaking load. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnair_Flight_394
- AOG Technics (UK, 2023): a distributor supplied CFM56 engine parts with forged airworthiness certificates. These were found on engines at multiple airlines (including Southwest, United, American, Delta, TAP, Virgin Australia), forcing groundings and inspections. CFM reported on the order of ~100 engines affected. The UK Serious Fraud Office prosecuted the founder, Jose Zamora Yrala. **[details not re-verified this session; Wikipedia page fetch 404]**
- Read: this is the strongest real-world case for tight parts provenance. But in aviation the answer is certification and traceability (FAA/EASA Form 1 / 8130-3 release documents, Part 145 repair stations), not OEM exclusivity. Aviation already lets independent MROs and non-OEM PMA parts (e.g., HEICO) compete under regulator certification **[not re-verified this session]**. So the safety function can be separated from OEM ownership. This supports C6 and undercuts C5: an AI-made part is worthless without a certified pedigree.

### F12. Industry concessions show the gate isn't binary [industry]
- Deere-AFBF MOU (Jan 2023): farmers and independents may repair, but they can't disclose trade secrets or override emissions controls (Wikipedia "Right to repair").
- Apple: Independent Repair Provider program (2019), Self Service Repair (2021/22). Small shops avoided the IRP over its legal burdens, and serial-number "parts pairing" stayed in place.
- Read: OEMs' concessions keep two lines. One is emissions/regulatory compliance, the only liability where the OEM is legally on the hook under the Clean Air Act. The other is anti-theft/identity of components. That marks the points where OEMs have a real legal stake, not just a commercial one.

### F13. Warranty: the law already rejects the "void if serviced elsewhere" defence [regulator]
- The Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act §102(c) forbids conditioning a warranty on using branded parts or service unless those are provided free. In April 2018 the FTC warned six firms (Hyundai, Asus, HTC, Microsoft, Sony, Nintendo) that their warranty-sticker terms may be illegal (Verge, 10 Apr 2018, via Wikipedia). The FTC also notes a warrantor may deny a claim it can show was caused by the third-party repair.
- Read: the warranty argument survives only in its narrow form. The OEM shouldn't pay for damage someone else caused, and that is already allowed.

## Synthesis for the founder

- **Where OEM control is efficient:**
  - **(a)** Where the OEM carries the outcome risk (uptime contracts). Here it's the efficient owner of fleet data and design feedback.
  - **(b)** Where there's a regulatory liability the OEM can't shed (emissions, airworthiness type certificates, vehicle safety standards).
  - **(c)** Where remote command access creates fleet-scale cyber risk.
  - **(d)** Where parts provenance is safety-critical and certification infrastructure is weak.
- **Where it's not:** consumer electronics, appliances and most equipment repair. Here the claimed safety, liability, security and reputation effects lack data even after regulators asked twice (FTC 2019-21, FDA 2018).
- **Implication for C2/C3:** the knowledge gap is partly artificial (withheld manuals, diagnostics, parts). So AI-driven knowledge diffusion and legal mandates both chip away at it. But OEMs keep three non-knowledge levers: authentication/pairing (software locks), certification/legal liability, and parts supply plus re-pricing (Jin et al.). Shrinking the knowledge gap therefore doesn't remove OEM leverage, which moves to locks, certification and pricing.
