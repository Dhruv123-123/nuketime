# s04: Automotive repair: diagnosis share of shop time, comebacks, misdiagnosis and "parts cannon" rates, technician shortage, ADAS calibration costs, EV battery repair; data from AAA, NIASE, consumer surveys.

## Method and limits (read first)
- The shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn (200 calls, all agents) was exhausted before this agent ran. Every search returned "budget used up". All findings below come from WebFetch on publisher URLs I already knew or found through publishers' own on-site search pages (AAA newsroom, TechForce, CCC, FTC, Recurrent, BLS).
- Big gaps: I found no primary source on (a) diagnosis as a share of shop time, (b) comeback rates, or (c) misdiagnosis / "parts cannon" rates. Shop trade press (Ratchet+Wrench, Motor Age) site search returned nothing usable. These numbers are mostly practitioner folklore and shop-management-software marketing, not audited data. Treat any figure you see elsewhere (for example "comebacks 2-5% of ROs") as unverified until you have the source.
- Anything marked [unverified background] is my prior knowledge, not checked in this session.

## Findings

### F1. Technician shortage: supply meets about 42% of demand (TechForce Foundation, 9th ed., May 2026)
- URL: https://techforce.org/supplydemand/
- Average annual job openings for technicians 2025-2029: 241,842. Annual tech graduates (IPEDS AY2023-24): 101,743, up 7.6% year on year. Supply meets about 42% of demand, so 58% is unmet. They claim 1.2M new techs needed by 2029 and $7.42B a year in lost economic output.
- Supply gap by sector: automotive 29%, diesel 52%, collision 57%. Median wages: automotive $49,670, diesel $60,640, collision $51,680.
- Caveat: TechForce is an advocacy and scholarship nonprofit backed by the industry. "Openings" includes replacing people who leave. The gap partly reflects low pay and churn, not only a lack of knowledge.
- Kind: data, from an advocate.

### F2. BLS: about 826k auto service techs, median $50,620, 66,200 openings a year (2025-35)
- URL: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/automotive-service-technicians-and-mechanics.htm (page modified Sep 14 2026)
- 825,800 jobs (2025). Median pay $24.34/hr. Growth 5% over 2025-35. About 66,200 openings a year.
- The neutral government estimate of openings (66k a year for auto service techs alone) is far below TechForce's 242k, which spans all tech categories. Using BLS against about 100k graduates a year (all programs), the "shortage" looks less dramatic in auto service. It is concentrated in collision and diesel (consistent with F1's sector split).
- Kind: data, from a regulator/government.

### F3. Consumer distrust: 2 in 3 US drivers do not trust repair shops (AAA, Dec 2016)
- URL: https://newsroom.aaa.com/2016/12/u-s-drivers-leery-auto-repair-shops/
- 67% do not trust repair shops in general. 64% do have a specific shop they trust. About 75M drivers have not found a trusted shop.
- Reasons for distrust: recommending unnecessary services 76%, overcharging 73%, bad past experiences 63%, worry the work won't be done right 49%.
- Read: the main complaint is an information asymmetry between shop and customer (the customer cannot check the diagnosis). That is a knowledge gap, but it sits between owner and shop, not between independent shop and OEM. It supports C2 in a different form: a credence-good problem.
- Kind: data (consumer survey). Sample size is not disclosed on the page.

### F4. One in three drivers cannot pay an unexpected repair bill; a third delay repairs (AAA, Apr 2017)
- URL: https://newsroom.aaa.com/2017/04/one-three-u-s-drivers-cannot-pay-unexpected-car-repair-bill/
- 64M drivers would need to borrow. Average bill $500-600. One-third skip or delay recommended service.
- Read: the "notice" step and decision to repair are limited by money, not only knowledge. Repair is deferred.
- Kind: data (survey). Dated.

### F5. ADAS: after a crash, sensors and calibration can be up to 37.6% of repair cost (AAA, Dec 2023)
- URL: https://newsroom.aaa.com/2023/12/fixing-advanced-vehicle-systems-makes-up-over-one-third-of-repair-costs-following-a-crash/
- Minor front collision: $1,540 ADAS cost (13.2% of total). Minor rear collision: $684, which is 40.9% of a $1,698 total. Side mirror: the ADAS camera is 70.8% ($1,067) of the repair. Windshield: ADAS is 25.4% ($360).
- Component ranges over and above bodywork: front radar $500-1,300, front camera $600-800, mirror cameras $740-1,600, windshield camera $900-1,200, ultrasonic $300-1,000.
- Vehicles: 2023 F-150, Rogue and Camry.
- Read: the cost is hardware plus calibration (specialised targets, floor space, procedure). It is not mainly "knowledge". AI does not make a radar module cheaper, and it does not remove the need for a level floor and targets.
- Kind: study.

### F6. AAA 2018 baseline: a minor ADAS collision costs up to $5,300, 2.5 times a non-ADAS car
- URL: https://newsroom.aaa.com/2018/10/new-vehicle-technologies-double-repair-bills-minor-collisions/
- Up to $3,000 of extra ADAS cost. A windshield with a camera is about $1,500, up to 3 times a plain one. Calibration "requires special training, tools and information". AAA tells consumers to ask for proof that the calibration was done.
- Read: inspection and verification (proof of calibration) is already a consumer-facing need, which supports C6.
- Kind: study.

### F7. CCC Crash Course 2025 (2024 data): calibrations on about 30% of repairable estimates, scans on 86%, total losses at a record 22.9%
- URL: https://www.cccis.com/crash-course
- Calibrations on 29.9% of repairable DRP appraisals (Q4 2024). 56.3% of calibrations appear only on supplements, meaning they were found after teardown rather than at first estimate. Average calibration cost per claim with a diagnostic operation is about $547 (hybrid $627, ICE $539, EV $415).
- Scans on 86.3% of repairable DRP appraisals. About 90% of vehicles 3 years old or newer get a scan. 91.7% of scans are on the initial estimate.
- Labor hours per claim about 27.3 (2024), up from 23.0 in 2013. Labor rates up 4.5% (2024) after 7.5% (2023).
- Total loss share hit a record 22.9% (non-comprehensive). Over 70% of those total losses were on vehicles 7 or more years old.
- EV repairs cost about $1,030 more than ICE. EV labor rates are about 30% higher. EV repair cycle time is about 37.5 days, the longest of any powertrain. DRP repair days are still 4-5 days above 2020.
- Read: (1) Diagnosis is already heavily tooled and routinised in collision (scans are near universal), yet costs keep rising. The cost drivers are labor hours, labor rates, parts and calibration. (2) More than half of calibrations surface as supplements. That points to a gap in up-front diagnosis (damage that is hidden until disassembly), which is a real target for better triage, including AI or imaging. (3) Cycle time is where the time goes, and much of it is waiting for parts, labor or approvals, not working out what is wrong.
- Kind: data (industry data vendor).

### F8. EV battery replacement is rare in modern EVs: 0.3% for 2022 and later, under 4% overall excluding recalls (Recurrent, Nov 2025)
- URL: https://www.recurrentauto.com/research/how-long-do-ev-batteries-last
- Based on more than 30,000 cars (a non-representative sample). First-generation EVs about 8.5%, second-generation about 2%, 2022 and later 0.3%. Warranties cover packs that fall below 70% capacity over 8-10 years.
- Read: battery failure is not common. The EV repair problem is collision damage to the pack and insurers totalling the car, not batteries wearing out. [unverified background: a Reuters report of Mar 2023 said insurers often total EVs with minor pack damage because OEMs don't share cell-level diagnostic data, and Thatcham and others pushed for pack repairability. I could not fetch Reuters to confirm.]
- Kind: data.

### F9. FTC "Nixing the Fix" (May 2021): "scant evidence" for manufacturers' repair restrictions; autos are the model sector
- URL: https://www.ftc.gov/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions (PDF: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf)
- Quote: "there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions."
- The 2014 national auto MOU, signed after Massachusetts's 2013 law, had OEMs sell dealers' diagnostic and repair information to owners and independents. It created "a broad, if not complete, right to repair in the automotive industry". Every OEM except Tesla signed (as of the 2019 workshop). In return, aftermarket groups agreed not to back new state bills.
- Telematics is the new chokepoint. Per LKQ: "Vehicle manufacturers retain exclusive insight in vehicle operations and diagnostics systems". OEMs can steer the customer after a crash. The Auto Care Association said the MOU does not cover telematics. Massachusetts voters in Nov 2020 extended the law to telematics (open data platform from MY2022).
- The FTC on safety: "The automotive sector demonstrates that consumers and independent repair shops are able to repair cars every day even though cars are ... complex machines".
- Read: in autos, the formal service-information monopoly was mostly broken by law and the MOU (2013-14). Yet independents still face friction: they pay subscriptions for OEM info and tools, need security gateway access to clear codes or program modules, and have no telematics data. The monopoly has moved from manuals to data access, cryptographic authorisation and real-time vehicle data. That is a legal and access gate, not a knowledge gate. AI cannot get past it. [unverified background: Massachusetts's 2020 telematics law has been stuck in Alliance for Automotive Innovation v. Campbell litigation and enforcement delays for years. Automakers and aftermarket groups signed a further voluntary commitment in Aug 2023. Federal REPAIR Act bills keep being reintroduced.]
- Kind: regulator.

### F10. ASE (NIASE): industry-founded in 1972, recertification every 5 years
- URL: https://www.ase.com/about-ase
- Certification is voluntary and does not license the person to work on cars. No US state requires a licence for general auto technicians. [unverified background: about 250,000 ASE-certified professionals; ASE launched xEV and ADAS-related tests in the 2020s.]
- Read: autos have weak inspection and certification compared with aviation. There is generally no mandatory sign-off after a repair, apart from state safety and emissions inspections and OEM position statements on calibration. The C6 gap here is almost the reverse of aviation: too little verification rather than a certification bottleneck. Proof of ADAS calibration is a specific hole, flagged by AAA (F6).
- Kind: data/practitioner.

### F11. AAA roadside 2024: 27M calls, ~13M tows, ~7M battery; EV calls ~215k (0.8%)
- URL: https://newsroom.aaa.com/2025/04/aaa-urges-drivers-to-stay-proactive-on-auto-repair-and-maintenance/
- Read: a big share of real-world failures (12V batteries, flat tyres, tows) are low-knowledge, high-logistics events. The cost is in moving the car or the person, not in diagnosis.
- Kind: data.

### F12. Apprenticeship growth (AAA/NAPA mentorship, Dec 2024)
- URL: https://newsroom.aaa.com/2024/12/aaa-and-napa-mentorship-program-auto-repair-technicians/
- Active apprentices up 22% on the previous decade (per DOL). The 18-24 month program targets 4 ASE certifications.
- Read: the industry treats skills as something you gain through supervised hands-on time, not something you look up. That counts against C3.
- Kind: practitioner.

## What I could NOT verify (do not cite without sources)
- Diagnosis share of shop hours. Practitioners often say diagnostic time is underbilled or unbilled, flat-rate pay punishes diagnosis, and "parts cannon" behaviour comes from flat-rate incentives and customer resistance to diagnostic fees. That makes it an incentive problem, not only a knowledge one. No hard number was found.
- Comeback rates. Shop-software vendors cite 1-5% of repair orders. Not verified.
- Misdiagnosis rates. No credible auto study found. The rate is plausible but unmeasured. The repair-order data needed to measure it sits with shop-management software (Tekmetric, Shopmonkey, Mitchell1, CCC). That itself is a data asymmetry.

## Synthesis

Where the money and time go:
- Collision: labor hours (27/claim), labor rates (rising 4.5-7.5% a year), parts (sensor-laden assemblies), and calibration (about $550/claim when present, about 30% of repairable claims). Cycle time is weeks (EVs 37.5 days). Diagnosis itself (scans) is near universal and cheap per claim. The costly failure is late discovery: 56% of calibrations appear on supplements.
- Mechanical: unverified, but the customer-side pain is trust/upselling (76%) and affordability (1 in 3 cannot pay). The labor constraint is real but concentrated in diesel and collision.
- EV: pack failures are rare. Collision-driven pack total losses and long cycle times are the issue.

Real bottleneck:
- (1) Skilled hands and bays: a 29-57% supply gap by sector, a pay/churn problem.
- (2) Access gates: security gateways, OEM subscriptions, telematics data monopoly. These are legal and cryptographic, not knowledge.
- (3) Verification and trust: the customer cannot check the diagnosis, and calibration needs proof.
- (4) Capital equipment: calibration targets and space, EV high-voltage tooling.
- Knowledge (procedures and wiring) is already largely purchasable since 2014 via the MOU, ALLDATA, Mitchell1 and Identifix. AI makes it cheaper and faster to use, but that layer was not the main cost.

Stance on claims:
- C1 mixed. The chain fits collision broadly. But estimate, teardown and supplement loops mean diagnosis repeats during repair, so the chain is iterative, not linear. Insurer authorisation and financing are steps the chain leaves out.
- C2 mixed. The OEM info monopoly was largely broken in autos in 2013-14. What remains is data and access control (telematics, gateways) plus owner-versus-shop credence asymmetry. That supports a reframed C2 (asymmetry and access), not "independents lack knowledge".
- C3 contradicts. Knowledge was already available. The binding gaps are hands, tools, access rights and incentives. Apprenticeships of 18-24 months show the skill is tacit.
- C4 contradicts/mixed. Diagnosis plus info is a modest slice of the bill. Parts and calibration are hardware. An order-of-magnitude drop across the four steps would not cut total cost by anything like 10x. Gains are possible in triage (supplements), parts logistics and cycle time.
- C5 silent/contradicts. Not relevant to autos in this evidence, which is driven by OEM sensor parts with calibration and security-pairing (VIN-coded modules). Printed parts don't solve programming and pairing.
- C6 supports. Calibration proof is a hole. Autos have weak post-repair verification, so the opportunity is "inspection-as-a-service" for ADAS, EV packs and post-repair safety.
- C7 mixed. Incentives (flat rate, DRP insurer steering, OEM telematics steering) do produce inefficiency. But the auto sector is also the FTC's model of a working open-repair market, so "built to be inefficient" overstates it.

Company shapes:
- ADAS calibration verification/attestation (records, proof, mobile calibration). AAA and CCC data show demand and a gap in verification.
- Pre-teardown damage triage (imaging plus AI) to cut the 56% of calibrations found on supplements and shorten cycle time.
- EV pack collision diagnostics and repair (cell or module level) to avoid total losses. Note: modern pack wear-out failures are rare (0.3%).
- Tools for technician productivity and training in collision and diesel, where the gaps are 52-57%. AI copilots help, but the bottleneck is hands.
