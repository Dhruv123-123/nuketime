## Hard data (verified this session)

### 1. Otis: service is 65% of sales and 91% of segment profit, at a ~25% margin
- Source: Otis Worldwide 10-K for FY2025, filed 2026-02-05. https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1781335/000178133526000011/otis-20251231.htm
- New Equipment: net sales $4,989m (2024: $5,367m), operating profit $240m, **4.8% margin** (2024: 6.1%).
- Service: net sales $9,442m (2024: $8,894m), operating profit $2,374m, **25.1% margin** (2024: 24.6%).
- Quote: the segments "contributed 35% and 65% of our net sales, and 9% and 91% of our segment operating profit, respectively."
- Maintenance & Repair grew +4% organically in 2025 (+6% in 2024). Modernization grew +9% (+12% in 2024). Modernization contract revenue was about $1.9bn.
- Maintenance portfolio is "approximately 2.5 million units globally", covering Otis and non-Otis equipment. About **1.1 million units are connected** (Otis ONE IoT).
- Reading: this is the razor-and-blade model in its plainest form. New equipment is sold close to break-even to win a service annuity that runs for decades. Profit lives in the repair and maintenance stage, not in making the product.

### 2. Otis: independents hold about 50% of units, but a smaller share of value
- Same 10-K, competition section. Independent service providers are "significant competitors in most of our local geographies" and "have an aggregate portfolio of about 50% of service units". They hold a smaller share of the service business by value.
- Reading: this cuts against a pure "OEM monopoly" story. Half the installed base is already maintained by independents. The OEMs keep the high-value end: high-rise buildings, complex controllers, modernization and new-equipment conversions. That points to segmentation by complexity and proprietary access, not a total lockout.

### 3. Otis still faces European cartel follow-on damages
- Same 10-K: "We are subject to ongoing claims related to alleged violations of anti-collusion laws in certain European countries", meaning civil claims for overcharges on elevators and escalators. Otis booked litigation settlement costs of $21m in 2025 and $18m in 2024 (Corporate and Unallocated; not all of it is cartel-related).

### 4. EU 2007 elevator cartel: EUR 992m fine covering installation AND maintenance
- Source: European Commission press release IP/07/209, 21 Feb 2007. https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/IP_07_209
- Total fines of EUR 992,312,200, then the Commission's largest cartel fine. ThyssenKrupp paid EUR 479.7m (including a 50% increase as a repeat offender), Otis EUR 224.9m, Schindler EUR 143.7m, KONE EUR 142.1m and Mitsubishi Elevator Europe EUR 1.8m.
- The cartels covered Belgium, Germany, Luxembourg and the Netherlands from at least 1995 to 2004. The firms rigged bids, fixed prices, allocated projects and shared markets on hospitals, stations and shopping centres.
- Commissioner Kroes said the damage "covered not only the initial supply but also the subsequent maintenance of lifts and escalators." The press release adds that maintenance is often performed by the installer, so the cartel's effects could last **20-50 years**.
- Reading: this is the strongest documented evidence for C7, that the inefficiency is structural and deliberate. Note that the mechanism was collusion on price and market allocation, not a knowledge gap.

### 5. Austria 2008 cartel and CJEU "umbrella pricing" ruling (Kone C-557/12, 5 June 2014)
- Source: CJEU Press Release No 79/14. https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2014-06/cp140079en.pdf
- In 2008 the Austrian authorities fined Kone, Otis, Schindler and others for a cartel on the Austrian market. ÖBB-Infrastruktur bought from suppliers outside the cartel and claimed EUR 1,839,239.74 because those suppliers priced up under the cartel's "umbrella".
- The CJEU held that cartel members can be liable for umbrella pricing by non-members, even without any contractual link to the victim.
- Reading: the cartel raised the market price for everyone, independents included. Independents are not automatically the cheap, competitive fringe; they can price up to the OEM level.

### 6. KONE: service base of 1.8m units, over 40% connected, and service is now its largest business
- Source: KONE Financial Statement Bulletin 2025, 5 Feb 2026. https://assets.kone.com/adobe/assets/urn:aaid:aem:f19e141e-fb15-4010-97ae-cdb42d175e05/renditions/original/as/KONE_Financial%20Statement%20Bulletin_2025.pdf
- Service sales were EUR 4,753.6m in 2025 (+7.6% at comparable FX), out of total sales of EUR 11,245.2m. Group adjusted EBIT margin was 12.2%.
- The service base was about 1.8 million units at end-2025, up from well over 1.7m. Growth came "in particular" from "conversions of new equipment deliveries to the service base" plus bolt-on acquisitions of service companies, mostly in Europe (EUR 117.7m spent on acquisitions).
- Quote: "the balance of service contracts that were won from or lost to competition was slightly negative outside China." In other words, KONE lost slightly more contracts to rivals, including independents, than it won.
- "In the Service and Modernization markets, the pricing environment was more favorable", compared with intense price competition in new equipment in China.
- Over 40% of the service base is connected. KONE rolled out "Dynamic Maintenance Planning to increase field productivity" in almost 30 countries in 2025.
- Risk section: "Labor availability constraints may also impact ... performance of maintenance and repair services."
- Reading: the OEMs are already using connectivity and AI-style planning to cut their own cost to serve. They capture the efficiency gain inside their own margin rather than passing it on, which is consistent with KONE's "favorable" service pricing. The binding constraint they name is labour (technicians), not knowledge.

### 7. KONE + TK Elevator merger (announced April 2026): 3.2m serviced units and ~EUR 700m synergies, mostly from field density
- Source: "KONE and TKE – Rise together" investor presentation, 29 April 2026. https://assets.kone.com/adobe/assets/urn:aaid:aem:ba4f16b0-4d7f-4fca-bc51-b6c982b121a7/renditions/original/as/KONEandTKE_RiseTogether_Presentation.pdf
- TKE: sales EUR 9.2bn (FY Sep-2025), adjusted EBIT 14.8% (up from 11.1% in FY2022), about 65% of sales from service and modernization, more than 1.4m units under maintenance, about 25k service technicians.
- Combined: sales EUR 20.5bn, adjusted EBIT 13.4%, **3.2m serviced units**, more than 100k employees. Service is about 45% of combined sales, modernization about 19-21%, new equipment about 35%.
- Synergies of about **EUR 700m** a year (3-4% of combined sales), with "productivity from higher density of combined service networks" listed first. The deck adds "Improved response times, efficiency and reliability ... meaningful uplift in Lifts in Service / Technician."
- Deal terms: EUR 5bn cash plus up to 270m new KONE B shares (about EUR 15.2bn), for an enterprise value of about EUR 29.4bn for TKE. The deal still needs regulatory approval within 12-18 months, with the earliest close in Q2 2027.
- The deck sets an "ambition of adj. EBIT margin substantially beyond current 16% target."
- Reading: the industry's own merger case says route density (units per technician per square km) is the main lever, not diagnosis or knowledge. A big-four to big-three consolidation is pending EU and US merger review. That is a live 2026-27 event the founder should track, because remedies such as divestitures of service portfolios could release units to independents.

### 10. Labour and licensing: union-controlled skilled labour is a cost driver
- Source: Wikipedia, International Union of Elevator Constructors (citing a 2018 Department of Labor filing). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Elevator_Constructors
- About 28,620 members. Elevator work is cited as the highest-paid US trade (median $51.24/hr in 2025). The IUEC lobbied for New York State to license all elevator mechanics.
- Reading: in the US, the scarce and expensive layer is licensed mechanic hours plus mandated inspections, not information.

## Background items, not verified this session (confirm before citing)
- **[UNVERIFIED] Japan, Toshiba Elevator Technos (Osaka High Court, 1993).** This is a classic aftermarket tying case. An OEM-affiliated maintainer refused to supply spare parts alone, or delayed them, unless the building owner also bought its repair labour. The court found this an unfair trade practice. It is the canonical "parts plus service tying" precedent in elevators.
- **[UNVERIFIED] ASME A17.1 / CSA B44, Section 8.6 (Maintenance Control Program), revised around the 2007 edition onward.** These editions require that maintenance records, MCP documentation and any unique product-specific procedures or tools needed for inspection and testing be available on site to the owner and the owner's maintainer. Independents have long complained that OEM proprietary "service tools" (handheld diagnostic units, sometimes time-limited or locked) are hard to get or degrade over time. US code language exists but enforcement varies by state.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** NYC Local Law (2018 onward) and New York State's elevator licensing law ("Adriana's Law", enacted around 2022-23, implementation delayed) increase licensing barriers. Supporters argue safety; critics argue they raise maintainer costs and lengthen outage times.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** The Center for Building in North America (Stephen Smith) reports and op-eds from 2024 argue that US elevators cost several times European ones. The drivers cited are a US-specific code (ASME rather than EN 81), cab-size rules, union labour, and fewer competing makers. Maintenance and outage times are also worse in the US. This supports C7 (structural inefficiency), but the cause is regulation and labour, not knowledge.

## Synthesis against the hypotheses
- **C1 (universal chain): mixed.** Elevators fit the chain, but the dominant activity is not reactive repair. It is contracted preventive maintenance: periodic visits, mandatory periodic inspection (in the US by the AHJ, in the EU by notified bodies) and modernization at 20-25 years. Much of the value sits in a recurring-visit model, not in incident chains.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed, leaning supports.** OEMs do hold proprietary controllers, service tools, documentation and parts, and EFESME explicitly asks for "fair access to data, tools, and documentation". But independents already maintain about 50% of units (Otis), and KONE is net-losing contracts to competition outside China. The moat is strongest on high-value and complex units. A further lever is collusion and contract structure (EU cartel fines, long contracts, conversion of new installs to service), which is not about knowledge at all.
- **C3 (AI erases the gap): contradicts/silent.** The gap is not only informational. It sits in locked controllers and service tools, access to parts and software, licensed labour and mandated inspection. AI does not unlock a proprietary handheld tool or a controller password. Meanwhile the OEMs are themselves deploying AI/IoT: Otis ONE on 1.1m units, KONE with over 40% connected plus Dynamic Maintenance Planning.
- **C4 (10x cost drop on diagnose, understand, source, deliver): contradicts.** The OEMs' own synergy case puts the main lever in field density and technician productivity. Of the roughly EUR 700m, the largest stated source is route density, not diagnosis. Labour availability is the stated risk. Mandated visit and inspection frequency sets a cost floor. A 10x drop in the information steps would shrink a minority of total cost.
- **C5 (bespoke parts factory solves obsolescence): mixed/silent.** Obsolete controllers are a real driver of modernization revenue (Otis modernization +9%, KONE +17.4%). But a replacement safety component (brakes, governors, door locks, safety gear) must be type-examined or certified (EN 81-20/50, ASME A17.1). A one-off AI-made part cannot legally go into service without that, so the binding step is certification, not fabrication.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports.** Mandatory periodic inspection is the anchor of the whole market and part of why incumbents' service economics are resilient. Remote monitoring is starting to be argued as a partial substitute for physical visits (KONE Dynamic Maintenance Planning adjusts visit frequency by data). That is exactly the "inspection evolving for AI" fight.
- **C7 (structurally built to be inefficient): supports.** New equipment is sold at a 4.8% margin to capture service at 25.1%. Multi-country cartels covered maintenance, with effects of 20-50 years. Consolidation continues (KONE+TKE). The pricing environment in service is "favorable". This is a market designed around capturing a locked installed base. The mechanism, though, is economic and legal structure, not ignorance.

## Where the money and time go
For the OEMs, the profit is almost entirely in the service annuity: Otis earns 91% of segment profit there. Cost is dominated by technician labour and travel (hence route-density synergies), mandated preventive-maintenance visits and inspections, and modernization of obsolete units. Diagnosis and parts information are a smaller slice. For owners, time is lost mostly to waiting for a licensed technician and parts, and to inspection scheduling (strongly so in NYC), not to figuring out what is wrong.

## Real bottleneck
Access, not knowledge. Specifically: locked controller software and service tools, parts supply controlled by the OEM, licensed and unionised technician supply, and the mandated inspection and certification regime. Underneath it all are contract lock-in and installed-base economics.

## Company shapes suggested
1. A **vendor-neutral connected-monitoring and diagnostics layer** for independents and building owners: a retrofit IoT box plus AI fault triage across OEM controllers. It would give the roughly 50% of units maintained by independents the same "connected" story Otis and KONE sell. Risk: OEM controller lock-outs.
2. **Owner-side audit and procurement software**: benchmark maintenance contracts, verify visits and log completeness against ASME 8.6 or EN 13015, and run re-tenders. It exploits the "favorable pricing" plus long-contract structure.
3. **Certified reverse-engineered and interoperable parts and controllers for obsolete units**, positioned as an alternative to full OEM modernization. The certification and type-examination pipeline is the moat (C5 plus C6).
4. **Remote and risk-based inspection tooling** for regulators and notified bodies. It would move inspection from fixed intervals to evidence based on monitoring data.
5. Watch the **KONE+TKE merger review (2026-27)**. Divested service portfolios or behavioural remedies, such as access to tools and parts, could create an opening for a roll-up of independents enabled by software.
