# v09: Section 1201 DMCA repair exemptions (Copyright Office triennial rulemakings 2015-2024): what did the record show about software locks blocking diagnosis and repair across vehicles, appliances, medical, industrial?

Researched 2026-10-20. Primary sources: the Register's Recommendations for the 6th (2015), 7th (2018), 8th (2021) and 9th (2024) triennial proceedings (full PDFs downloaded and text-searched), plus the 10th (2027-cycle) AdvaMed opposition filed Sept 2026, and secondary coverage. ## Timeline of what was granted / denied (hard facts from the rules)

| Cycle | Granted | Denied / limited |
|---|---|---|
| 2015 | Vehicle ECU software (incl. farm equipment) for diagnosis, repair, lawful modification; **delayed 12 months** to let EPA/DOT/FDA prepare | Telematics & entertainment ECUs excluded; "on behalf of owner" (third-party help) refused as possible anti-trafficking violation; EPA opposed, DOT had "substantial reservations" |
| 2018 | Vehicles expanded (maintenance; third-party "on behalf of" language allowed with caution); new: smartphones, home appliances, home systems | "Devices generally" refused on "sparse" record; avionics data (Air Informatics) refused because factual aircraft data is not copyrightable, so 1201 doesn't apply; Office says it will "generally decline" health/safety/environment concerns going forward |
| 2021 | All devices "primarily designed for use by consumers"; marine vessels added to vehicles; NEW medical devices & systems + related data files (manuals); "other laws" illegality limitation removed | Commercial/industrial devices (SCADA, building systems, PBX) refused – record too thin, possible negotiated licenses; no change on tools/third-party assistance (no authority over anti-trafficking) |
| 2024 | All repair exemptions renewed (medical renewed over MITA/Philips/ACI/AdvaMed opposition); NEW retail-level commercial food preparation equipment (Taylor soft-serve, espresso machines, ovens, refrigerators) | Broad "all commercial and industrial equipment" refused again: construction equipment, PLCs, enterprise IT each found insufficient; industrial-scale food equipment excluded; still no tool distribution |
| 2027 cycle (open) | Renewal petitions filed Aug 2026 (iFixit, TriMedx, Crothall, ECMS, etc.) | AdvaMed/MITA contest renewal citing cloud-hosted, tiered, licensed "Servicing Works" |

## Findings

### F1. Vehicles (2015): locks force owners to dealers or expensive authorized tools; even authorized tools can misdiagnose
2015 Rec. pp. 223-225 (Class 21). Proponents (EFF, USC IP clinic, Open Garages) said TPMs on ECUs meant owners "must take their cars to authorized repair shops, or purchase expensive manufacturer-authorized tools, to diagnose and repair their vehicles." Replacing hardware often requires reprogramming ECUs to "calibrate the new part" (e.g., new gear radius -> speedometer). Open Garages example: the **authorized diagnostic tool said a power window was operational**; only reverse engineering the CAN bus showed the window's comms unit was faulty. Ag: "farmers must often send their machines to far-away dealerships, or wait for a technician to travel to their farm... even for minor problems such as a blown fuse." Ag ECUs "completely shut the machine down if they detect aftermarket 'modules'."
Opponents: Deere, GM, Auto Alliance, Global Automakers. Deere argued owners hold only "an implied license for the life of the vehicle," and that individual owners "do not have the technical resources" to write safe software. Opponents also pointed to the 2014 national auto-repair MOU giving independents diagnostic access — proponents replied it covered only certain model years, not all OEMs, and excluded ag vehicles, motorcycles, RVs.
Kind: regulator record (practitioner testimony). Relevance: supports C2 (diagnosis gated by OEM tooling), but also shows the gate is legal + tooling, not knowledge alone.

### F2. Vehicles (2015): safety/environment regulators split the government; result was a 12-month delay
2015 Rec. pp. 2-3: "NTIA has endorsed broad exemptions..., while the Environmental Protection Agency is opposed, and the Department of Transportation expresses substantial reservations." Register recommended "a window of twelve months before exemptions that may implicate public safety and environmental concerns become effective." By 2018 the Office said it would "generally decline to consider health, safety, and environmental concerns" because those belong to other agencies (2018 Rec. p. 4-5).
Kind: regulator. Relevance: C6 — certification/safety regimes (EPA emissions, NHTSA, FDA) sit outside copyright and remain the binding constraint once the copyright lock is lifted.

### F3. Vehicles (2018): diagnostics migrating from OBD port to telematics; ~20% of consumers DIY
2018 Rec. p. 211-212: proponents said telematics "are becoming the nexus for diagnostic information"; some OEMs "migrated some or all data relating to vehicle function from physical OBD ports to telematics systems"; "BMW and Tesla—are considering removing or have already removed the OBD port entirely." Auto Care/CTA/iFixit: "approximately 20 percent of American consumers buy automotive parts and products to maintain and repair their own vehicles" (repeated by iFixit in 2024, Rec. p. 34). MEMA, which opposed in 2015, supported by 2018.
Number: ~20% DIY. Relevance: the locus of diagnostic data moves off-device to OEM-controlled channels — a structural trend (see F11 for medical).

### F4. Third-party help and tools: the structural hole that every cycle left open
2015: Register refused "on behalf of" the owner language (anti-trafficking risk). 2018: Farm Bureau filed six declarations that farmers "need local expert assistance"; proponents argued owner-only exemption is "effectively meaningless for those who lack the technical knowledge to access and manipulate increasingly complex embedded computer systems." 2021 Rec. p. 230: "the Librarian's authority under the statute to grant exemptions to the anti-circumvention provisions does not extend to the anti-trafficking provisions"; Register made no change. 2024: same for construction kits ("the Librarian's statutory authority does not extend to the provision of circumvention tools"). Kyle Wiens (iFixit) on 2024: inability to share/distribute tools is "a major limitation" (404 Media). The Office has recommended Congress legislate (2017 Section 1201 Report pp. 59-60, 88-95: permanent repair exemption + assistance).
Relevance: Strong support for C2 in a specific form — the law lets you personally circumvent but forbids the market from selling you the means. Knowledge (how to circumvent) is not the bottleneck; legal ability to productize and distribute it is. Contradicts C3 (AI can know how to bypass, but distributing an AI-generated bypass tool is still trafficking).

### F5. Consumer devices (2018-2021): appliances becoming connected; the Office found repair is fair use and the software has "no independent value"
2018 Rec.: Wiens: "LG, since the beginning of 2017, hasn't sold any appliances that aren't Wi-Fi connected"; inability to restore iPhones to full function after replacing batteries/home buttons (parts pairing). 2021 Rec. pp. 194-197: catalog of locked devices (washing machines, refrigerators, thermostats, garage openers, lightbulbs, power drills, CPAP, wheelchairs, hearing aids, glucose monitors); "shipments of smart home devices are increasing annually at a rate of up to 31%"; the underlying software is generally "the Linux operating system." Register: diagnosis, maintenance, repair of consumer devices "are likely to be fair uses where the purpose is to restore device functionality"; "repair supports—rather than displaces—the purpose of the embedded programs."
Counter-evidence: AHAM (appliance makers), 2024: "almost all repairs [of home appliances] are mechanical in nature . . . [t]here are no repair activities that would require the circumvention of installed TPMS." Joint Creators 2024: complaint that machines "display cryptic error codes and break a lot. But neither of those is a circumvention issue."
Relevance: for washing-machine-scale goods, the industry itself says the bottleneck is mechanical parts/labor, not software knowledge. Mixed for C2.

### F6. Medical devices (2021): hard numbers on OEM vs ISO cost, access denial, and contested safety data
2021 Rec. pp. 224-229.
- Price: IAMERS testimony "a price differential of $150-200 per hour for ISO repair compared to $600-800 per hour for OEM repair with a four-hour minimum" (roughly 3-5x).
- Ventilators: manufacturers charging ~$7,000 per technician for training/access (Business Insider, cited); biomed unable to repair Strategic National Stockpile ventilators (The Markup).
- Transtate: "basic" repair "documentation and software is not available on all medical systems," and some systems "require[] the purchase of thousands of dollars in hardware per engineer."
- Rural/regional hospitals "underserved by OEMs in terms of cost and timeliness of repair."
- OEM side: Philips gives ISOs setup/service materials "but restricts them from accessing more advanced unlicensed features and service functionalities"; "End of Service" equipment "is no longer safe and effective to operate."
- Safety claim: AdvaMed "aware of at least 281 adverse events . . . from 2012 to 2017 associated with third party servicing"; ACT cited FDA 2017 MDR data that 1% of adverse incidents attributable to ISOs amounted to "40 deaths, 294 serious injury."
- FDA rebuttal: FDA's 2018 servicing report found "the available evidence was insufficient to conclude whether or not there is a widespread public health concern relating to medical device servicing" and "did not justify imposing additional regulatory requirements on ISOs"; FDA 2021 said ISOs "may be well positioned to help identify and address security vulnerabilities."
- Competition: Register said the exemption "could help to address the broader competitive concerns" (Exec. Order 14036; FTC Nixing the Fix). Antitrust results mixed: Philips v. Summit Imaging (W.D. Wash. 2020) dismissed ISO monopolization counterclaims; Red Lion v. GE (E.D. Tex. 2017) jury found GE liable re anesthesia machine servicing.
Kind: regulator record + practitioner testimony. Relevance: strongest support for C2 in the record; numbers suggest 3-5x labor price premium where knowledge/tools are gated.

### F7. Medical devices: PIRG 2020 survey (cited in 2021 record)
222 biomedical repair professionals surveyed: 91.8% reported being denied access to service information for critical equipment (defibrillators, ventilators, anesthesia, imaging); nearly half (~49%) denied "critical repair information, parts or service keys" since March 2020; ~29% of those working with ventilators had devices they could not use for lack of parts or information.
Kind: campaigner survey (advocacy, self-selected sample). Source: https://uspirgedfund.org/reports/usp/hospital-repair-restrictions

### F8. 2024: the Taylor/McDonald's ice cream machine — the canonical "locked diagnosis" case
2024 Rec. pp. 149-152. Error codes are "unintuitive"; shipped manuals "often outdated and incomplete" because "[e]rror codes change with each firmware update"; other codes accessible only via a service manual for authorized technicians or a "TPM-locked on-device service menu" reachable only with a manufacturer-approved diagnostic tool or "an extended, undocumented combination of key presses" (a 16-press sequence that may have been changed by firmware updates). Register: "diagnosis of the soft-serve machine's error codes for purposes of repair can often only be done by accessing software on the machine that is protected by TPMs... and the threat of litigation from circumventing them inhibits users." Kytch v. McDonald's lawsuit noted; Kytch's product (a commercial circumvention tool) falls under anti-trafficking. Extended to retail espresso machines, ovens, refrigerators with password-locked functions. FTC and DOJ Antitrust Division filed in support.
Relevance: pure example of diagnosis-as-gated-knowledge (C2). But note the rule still forbids selling a Kytch-like product.

### F9. 2024: industrial equipment rejected — construction, PLCs, enterprise IT
2024 Rec. pp. 137-157. Proponents (Public Knowledge, iFixit) offered four "index examples": commercial food prep, construction equipment, PLCs, enterprise IT; claimed "significant, quantifiable financial harm due to equipment downtime." Register: "the record is too sparse," only "a handful of examples," "no other documentary evidence."
- Construction: diagnostic info "locked behind TPMs that can only be bypassed by using authorized, licensed, and branded tools"; two of three OEMs require proprietary kits. Rejected because the lock is on the separate diagnostic tool, not the machine — circumventing the machine alone may not enable diagnosis. AED/NAM: OEMs already facilitate self-repair.
- PLCs: passwords "held by a long-departed administrator" or vendor out of business render a PLC "unusable"; but NAM showed users can opt out of passwords and recovery methods exist (Siemens S7 reset guide). "Inconclusive."
- Enterprise IT: storage arrays need a cryptographic key the OEM "will not supply... under any circumstances" to non-employees; mainframe maintenance "authorized by passwords which are only made available to [the manufacturer's] employees"; for older models "no possible way to replace the CPU without circumvention." Opponents: OEMs support past End of Life; software separately priced.
- Industrial-scale food equipment explicitly excluded.
Relevance: For B2B/industrial (closest to the founder's airliner/industrial thesis) the regulator found the evidence base thin and the situation heterogeneous; contracts and service agreements between sophisticated parties are the norm. Mixed/contradicts C7 as a universal claim; supports C1-style heterogeneity concerns (no single "industry").

### F10. Aviation (2018): the 1201 lever doesn't even reach it — aircraft data is facts, not copyright
2018 Rec. pp. 314-318 (Class 11, Air Informatics). Aircraft systems data ("flight operations, fuel economy, digital flight data recorder, maintenance, fault and information security") is compressed, encrypted and transferred via PKI by avionics makers such as GE and Honeywell; proponents said OEMs "use TPMs and threats of legal action under § 1201" so operators "must pay manufacturers for access to the aircraft data to ensure compliance with FAA rules." The Acting Register denied the exemption because the data is factual and formatted to an industry standard (ARINC/SAE), hence not copyrightable — so 1201 doesn't apply and "it is not clear that section 1201 is facilitating those actions." NTIA: parties "can come to an agreement for access." Petitioner explicitly excluded modifying avionics software because it is "under the strict control and certification by the FAA and not allowed."
Relevance: For airliners, the knowledge gate is contractual/commercial (data licensing, OEM analytics fees) and FAA certification, not copyright. Supports C2 (OEMs monetize maintenance data), supports C6 (FAA certification decisive), and suggests the founder's airline narrative (OEM sends teams) is about certification/authority and data contracts more than know-how.

### F11. 2026-27: the lock is moving to the cloud and becoming a priced, tiered product
AdvaMed 2027-cycle opposition (Sept 2026), with declarations from Carestream, GE HealthCare, Hologic, Philips, Siemens Healthineers:
- Siemens: "For some imaging devices, most of the System Servicing/Diagnostic Software is hosted on the cloud, physically separated from the imaging device."
- Philips: documentation in Levels 0-3; Level 0 on request; Level 1 licensed to hospital biomeds under cooperative agreements with training + NDA; "Level 2 and Level 3 access is not available for license and is retained for Philips"; docs "nearly exclusively stored on a cloud server" (InCenter) behind multifactor auth.
- GE: service software classes A1 (public), A2/A3 (licensed, free in US), C (sold to entitled customers), M ("Highly Confidential," trade secret, internal only).
- Carestream: non-contract servicers pay "a yearly subscription on a per-person, per-unit basis" for credentials (SecureLink MFA).
- AdvaMed argues these "Servicing Works" have their own market, contradicting the Register's premise that repair software has "no independent value."
D.C. Circuit (MITA IV, Sept 1 2026) upheld the 2021/2024 exemption as not arbitrary or capricious, rejecting the separate-repair-software theory for lack of record evidence.
Relevance: This is the clearest evidence of a literal, priced "knowledge monopoly" (C2). It also cuts against C3: the knowledge is not missing from the world, it's behind credentialed remote servers; an AI cannot infer a Level 3 service key or a calibration routine that lives on the OEM's cloud. As locks move off-device, an on-device circumvention exemption reaches less of it.

### F12. What the record does NOT show (gaps)
- No study in any cycle quantifies how much repair cost or downtime is attributable to TPMs versus parts, labor, or logistics. The Office repeatedly called records "sparse" (2018 devices generally; 2021 industrial; 2024 industrial).
- No usage data on how many people actually used the exemptions. Without legal tools/services, uptake is likely small (inference).
- Opponents' safety data (281 adverse events 2012-17) vs FDA's "insufficient evidence" finding — no resolution.
- Exemptions renewed with little opposition for vehicles and consumer devices in 2024, suggesting OEMs see the copyright route as low-stakes; the real fights moved to state right-to-repair laws, FTC enforcement (e.g., FTC v. Deere, Jan 2025), and contracts.

## Reasoning vs hypotheses
- C1 (universal chain): Record implicitly follows diagnose -> access info/software -> parts -> repair -> recalibrate/verify, which fits. But it adds a step the chain misses: **re-authorization/calibration/pairing** after repair (ECU recalibration, parts pairing, medical "verify before returning to clinical use"). And the Office repeatedly found commercial/industrial categories too heterogeneous to treat uniformly. Mixed.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): Strongly supported in medical (tiered, licensed servicing works; 3-5x price), vehicles/ag (dealer-only tools), food equipment (hidden error codes), enterprise IT (keys withheld). But AHAM argues appliance repair is mostly mechanical; for aviation the gate is data contracts + FAA. And the "monopoly" is enforced by law (1201 anti-trafficking, contracts, trade secret), not just by ignorance.
- C3 (AI removes the gap): Contradicted. The binding constraints in the record are legal (anti-trafficking ban on tools/services, licenses, NDAs), cryptographic (keys, PKI, MFA, cloud-hosted service software) and regulatory (FDA QSR, EPA, FAA). AI can explain an error code it has seen, but cannot lawfully produce a tool to bypass a TPM for others, nor derive a secret key.
- C4 (10x cost drop in middle steps): Record contains one relevant price gap (ISO $150-200/hr vs OEM $600-800/hr, 4-hr minimum), consistent with 3-5x, not 10x, and that gap is labor price under monopoly, not diagnostic cost. No evidence for 10x across categories. Mixed/weakly contradicts.
- C5 (AI parts factory): The 3D printing exemption (2015, 2018) is the only adjacent record: 2015 version excluded printers producing goods "subject to legal or regulatory oversight or a related certification process"; proponents complained "any 3D printer can produce aircraft parts (subject to FAA approval) and medical devices (subject to FDA approval)," making the exemption nearly useless; 2018 removed that clause but the regulatory approval requirement itself remains. Stratasys: closed material systems mitigate fire/fume hazards. Contradicts "effectively solved" for regulated parts; certification, not fabrication, is the gate.
- C6 (inspection/certification essential): Supported throughout — EPA/DOT/FDA concerns (2015 12-month delay), FDA QSR arguments, FAA certification of avionics software, "verify device functioning before return to clinical use" as a servicing-software function.
- C7 (structurally built to be inefficient): Partly supported: Office itself said 1201 "was not intended to facilitate manufacturers' use of TPMs to facilitate product tying or to achieve a lock-in effect" (2017 Report p. 92, cited 2018), and the process requires product-by-product petitions every three years (proponents: "scattershot"). But "built to be inefficient" overstates: opponents' rationales (safety, cybersecurity, separately licensed software) were partly credited in B2B, and the Office found many industrial users have alternatives (service agreements, password recovery).

## Surprises
1. Aircraft maintenance data was found NOT copyrightable, so DMCA isn't the lever in aviation at all; the lock is contracts and certification.
2. The DMCA lets you circumvent but bans anyone selling you the tool or (arguably) doing it for you — a law that deliberately prevents a repair-tools market from forming. Nine years of rulemaking couldn't fix it; only Congress can.
3. Medical OEMs now openly describe a tiered, priced market for repair knowledge (Philips Levels 0-3, GE Class M trade secret, Carestream per-person-per-unit subscriptions), with service software moving to OEM clouds.
4. Industrial/B2B repair, the category closest to a deeptech startup, is exactly where the regulator has twice (2021, 2024) found the evidence too thin.
5. Even OEM-authorized diagnostic tools misdiagnose (2015 power-window example).
6. Appliance makers claim almost all appliance repair is mechanical, not software-gated.
