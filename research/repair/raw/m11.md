# m11: Naval ship maintenance: GAO findings on Navy depot maintenance delays (days of maintenance delay, causes: parts, unplanned work, workforce, planning); submarine idle time

Research date: 2026-10-20. Method note: the shared WebSearch budget for this run was used up before this agent started, so no web searches were run. All findings below come from GAO product pages and full-report PDFs fetched directly from gao.gov (known report numbers, plus GAO's military readiness topic page, which surfaced the 2026 reports). Full-text PDFs were converted locally with pdftotext. Everything is a primary source (GAO, the US federal audit agency), so it is hard data unless marked otherwise.

## Findings

### F1. Fleet-wide scale: 33,700 extra days, about 75% late (FY2014-2019)
- Navy ships spent more than 33,700 more days in maintenance than planned in FY2014-2019. About 75% of maintenance periods missed their scheduled completion date. More than half of the FY2019 delays exceeded 90 days.
- GAO's stated causes: insufficient shipyard capacity, a shortage of skilled personnel, and maintenance deferred during deployments.
- The Navy estimates 20 years to improve shipyard infrastructure and 4 years to restore crew levels.
- Source: GAO-20-257T, "Navy Maintenance: Persistent and Substantial Ship and Submarine Maintenance Delays Hinder Efforts to Rebuild Readiness" (testimony, Dec 2019). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-257t

### F2. Attack submarines, FY2008-2018: 10,363 idle or delay days, $1.5B
- Attack submarines lost 10,363 days to idle time and maintenance delay in FY2008-2018, caused by delays getting into and out of shipyards.
- About $1.5B (FY2018 dollars) was spent supporting submarines that provided no operational capability.
- USS Boise: its maintenance was scheduled for 2013. By June 2016 it had lost its dive certification and could not operate, and it then sat idle pierside for more than 2 years.
- Causes: public shipyards ran above capacity for years, workforce shortages, and poor allocation of work between public and private yards (there was no business case analysis).
- GAO 2017 and 2018 also list parts shortages, poor facilities, changes in planned work, and weather. GAO recommended an analysis of spare parts shortages.
- Source: GAO-19-229, "Navy Readiness: Actions Needed to Address Costly Maintenance Delays Facing the Attack Submarine Fleet" (Nov 2018). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-229 ; PDF https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-19-229.pdf

### F3. Carriers and submarines at public shipyards, FY2015-2019: causes quantified
- 38 of 51 maintenance periods (75%) finished late, for 7,424 days of delay in total. The average delay was 113 days for carriers and 225 days for submarines.
- Only 5 of 33 submarine periods (15%) were on time.
- GAO identified 10 causal factors. Unplanned work and workforce were the main two:
  - Navy letters named them as causes in 30 of 31 periods that had identifiable causes.
  - Unplanned work contributed to at least 4,100 days of delay and was cited in 28 of 31 periods. "New work" (work not in the final work package) accounted for more than 3,800 of those days.
  - Workforce contributed to more than 4,000 days, of which capacity (not enough workers) was more than 2,500 days.
- For submarines, the survey ranked parts as the #2 cause, after unplanned work and before workforce.
- Planning:
  - Estimates are built up to 2.5 years before work starts.
  - Work packages carry a 5-10% reserve for new work, which actual new work often exceeds.
  - Carrier work in FY2011-2014 needed 17% more work than estimated.
  - The Navy underestimated needed workforce resource days by 36%.
  - For 7 consecutive years the Navy overspent its ship maintenance budget by $5.7B in total, about 50% of it from shipyard shortfalls. A 2016 Navy report attributed this to "material weaknesses in planning."
- Overtime in some shops averaged 25-32%, with peaks of 45%.
- One submarine at Puget Sound was delayed 193 days by new work on its propeller shaft.
- Survey quote (practitioner): "unplanned work can be the result of insufficient supply system support, technical documentation, planning, overseeing, executing modernization, and the effects of deferred maintenance."
- Source: GAO-20-588, "Navy Shipyards: Actions Needed to Address the Main Factors Causing Maintenance Delays for Aircraft Carriers and Submarines" (Aug 2020). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-588

### F4. Attack submarines, FY2016-2025 (newest data): idle time cut, depot delays not
- More than 15,000 operational days were lost over 10 years to depot maintenance delay plus active idle time, at an estimated $3.4B.
- Depot maintenance delay was 3,040 days in FY2016-2020 and 3,454 days in FY2021-2025, so it is getting worse.
- Average depot period length rose from 744 to 809 days.
- Only about 11% of attack submarine depot maintenance at the public shipyards finished on time in FY2016-2025.
- Active idle time (a boat lost its certification and is waiting for a shipyard slot) totalled 8,906 days. It peaked at 1,518 days in FY2022 and fell to 166 days in FY2025. This was the success of the Navy's 2018 plan: hiring, training, and moving work to private yards.
- Inactive idle time (boats awaiting reactor defuel in a dry dock) totalled 3,287 days and peaked at 1,260 days in FY2025.
  - Projection: more than 14,000 days and $3.1B for 15 boats in FY2026-2030 unless mitigated.
  - USS Pasadena waited about 4 years (1,414 days).
- Table 1 totals: 18,687 days and about $4.17B.
  - Daily operating cost is about $219,700 for a Los Angeles-class boat and $237,080 for a Virginia-class boat.
  - Breakdown: active idle 8,906 days ($1.98B), inactive idle 3,287 days ($722M), depot delay 6,494 days ($1.46B).
- The Navy had said its actions would eliminate idle time and the backlog by the end of FY2023. It did not.
- CNO goal: 80% operational availability for attack submarines by 2027.
- Fleet: 44 attack submarines in FY2025.
- New construction: Virginia-class is built at about 1 per year against a goal of 2. Both 2025 deliveries were more than 3 years late, driven by "late material deliveries from suppliers and limited availability of experienced workers."
- The binding constraint for inactive idle time is dry-dock capacity to defuel reactors. GAO recommends evaluating defueling outside dry docks and reduced crewing.
- Source: GAO-26-109256, "Navy Readiness: Actions Needed to Address Costly Attack Submarine Maintenance Challenges" (Aug 27, 2026; public version of a July 2026 classified report). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109256 ; PDF https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-109256.pdf

### F5. Intermediate (pierside) maintenance, FY2015-2020: parts shortages and cannibalization
- 191 of 414 submarine intermediate maintenance periods (46%) were late, for 2,525 days of delay. The Navy did not even collect this data for surface ships or carriers. Average high-priority maintenance spend was $2.1B per year.
- Four main challenges: crew and shore workforce shortages, high operational tempo, training limits, and parts and material shortages.
- Parts:
  - All 16 crews interviewed reported trouble getting parts. Electrical safety equipment was backordered up to 2 years.
  - 10 of 16 crews cannibalized parts from other ships.
  - 11 of 16 struggled to source parts for old or obsolete systems.
  - 6 of 7 submarine crews could not get oxygen-generation parts.
  - Parts that arrived were sometimes wrong, expired, or did not fit.
  - Crews used "duct-tape" and "bubble-gum" workarounds.
  - 14 of 18 shore organizations confirmed the problems.
- Submarine "departures from specification" (approved deviations from procedure) averaged 10% of jobs and rose from 9% (FY2015) to 15% (FY2020).
- Crew shortfalls rose from 8% to 15% by September 2020.
- Intermediate maintenance includes "the emergency manufacture of unavailable parts." The Navy already does bespoke part fabrication at its intermediate maintenance facilities.
- Source: GAO-22-104510, "Navy Ship Maintenance: Actions Needed to Monitor and Address the Performance of Intermediate Maintenance Periods" (Nov 2021). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-104510

### F6. Sailor-led maintenance (2024): the knowledge and training gap is real, but parts and people dominate
- Survey of executive officers on 232 ships (91% response):
  - 73% said sailor-led maintenance was moderately to extremely difficult with the spare parts on board; 88% said the same for repairs.
  - 64% said it was difficult with the formal training sailors had received.
  - 63% said repairs underway were difficult with the sailors assigned.
- The Navy missed its goal of supplying 65% of requested items from onboard spares in every year from FY2017 to FY2022. Allowance lists (COSAL) are out of date, and the demand models may be wrong.
- One submarine had 222 items removed and given to 17 other submarines in a single maintenance cycle, plus 46 more parts earmarked for other boats.
- Sailors reverse-engineer parts (for example, an electric motor rebuilt in a carrier's shop), buy door handles at hardware stores, and swap motors between systems.
- Knowledge: initial skills training "usually does not cover troubleshooting." Sailors lack hands-on training.
- Software: practitioner quotes cite "outdated software for maintenance logistics" and a "lack of new system technical documentation."
- Maintenance cards carry a formula that adds 30% to time estimates for set-up and tear-down.
- Source: GAO-24-106525, "Navy Readiness: Actions Needed to Address Ship Maintenance Challenges" (Sept 2024). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106525

### F7. Sustainment metrics have worsened fleet-wide, FY2011-2021
- For the 10 ship classes reviewed (151 ships), per ship:
  - Cannibalizations rose by 6 (FY2015-2021) and increased every year except 2017.
  - Category 3 and 4 casualty reports rose by 15.
  - Days of maintenance delay rose by 14. Examples: San Antonio-class LPD +33, DDG-51 +20.
- Steaming hours fell.
- Operating and support (O&S) costs rose $2.5B to $17.1B (+17%), including maintenance up $1.2B to $6.2B (+24%).
- 9 of 10 program offices reported spare parts challenges. Drivers named: "shortages of trained maintenance personnel and diminished manufacturing sources for parts."
- Acquisition-born sustainment problems: GAO found 150 class-wide design or reliability problems in ships delivered over 10 years. Fixing just the 30% that had cost data would take $4.2B (from GAO-20-2, March 2020).
- Source: GAO-23-106440, "Weapon System Sustainment: Navy Ship Usage Has Decreased as Challenges and Costs Have Increased" (Jan 2023). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106440

### F8. Shipyard infrastructure: the physical capacity bottleneck, growing to more than $200B over 50+ years
- SIOP (the Shipyard Infrastructure Optimization Program) began in 2018 as a 20-year, $21B plan.
  - The 2022 update reported that more than half of capital equipment is past its service life.
  - The backlog of restoration and modernization projects grew by more than $1.6B in 5 years.
  - The first 3 dry-dock projects grew by more than $4B.
- 2023:
  - Without the upgrades, the Navy cannot support almost a third of planned carrier and submarine maintenance periods through 2040.
  - Portsmouth dry dock: $528M grew to $2.2B.
  - Pearl Harbor: $6.1B grew to $16B.
- 2026: SIOP will surpass $200B and could take more than 50 years. Environmental and seismic issues at Puget Sound added work.
- Sources:
  - GAO-22-105993 (May 2022). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105993
  - GAO-23-106067 (June 2023). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106067
  - GAO-26-107830 (Sept 25, 2026). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107830

### F9. Surface ships at private yards: contract and funding friction causes delays
- Under the MAC-MO contracting approach, average cost growth across 41 availabilities was 5%.
- If an availability runs into a new fiscal year and needs more than $4M in prior-year funds, Navy and DoD approval is required. This approval took 26-189 days and was a persistent cause of delays.
- Expected but unquantifiable repairs at contract start also cause slips. The Navy now funds "undefined but expected" work at award.
- Source: GAO-20-370, "Navy Ship Maintenance: Evaluating Pilot Program Outcomes and Making Key Decisions Could Improve Maintenance Contracting" (May 2020). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-370

### F10. Overseas-homeported surface ships, FY2014-2018
- 50 of 71 periods (about 70%) ran long, for 3,475 days in total. More than half of these delays lasted a month or longer.
- Causes:
  - work added after the package was awarded (unplanned work)
  - a shortage of government civilian personnel
  - contractor performance shortfalls
  - crew-related delays
- Source: GAO-20-86 (Dec 2019). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-86

### F11. The Navy's own fix targets were missed; deferred-maintenance backlog
- The July 2020 Navy report set a goal of cutting days of maintenance delay by 80% in FY2020. GAO found it was not achieved, and the Navy report "did not identify costs."
- GAO has made 39 Navy maintenance recommendations since 2015. As of Sept 2020, 37 had concurrence and 6 were implemented.
- Source: GAO-21-66 (Oct 2020). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-66
- Deferred depot maintenance backlog is about $1.8B (about $1.7B for surface ships). The Navy understated it by about $1.6B in its 2021 financial reports. 9 ships were decommissioned early, losing 34 years of service life.
- Source: GAO-22-105032 (2022). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105032

### F12. Analog from aviation: the F-35 repair backlog
- The F-35 mission-capable rate was about 55% in March 2023. More than 10,000 components were awaiting repair. Of the more than $1.7T life-cycle cost, $1.3T is sustainment.
- This shows the same pattern outside ships: depot repair capacity, and access to OEM technical data, are the binding constraints.
- Source: GAO-23-105341 (2023). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341

## Reasoning against the claims

**C1 (universal chain): mixed.**
- The Navy's process mostly maps onto the chain. Inspection and certification (SUBSAFE unrestricted-operations certification) gate return to service.
- But the dominant time sinks are not stages of a single repair:
  - queueing for capacity (active idle time waiting for a dry dock or shipyard slot)
  - planning 2.5 years ahead
  - funding approvals (26-189 days)
  - discovery work found mid-repair
- Repair at scale is a scheduled, capacity-constrained portfolio problem with feedback loops: deferred maintenance creates more unplanned work, and cannibalization breaks the donor ship. It is not a linear chain.
- Decommissioning (defueling) competes for the same dry docks.

**C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed, leaning contradicts for this domain.**
- The Navy owns its shipyards and, largely, its technical data. The knowledge is not monopolised by an outside OEM; the government is the OEM-equivalent.
- Yet delays persist. The top causes are unplanned (new) work, workforce capacity and skill, parts, and physical infrastructure.
- There is a knowledge element:
  - inaccurate condition knowledge before work begins (the root of unplanned work)
  - sailors lacking troubleshooting training
  - outdated technical documentation and allowance lists
- It is "unknown ship condition" and "tacit skill" knowledge, not proprietary-monopoly knowledge.

**C3 (AI erases the knowledge gap): contradicts or silent.**
- None of the GAO evidence suggests information alone fixes the bottleneck.
- Survey answers stress hands-on training, people, parts, and dry docks.
- AI could help on condition-based prediction of unplanned work, parts demand modelling (the COSAL allowance lists are inaccurate), and technical documentation. But the tacit, certified, physical skill gap remains.

**C4 (10x cost drop in diagnose, understand, source, deliver): mostly contradicts.**
- The large costs are pierside idle days (about $220-237K per sub-day), physical dry-dock capacity (SIOP at more than $200B), and labor-hours.
- "Diagnose and understand" partly hides inside unplanned work, which is about 4,100 of 7,424 delay days, so better upfront condition assessment could cut a meaningful share.
- Parts sourcing and delivery matter: parts are the #2 submarine cause, onboard spares miss the 65% goal, and backorders run up to 2 years.
- Even so, a 10x combined drop would not fix capacity and workforce, which carry more than 4,000 delay days plus most idle time.

**C5 (bespoke parts solved): contradicts as stated; the need is real.**
- Obsolete parts, diminishing manufacturing sources, 2-year backorders, cannibalization, and reverse engineering by sailors all show the demand is huge.
- But nothing indicates it is solved. Cannibalization keeps rising, and qualified, certified parts for SUBSAFE and nuclear systems need more than fabrication.
- The Navy already does "emergency manufacture of unavailable parts" at its intermediate maintenance facilities.

**C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports.**
- SUBSAFE certification decides whether a boat may submerge. Its expiry is the trigger that turns a boat into an idle boat (Boise, Pasadena).
- Departures from specification rose from 9% to 15% of submarine jobs.
- Cannibalized parts need extra inspection and testing.
- Certification gates are where time and cost pile up.

**C7 (structurally built to be inefficient): partially supports.**
- Annual budgeting, the funding approval rules, planning 2.5 years ahead, workforce hiring sized to planned work only ("no reserve or surge capacity"), and acquisition that ignores sustainment ($4.2B of design-born problems) are all structural inefficiencies.
- GAO repeatedly finds a lack of data: the Navy did not even track surface ship intermediate delays.
- But the 2018 plan did cut active idle time by about 90% from its peak (1,518 to 166 days), which shows targeted management changes can work without a full rebuild.
- The remaining constraints are physical: dry docks and 100-year-old shipyards.

## Where the money and time go
- The money and time go to waiting (idle time waiting for capacity), plus overruns from discovery work and too few skilled workers. They do not go to diagnosis as a separate step.
- Submarines (FY2016-2025): 18,687 lost days and $4.17B.
  - Waiting (active plus inactive idle): about 12,200 days ($2.7B)
  - Depot overruns: 6,494 days ($1.46B)
- Within overruns at carrier and submarine shipyards (FY2015-2019, overlapping attribution):
  - unplanned work: at least 4,100 days (new work alone more than 3,800)
  - workforce: more than 4,000 days (capacity more than 2,500)
  - parts: the #2 cause for submarines
- Capital: SIOP at more than $200B.

## Real bottleneck
Physical and human capacity at certified repair facilities: dry docks (including dry docks for reactor defueling), skilled shipyard workers, and qualified spare parts for obsolete systems. All of it is compounded by poor upfront knowledge of ship condition, which shows up as unplanned "new work," and by rigid funding and planning rules.

## Company-shape implications (speculative)
- Condition assessment and "growth work" prediction before a maintenance period: sensing, digital twins, and ML on past work packages. This attacks the largest single delay cause (unplanned work).
- Obsolete and diminishing-source parts: reverse engineering, plus qualified additive or advanced manufacturing, with a technical data package and certification pipeline. This is demand-proven by cannibalization trends and sailor workarounds. The moat is qualification, not printing.
- Spares demand modelling and allowance-list accuracy (onboard fill rate below the 65% goal).
- Shipyard throughput tools: workforce scheduling, AR or AI work instructions for less experienced trades, and digital inspection and certification evidence.
- Capacity-adding hardware: modular or floating dry docks, and defueling outside dry docks (GAO explicitly recommends evaluating this).
