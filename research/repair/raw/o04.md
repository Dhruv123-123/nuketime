## Core findings

### 1. Neely (2008): servitized firms are larger but less profitable and go bankrupt more often (hard data)
- Neely, A. "Exploring the financial consequences of the servitization of manufacturing", Operations Management Research 1, 103-118 (2008). https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12063-009-0015-5
- Sample: 10,028 firms in 25 countries (OSIRIS database).
- Servitized firms have higher sales revenue but lower net profit as a % of sales overall. Servitization seems to pay off in smaller firms (by employees) and is more problematic in large ones. Bankrupt firms are over-represented among servitized firms.
- [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN] Widely quoted numbers from the paper: about 30% of the manufacturers in the sample were servitized, and in the US the share was about 58%. Servitized firms earned about 3-4% net profit versus about 6-7% for pure manufacturers.
- Meaning: owning service/repair revenue does not by itself make an OEM more profitable. This is the "servitization paradox".

### 2. Gebauer, Fleisch and Friedli (2005): the "service paradox" (study, highly cited)
- "Overcoming the Service Paradox in Manufacturing Companies", European Management Journal 23(1) 2005, doi 10.1016/j.emj.2004.12.006 (995 citations per OpenAlex).
- [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN, content from prior knowledge] Firms invest heavily in extending services, so service offerings and costs rise, but the expected returns do not follow. The paper studies Swiss and German machinery and equipment makers, and the causes it gives are managerial and organisational: no service strategy, no separate service organisation, product-centric culture, and services given away free.
- Gebauer et al. also report (frequently cited) that in many machinery firms services were a large share of revenue but were not managed as a profit centre.

### 3. Fang, Palmatier and Steenkamp (2008): a critical mass of service before value appears (hard data)
- "Effect of Service Transition Strategies on Firm Value", Journal of Marketing 72(5). doi 10.1509/jmkg.72.5.001. Abstract via OpenAlex.
- 477 publicly traded manufacturers, 1990-2005. The effect on Tobin's q is "relatively flat or slightly negative until the firm reaches a critical mass of service sales (20%-30%)" and positive after that. Services work best when they are related to the core product and the firm has resource slack.
- Meaning: most manufacturers sit below the 20-30% threshold, so most do not capture value from services.

### 4. Suarez, Cusumano and Kahl (2013): a U-shaped curve in software (hard data, analog)
- "Services and the Business Models of Product Firms", Management Science (2013). doi 10.1287/mnsc.1120.1634.
- Prepackaged software firms, 1990-2006. There is a convex, non-linear link between the services share of sales and operating margin. Pure product firms are most profitable. Margins decline as services rise, and services add to profit only once they pass the majority of sales.

### 5. Visnjic Kastalli and Van Looy (2013): a profitability hurdle that only scale overcomes (hard data, single-firm panel)
- Journal of Operations Management, doi 10.1016/j.jom.2013.02.001.
- 44 national subsidiaries of one global manufacturer, 2001-2007. Maintenance and other labour-intensive services increase product sales. Profitability rises steeply at first, then dips, then recovers. "Profitable growth seems feasible only to the extent that investments in service capability are translated into economies of scale."
- Meaning: repair and maintenance pay back mainly through scale and density of the installed base. Knowledge alone does not do it.

### 6. Benedettini, Neely and Swink (2015): why servitized firms fail (hard data)
- "Why do servitized firms fail? A risk-based explanation", IJOPM 35(6), doi 10.1108/IJOPM-02-2014-0052 (Crossref abstract).
- 129 bankrupt manufacturers (75 servitized, 54 not). Having a service business brings more bankruptcy risks, mainly internal ones. Demand-chain services also bring environmental risk.
- Meaning: service and repair businesses are operationally hard, with labour, logistics, risk-bearing contracts and working capital. They are not a free information rent.

### 7. Visnjic, Wiengarten and Neely (2016): "Only the Brave" (hard data)
- Journal of Product Innovation Management, doi 10.1111/jpim.12254.
- When service business model innovation is combined with product innovation, it gives long-term gains at a short-term cost. On its own it gives short-term profit but "long-term knowledge loss" and a decline in market performance.
- Meaning: knowledge flows between field service and product design both ways. Service knowledge feeds product R&D, which is part of why OEMs value controlling repair.

### 8. Valtakoski (2017): servitization failure and deservitization from a knowledge perspective (study)
- "Explaining servitization failure and deservitization: A knowledge-based perspective", Industrial Marketing Management 60, doi 10.1016/j.indmarman.2016.04.009 (metadata confirmed via Crossref; abstract not retrievable).
- [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN content] The paper argues servitization fails when the firm cannot build and integrate the knowledge needed (customer process knowledge plus product knowledge). Deservitization happens when the knowledge asymmetry that justified the service disappears, because customers or third parties learn to do it themselves, or because services get commoditised.
- This is the most direct academic analog of C2 and C3: an OEM's service rent depends on a knowledge gap. When the gap closes, service revenue moves to customers or independents. That supports the mechanism, but it also implies the rent is competed away rather than the system becoming "efficient by default".

### 9. Vandermerwe and Rada (1988), Baines et al. (2009), Oliva and Kallenberg (2003): definitions and process (history)
- Vandermerwe and Rada, "Servitization of business: adding value by adding services", EMJ 1988 (2,637 citations). The term originates here: firms move to "bundles" of goods, services, support, self-service and knowledge. They name knowledge explicitly as part of the bundle.
- Baines et al., "The servitization of manufacturing", JMTM 2009 (1,539 citations): "Servitization does not represent a panacea for manufacturers" and "there is little work to date that can be used to help practitioners."
- Oliva and Kallenberg, "Managing the transition from products to services", IJSIM 2003 (11 capital equipment makers). The transition is a deliberate stage-by-stage build of capabilities: first consolidate product-related services, then enter the installed-base service market, then move to relationship and process-centred (outcome) services.

### 10. Practitioner data: Bain, HBR, Wise and Baumgartner (practitioner view)
- Bain (Baveja, Gilbert, Ledingham 2004), "From products to services: why it's not so simple", https://www.bain.com/insights/from-products-to-services-why-its-not-so-simple/ . Product firms that move into services "often do not meaningfully outperform" pure product peers, because of "false assumptions about services commonly held by product executives" (four "service traps").
- [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN] Bain's widely quoted figure: only about 21% of companies that tried service strategies succeeded. Quoted repeatedly in the servitization literature; the primary text was not retrievable.
- Wise and Baumgartner, "Go Downstream", HBR 1999, https://hbr.org/1999/09/go-downstream-the-new-profit-imperative-in-manufacturing . Only 1 in 8 of the 1,000 largest manufacturers beat the S&P 500 after 1988. The thesis is that profit lies downstream in the installed base. [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN: the article argues installed-base service revenues are often several times new-equipment revenue in industries such as locomotives and cars.]
- Cohen, Agrawal and Agrawal, "Winning in the Aftermarket", HBR 2006, https://hbr.org/2006/05/winning-in-the-aftermarket . "Top managements the world over treat aftermarket services as a mere afterthought." [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN: aftermarket of about $1.5T, about 8% of US GDP. Aftermarket brings in about 25% of revenue but a much larger share of profit, and service quality is often poor.]

### 11. Why OEMs keep control of repair knowledge: the regulator view (regulator, hard record)
- FTC, "Nixing the Fix" report to Congress, May 2021, https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf
- The control methods it lists are limited parts availability, withheld manuals, and diagnostic software/tools restricted to authorised networks, plus design, software locks and warranty tying.
- Key quote: "there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions" (safety, security, IP, liability, reputation). Commissioners Wilson and Phillips noted that IP rights "may provide legitimate justification for some repair restrictions."
- Car makers have "taken important steps" (the 2014 auto right-to-repair MOU). Other industries have not.

### 12. FTC and states v. Deere (Jan 2025): the clearest case of a knowledge or tool monopoly (court/regulator allegation)
- https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-states-sue-deere-company-protect-farmers-unfair-corporate-tactics-high-repair-costs
- Service ADVISOR, only for dealers, is allegedly the only fully functional tool for all repairs. The customer version is inferior. Deere allegedly will not give generic tool makers the information they need, unlike auto and trucking makers. That gives an alleged "100% market share" in repairs that need the tool. Dealers "almost always use expensive Deere-branded parts", which feeds a multi-billion-dollar parts business. These are allegations: the vote was 3-2.
- Meaning: the bottleneck here is ACCESS (software authorisation, ECU pairing, dealer exclusivity), not cognition. An AI that "knows" how to fix the tractor still cannot run the authorised calibration or pair the part.

### 13. Rolls-Royce TotalCare: outcome contracts lock in knowledge through data (OEM view)
- https://www.rolls-royce.com/media/our-stories/discover/2017/totalcare.aspx
- Fixed $ per flying hour. Maintenance burden and risk move to Rolls-Royce. RR collects engine performance data and uses "deep design knowledge and in-service experience". It claims overhaul intervals about 25% longer and up to 95% of a used engine recoverable.
- Meaning: in aerospace the OEM keeps control of knowledge through the data flow (engine health monitoring) plus certification of design data and repairs. The contract makes the customer's lack of knowledge a feature the customer pays for (risk transfer). An AI tool that gives the airline the knowledge does not remove the reason to buy risk transfer.

## Synthesis

Fraction that succeed: no single clean number exists. The evidence points the same way:
- Bain: about 1 in 5 succeed ([UNVERIFIED] 21%).
- Neely: servitized firms are on average less profitable and more often bankrupt.
- Fang et al.: no value until services reach 20-30% of sales.
- Suarez et al.: no margin benefit until services pass 50% (software).
- Visnjic and Van Looy: a profitability dip that only scale overcomes.
Fair summary: a minority, roughly 20-30%, of manufacturers that push into services earn superior returns from it. Those that do have scale, high installed-base density, separate service organisations and outcome contracts (Rolls-Royce, GE, Caterpillar, Otis/Kone elevators, Siemens Healthineers).

Why OEMs keep control of repair knowledge (mechanisms from the literature):
1. Economic rent. The aftermarket is high-margin and counter-cyclical, so locking it in is deliberate (Wise and Baumgartner, Cohen et al., FTC). This partly supports C7, which says inefficiency is designed in. But it is designed for the OEM's profit, not by accident.
2. Information asymmetry as the basis of services. Valtakoski: when knowledge spreads, services are deservitized and the rent moves.
3. Data feedback to R&D. Visnjic et al.: knowledge loss when service is separated from product.
4. Access controls, not just know-how: software tools, parts pairing, authorised calibration, warranty tying (FTC, Deere).
5. Certification and liability. In aviation, medical devices and nuclear, approved data and repair schemes belong to the type-certificate holder. This is a legal monopoly on approved knowledge, not a cognitive one.
6. Scale economies in field service: labour density, parts logistics and risk pooling (Visnjic Kastalli and Van Looy, Benedettini et al.).

Implications for the hypotheses:
- C2 is partly supported. Knowledge asymmetry is real and is the basis of OEM service rent. But the literature says knowledge is only one of several locks. Access/authorisation, certification, data ownership, parts supply and scale economics are equally or more binding.
- C3 is contradicted as stated. AI can close the cognitive gap (manuals, diagnosis reasoning). It does not close the authorisation gap (dealer-only software, parts pairing), the data gap (OEMs own telemetry), the certification gap, or the field-labour and logistics scale gap. History (Valtakoski on deservitization) suggests that when a gap closes, OEMs move to other locks, such as software and outcome contracts.
- C4 is mixed. The servitization evidence shows profit and cost sit in labour, logistics and risk-bearing more than in diagnosis. Benedettini: failures come from internal operational risks. An order-of-magnitude drop in diagnosis cost alone does not cut total repair cost by 10x.
- C7 is mixed. The aftermarket is built for OEM rent capture (FTC: "scant evidence" for the justifications), so its inefficiency is partly deliberate. But many OEMs fail at services, which suggests the system is not a well-run monopoly either. It is fragmented and badly managed ("afterthought"). That is an opening for independent and third-party operators.
- C1, C5 and C6: little direct evidence. C6 is indirectly supported because certification is a lever that OEMs keep.

Surprises:
- Most OEMs are BAD at service. The supposed "knowledge monopolists" often fail to monetise it (Neely, Bain). The rent is concentrated in a few scale players, plus legal and software locks.
- Deservitization is a documented phenomenon. When knowledge diffuses, OEM service businesses shrink. That is a direct analog of the AI thesis, and in past cases the value moved to independents and customers.
- Separating service from product innovation causes "long-term knowledge loss". Independents with AI do not get the design-feedback loop.

Company shapes suggested:
- An independent service organisation (ISO) "operating system": AI diagnosis plus parts sourcing plus technician dispatch for a fragmented installed base where OEM service is weak. Examples are industrial machinery, medical imaging, and orphaned equipment with defunct OEMs. Reason: the literature says most OEMs fail at service, so a share is there to win.
- Outcome or uptime contracts sold by a third party (power-by-the-hour for non-OEM fleets). AI is used to price risk. Reason: the profitable form of servitization is risk transfer at scale, not information.
- Tooling that targets access locks (open diagnostic interfaces, compliance for right-to-repair laws). Reason: Deere and the FTC show the binding constraint is authorisation and software.
- Avoid: a pure "AI repair knowledge" layer. Knowledge is the lock that is easiest to commoditise, and OEMs are already moving to software, data and certification locks.
