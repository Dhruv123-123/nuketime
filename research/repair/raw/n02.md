## Findings

### F1. Origin: airlines found that fixed-life overhaul was wrong (history, verified via Wikipedia)
- In the early 1960s, with FAA approval, airlines studied in-service aircraft. They found that the assumption that components have a fixed reliable life, after which they must be overhauled, "was wrong in nearly every example studied."
- The term RCM is attributed to Tom Matteson, Stanley Nowlan and Howard Heap of United Airlines. In 1978 the DoD sponsored the UAL textbook and a RAND evaluation.
- Adoption: the US military from the mid-1970s, US commercial nuclear power in the 1980s, Statkraft hydro in the mid-1990s, and Disney parks in 1997 (with McKinsey).
- Source: Wikipedia, "Reliability-centered maintenance", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliability-centered_maintenance

### F2. Six failure patterns (Nowlan & Heap 1978) [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN, canonical figures]
These are the age-reliability curves for UAL civil aircraft components:
- A: bathtub, 4%
- B: wear-out at the end, 2%
- C: failure probability rising slowly, 5%
- D: low when new, then rising quickly to constant, 7%
- E: random / constant, 14%
- F: infant mortality, then constant, 68%

So about **11% (A+B+C)** show age-related wear-out that a fixed-interval overhaul could exploit. About **89% (D+E+F)** have no identifiable wear-out age. For these, scheduled overhaul does not reduce failures, and because of infant mortality (F) it can raise them.
- Source: F. S. Nowlan & H. F. Heap, *Reliability-Centered Maintenance*, United Airlines for the Office of Assistant Secretary of Defense, Dec 1978, DTIC AD-A066579, https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/citations/ADA066579 (403 this run)
- Caveat: later studies found different mixes. For example, the US Navy SUBMEPP (2001) and the 1973 Broberg study are often cited with different percentages, including more wear-out in mechanical and structural items. The percentages depend on the item and industry; they are not universal constants. Practitioner critics (for example in the IEEE "A critical look at the bathtub curve", Klutke et al. 2003, cited on Wikipedia's Bathtub curve page) also say the classic bathtub curve is the exception, not the rule.

### F3. Maintenance cost and labour fell sharply with no loss of safety [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN, canonical figures from Nowlan & Heap]
- The DC-8 (traditional hard-time program) had **339 items** with scheduled overhaul. The DC-10 (MSG-2) program had **7**.
- Structural inspection: under the traditional approach, the DC-8 needed about **4,000,000 man-hours** of major structural inspection before 20,000 hours of operation. The 747, under MSG-1, needed about **66,000 man-hours** for the same interval. That is roughly a **60x reduction** on a larger, more complex aircraft.
- Safety records improved over the same period. The jet-age accident rate fell by orders of magnitude from the 1960s to today; that trend is well known and not specific to this source.
- Interpretation: the order-of-magnitude saving came **from deciding NOT to do work**: abandoning hard-time overhauls, adopting on-condition inspection, and doing failure-finding tasks for hidden functions. It did not come from cheaper diagnosis or parts logistics. It is also an example of a 10x+ fall in maintenance cost achieved in 1965-1980 with decision logic and fleet data, with no AI involved.

### F4. MSG lineage: an industry–OEM–regulator consortium, not a single holder of knowledge [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN, well documented]
- MSG-1 (1968) was written for the Boeing 747 by the Maintenance Steering Group, made up of airlines, Boeing and the FAA. MSG-2 (1970) covered the L-1011 and DC-10. MSG-3 (1980) followed the Nowlan & Heap logic, first applied to the 757/767. It has been revised repeatedly since; the current edition is A4A MSG-3 Revision 2018.1 (cited on Wikipedia).
- The process: industry steering committees and working groups, made up of operators, the OEM and suppliers, apply MSG-3 logic to each system. The output goes to a Maintenance Review Board chaired by the regulator, under FAA AC 121-22 (the MRB process; PDF 403 this run, https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_121-22C.pdf). The result is the MRBR, and the OEM's Maintenance Planning Document is derived from it.
- Implication for C2: in aviation, scheduled-maintenance knowledge is **co-produced and openly codified** across operators, OEMs and regulators. It is not hoarded by the OEM. The knowledge that stays proprietary is engineering data: repair schemes beyond the SRM, component maintenance manuals, and tooling.

### F5. MSG-3 is task-oriented and asks "can you notice it?" first (design logic)
- MSG-3 asks, for each function: is the failure evident to the operating crew? Hidden failures get failure-finding tasks. It then classifies consequences: safety, operational, economic, and hidden-safety or hidden-non-safety. Only then does it choose a task, in order of preference: lubrication or servicing, operational or visual check, inspection or functional check (on-condition), restoration, discard. If no task is "applicable and effective", the result is run-to-failure or a redesign. [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN, standard MSG-3 logic]
- SAE JA1011 (1998) sets the seven RCM questions: function, functional failure, failure modes, effects, consequences, proactive task, default action. (Wikipedia)
- **Implication for "noticing":** RCM puts *detectability* at the centre. It splits failures into evident and hidden. For the 89% with no wear-out age, the only effective proactive task is to notice a *potential failure*: the P-F interval, a term popularised by Moubray in RCM2, early 1990s. The inspection interval must be shorter than the P-F interval. Noticing is therefore the gatekeeper step, and cheap continuous sensing directly widens what can be managed on condition.

### F6. RCM pushed maintenance toward condition monitoring and predictive maintenance (verified via Wikipedia, mostly qualitative)
- RCM's key ideas: most failures are not linked to age, so manage the failure process and manage assets on condition. There are five strategies: predictive, preventive restoration or replacement, detective (failure finding), run-to-failure, and one-time design change.
- Predictive maintenance techniques: vibration, infrared, ultrasonic, oil analysis, motor current signature analysis. Wikipedia's PdM page gives **no rigorous savings figures**. The only number is that unplanned downtime can cost "hundreds of thousands per day", citing a vendor blog. This shows how much of the PdM savings literature is vendor claim, not measured data.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predictive_maintenance

### F7. Failure curves change over time and need fleet-scale data to see (data, verified)
- Backblaze hard-drive fleet, 2013: failure rate 5.1% at 0-18 months, 1.4% at 18 months to 3 years, 11.8% at 3-4 years, a classic bathtub. Survival was about 80% at 4 years and about 50% at 6 years. By 2021, survival at 6 years was 88%, the early-failure phase had almost vanished ("hockey stick"), and wear-out started later.
- Implication: failure patterns are empirical and specific to each fleet, and they shift with design, burn-in and supplier practice. "Noticing" at fleet level requires large, pooled operating data. Like the UAL dataset, that data is a scarce asset, held by fleet operators rather than individual owners.
- Source: Backblaze, "Drive Failure Over Time: The Bathtub Curve Is Leaking", https://www.backblaze.com/blog/drive-failure-over-time-the-bathtub-curve-is-leaking/

### F8. Disney RCM case: cost savings and a safety backlash (news, via Wikipedia)
- Disney introduced RCM in 1997 with McKinsey, which led to layoffs and "large amounts of money" saved. Some people blamed it for later incidents at Disneyland (Los Angeles Times, 2003).
- Implication: cutting maintenance work under the RCM banner can go wrong when the analysis is diluted. Wikipedia also notes that "many later methods reduced the rigor... some misleading, inefficient or even dangerous." This contradicts any assumption that a cheaper process stays safe automatically, and it supports C6.

### F9. Maintenance-induced failure (pattern F) [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN, follows from F2]
- 68% of items showed infant mortality, so intrusive maintenance and repair are themselves a major source of failure. RCM's response was to do *less* invasive work. For the hypothesis: the "repair" and "safety inspection" steps are not neutral. Every intervention carries risk, and much of the inefficiency in old programs was over-maintenance, not slow repair.

### F10. RCM is expensive to do well (practitioner view) [UNVERIFIED-THIS-RUN]
- The common practitioner critique, which drove "streamlined RCM" variants and Moubray's RCM2, is that a full RCM/FMEA analysis per system is labour-intensive. It needs cross-functional teams for weeks to months per asset class. That is why most industries outside aviation, nuclear and defence never adopted it rigorously.
- This is a **real opening for AI**: drafting FMEAs, failure modes and task selection from manuals and work-order histories. But the bottleneck is validation and regulatory acceptance, not text generation.

## Reasoning about the hypotheses
- **C1 (universal chain):** RCM complicates the chain. It adds "hidden failures" that nobody notices until a failure-finding task runs. It treats "do nothing / run to failure" as a legitimate optimal choice. And its biggest cost savings came from *removing* scheduled repairs entirely. So the chain is real for corrective repair, but the main lever in maintenance is upstream: choosing which failures to prevent, detect or tolerate. The verdict is mixed.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly):** In aviation scheduled maintenance, knowledge was deliberately pooled through MSG working groups (airlines + OEM + FAA). The 1960s inefficiency, hard-time overhaul of 339 items, came from a *shared wrong belief* (the age-reliability assumption). It did not come from knowledge withheld from owners. The evidence is mixed, leaning against C2 as the main cause.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap):** The 1960s–70s gap was closed by *data* (fleet failure statistics) plus *decision logic*. The scarce input was operating data at fleet scale. AI does not create that data. The evidence is silent on AI directly and points to data access as the constraint, so the verdict is mixed.
- **C4 (10x cost drop):** There is historical precedent for a roughly 60x fall in structural inspection labour (4M vs 66k man-hours) and a roughly 50x fall in hard-time items (339 vs 7). So order-of-magnitude falls in maintenance cost have happened. But they came from *eliminating unnecessary work and noticing earlier*, not from cheaper diagnosis or parts logistics. This partly supports C4 (10x is possible) and partly contradicts it (the lever is different).
- **C5 (bespoke parts):** Silent.
- **C6 (inspection essential, must evolve):** Strongly supported. RCM made inspection, meaning on-condition checks and failure-finding tasks, the core of maintenance. Inspection intervals are set from P-F intervals, and the MRB regulator sign-off remains the gate. Disney's diluted RCM shows the risk of cutting inspection.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient):** Aviation maintenance was *fundamentally rebuilt* once already (1960–1980) and is now among the most data-driven maintenance regimes. That contradicts "built to be inefficient" for aviation. It does support the view that other sectors have not had their RCM moment: most industrial, consumer and municipal maintenance still runs on time-based or reactive regimes. The verdict is mixed.

## Where the money and time go (from this evidence)
Before RCM, money went to **scheduled overhauls and inspections of items that did not wear out**: over-maintenance and maintenance-induced failures. The RCM revolution cut that by 1-2 orders of magnitude. What remains is detection (inspection and condition monitoring at intervals shorter than P-F), and unscheduled corrective work after random failures. Diagnosis and parts sourcing were not the dominant cost in the RCM story; the size of the scheduled task list was.

## Real bottleneck
Fleet-scale failure and operating data, plus a validated, regulator-accepted decision logic for what to monitor and when. Noticing potential failures within the P-F window is the key step for the ~89% of items with no wear-out age. Detectability and data are scarce, and so is the authority to change a maintenance program, which requires MRB or regulator acceptance.
