# s03: HVAC and plumbing trades: technician shortage numbers, how diagnosis is done, callbacks, ServiceTitan data, AI copilots for technicians (e.g., XOi, Bluon); evidence of AI improving first-time fix

Date: 2026-10-20.

## Findings

### F1. Labour supply (BLS hard data)
- HVACR mechanics and installers: 440,900 jobs in 2025, projected 489,100 in 2035 (+11%, +48,200). About 40,600 openings a year, mostly replacement (retirements and people leaving the trade). Median pay was $61,010 in May 2025 ($29.33/hr). Source: BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/heating-air-conditioning-and-refrigeration-mechanics-and-installers.htm
- Plumbers, pipefitters and steamfitters: 510,600 jobs in 2025, projected 545,200 in 2035 (+7%). About 42,000 openings a year, mostly replacement. Median pay $63,800 ($30.67/hr). Most states require a plumbing licence (often 2-5 years of experience plus an exam), and some require a separate gas licence. Source: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/plumbers-pipefitters-and-steamfitters.htm
- Reasoning: about 82,600 openings a year across the two trades, and most of the need is replacement, not growth. The binding constraint is people (time-to-competence and retirements), not knowledge access as such. Median pay near the all-occupation median for the trade as a whole is weak evidence against an extreme scarcity premium, but pay dispersion (top service techs) is not visible in medians.
- Widely quoted shortage figures, such as "110,000 HVAC tech shortfall" or "80,000 unfilled jobs a year", are industry or vendor numbers. [UNVERIFIED, from memory; origin not traced.] BLS does not publish a "shortage" figure.

### F2. ServiceTitan 10-K (FY ending Jan 31, 2026; filed 2026-03-25). Hard data and management statements
Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1638826/000163882626000028/ttan-20260131.htm
- Revenue was $961.0M (+24% from $771.9M), with a net loss of $159.9M. About 10,800 Active Customers (over $10k annualised billings) make up over 97% of billings. Gross dollar retention has been over 95% for three years. Some franchise networks run over $1B GTV each.
- Labour: "Trades businesses have experienced labor shortages as a result of an aging labor force... These labor shortages can prevent trades businesses from accepting new jobs." ServiceTitan's pricing is partly per technician, so the shortage is a direct revenue risk for it. No quantified shortage data.
- Structure: "Consolidation of trades businesses into larger industry participants within the trades has accelerated in recent years" (private equity roll-ups), along with "increased prevalence of marketplaces for contractors."
- AI products: Atlas (agentic layer, fiscal 2026), Dispatch Pro (tech matching and job-value prediction), Field Pro (technician coaching), Virtual Agents (after-hours calls), Pricebook Pro, Marketing Pro and Benchmark Insights. Claimed outcomes are framed as "close rates, ticket sizes and revenue". There are no first-time-fix or callback metrics, and no quantified results at all.
- AI risk language: outputs "could produce inaccurate or misleading content"; "LLM hallucinatory behavior"; agent actions "could be incorrect or unintended"; customers "may be reluctant to adopt AI-powered solutions, especially agentic workflows"; and the company depends on third-party LLMs ("such as Microsoft and OpenAI").
- Reasoning: the largest trades software platform aims its AI mostly at the commercial side (booking, dispatch, upsell, pricing), not at the technical knowledge of diagnosis. The money in residential HVAC and plumbing software is in demand capture and ticket size.

### F3. ServiceTitan Atlas product page (vendor)
https://www.servicetitan.com/atlas
- In the field, Atlas answers questions from ServiceTitan data and searches "equipment manuals, spec sheets, and installation guides" (with Field Pro). It "help[s] diagnose problems, troubleshoot equipment" with "diagnostic tests and calculators". The office features are marked "Coming soon". No numbers; the only evidence is one testimonial.

### F4. Bluon (vendor claims, HVAC "asset intelligence" plus AI tech support)
https://www.bluon.com/
- Model-number lookup for specs, documentation, bill of materials, OEM parts, compatible replacements, common problems, diagnostics and "who carries each part".
- Coverage: 260-268 OEM brands, 34-35M model numbers (4.9M base models), 18M OEM parts and 1B cross-references. The counts are inconsistent across sections of the page.
- MasterMechanic AI is "built on 135,000 real tech support calls and 50,000 quality-checked problem/solution sets". It claims 99% troubleshooting accuracy.
- Self-reported accuracy against "generic AI with web search": replacement parts 95% vs 11%; procurement 90% vs 12%; OEM part numbers 95% vs 59%; manuals 95% vs 65%; tech support 99% vs 75%. No methodology or test set is published.
- Outcomes: residential customers report an "80% reduction in calls to the Service Manager" and a "notable" reduction in callbacks. Commercial customers report increased first-time fix, with no figure.
- Reasoning: (a) Bluon's own benchmark says generic LLMs are poor at the parts/sourcing layer (11-12%) and fine at general troubleshooting (75%). The value is a proprietary, curated equipment-to-parts graph built from human tech-support calls, not general AI. This contradicts a strong form of C3 (the gap is not gone for anyone with ChatGPT); it moves to whoever owns the structured data. (b) The 80% figure is about escalations to a senior person (the senior tech or service manager as knowledge bottleneck), which supports C2's "knowledge gap" in its internal-to-firm form.
- [UNVERIFIED, from memory] Bluon began as a refrigerant company (the R-458A "BluonEnergy" drop-in) and pivoted to an app with free live tech support for techs. It has raised venture funding.

### F5. XOi (vendor, commercial HVAC/mechanical service)
https://www.xoi.io/
- An "asset intelligence platform" that expands make/model/serial into a structured asset record. It offers guided tech workflows with a knowledge base and "AI job summaries generated after every visit".
- Scale: 85k+ unique models, 150+ manufacturers, 250k+ manuals and technical resources, 18M+ completed jobs.
- Outcomes cited are commercial ones: Veregy $6k extra "pull-through revenue per technician"; JM Brennan 2x close rate; a senior-living operator about $250k value in year one. No first-time-fix or callback figure is on the page.
- Reasoning: like ServiceTitan, XOi's strongest published outcomes are sales pull-through, not fix rates. The AI is mostly documentation capture: video and photos from the visit become an asset record and a quote.
- [UNVERIFIED, from memory] XOi raised a large growth round, around $85M (2022), and has partnerships with OEMs and distributors. It began as a video documentation app for techs.

### F6. Aquant 2026 Field Service Benchmark (vendor dataset, the largest quantitative first-time-fix source found)
https://www.aquant.ai/resources/post/aquants-2026-field-service-benchmark-companies-can-unlock-up-to-26-in-service-cost-savings-by-scaling-knowledge-across-the-workforce
- Data: anonymised data from 161 service organisations, about 30M service events, 7M assets and $8.3B of service cost over 3 years. This is cross-industry field service (equipment OEMs and service organisations), not only residential HVAC.
- First-time fix: benchmark 77%; top performers 88%, bottom performers 60% (a 28-point gap).
- Mean time to resolution: benchmark 4.5 days; top performers 2.5 days, bottom performers 10 days.
- Failed visits are 25% of total service cost at the median: 44% for bottom performers, 14% for top performers.
- About 1 in 5 cases could be resolved remotely.
- Skills gap (first-time fix of top techs minus the rest): 2.9 points at best-in-class organisations, 10 points at underperformers.
- Modelled savings from "scaling knowledge" are typically low double digits, up to 26% in some segments. These are modelled, not observed.
- Retention: top companies keep 87% of employees, underperformers 66%.
- Reasoning: this is the most important number for C4. Even a vendor whose business is selling AI knowledge tools models the prize from closing the knowledge gap at about 10-26% of service cost, not 90%. Failed visits are 25% of cost: eliminating ALL of them, which is impossible, caps the gain at about a quarter, so it is not an order of magnitude. The 28-point first-time-fix spread does show large variance explained by organisation and knowledge, which supports C2.

### F7. Aquant "expertise gap" article (opinion and anecdote)
https://www.aquant.ai/resources/post/the-expertise-gap-isnt-a-future-problem-its-the-problem
- Unattributed: industry first-time fix about 75-80% (top performers 88%+); "roughly one in seven truck rolls is unnecessary", mostly from the wrong tech, wrong part or wrong diagnosis.
- Service VP anecdotes: "We used to escalate two out of three of these calls"; AI "gets us 80% of the way there, fast."
- The headline claims "Half your senior technicians are over 50" but gives no data in the body.
- Reasoning: practitioners' framing is "AI gets 80% of the way". That describes better triage and fewer escalations, not that the gap is gone.

https://www.aquant.ai/

### F9. How HVAC diagnosis is actually done, and how often it is wrong (NIST, hard data from a field-study review)
Source: Domanski, Henderson, Payne, "Sensitivity Analysis of Installation Faults on Heat Pump Performance", NIST Technical Note 1848 (2014), https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/TechnicalNotes/NIST.TN.1848.pdf (doi 10.6028/NIST.TN.1848)
- Downey and Proctor (2002), 13,000 systems tested during routine install, repair and maintenance visits: 65% of 8,873 residential systems needed repairs, as did 71% of 4,384 light commercial systems. "Improper refrigerant charge was found in 57% of all systems."
- Proctor (2004), 55,000 units: 62% of residential and 60% of commercial ACs had incorrect charge. 95% of residential units failed a diagnostic test (duct leakage, airflow, charge, non-condensables or sizing).
- Mowris et al. (2004), 4,168 units: 72% had improper charge and 44% improper airflow. About a 20% efficiency gain after correction.
- Rossi (2004), 1,468 systems: 67% needed service. About 50% of units ran at 80% or less of design efficiency, and 20% at 70% or less.
- Proctor (1997), new installs: only 18% had correct refrigerant charge.
- Parker et al. (1997): airflows below 350 cfm/ton "render invalid most field methods for determining refrigerant charge and can lead to improper charging by a service technician who often does not check the evaporator airflow". This is a diagnosis-method failure: the standard field heuristic (superheat/subcooling, temperature split) is wrong under common conditions. Downey and Proctor also note that "the simple temperature split method for identifying units with low airflow is flawed".
- NIST: the first two problem categories (bad duct design and wrong equipment selection) "are a result of negligent or incompetent work prior to the heat pump installation". Faults combine non-additively (duct leakage plus undercharge are "well-beyond additive").
- NIST also notes that field-survey literature is mostly not in indexed journals and so "not readily available". The knowledge exists but is scattered.
- Reasoning: this is strong, older hard evidence for C2 and C7. A majority of installed residential systems run degraded, and the profession's routine diagnostic heuristics are known to be unreliable. The failure lies in measurement, procedure and installation quality, not only in a lack of information. Fixing it needs instruments (pressure and temperature probes, airflow measurement), time on site and incentive to do it, which AI alone does not provide. Supports C1 only partly: in HVAC most "repair" value is lost to faults nobody notices, so the "notice" step fails silently.
- [UNVERIFIED, from memory, important contrary lead] California utility RCA (refrigerant charge and airflow) incentive programmes, evaluated by CPUC/DNV-KEMA around 2010-2012, found realised savings far below claims. Technicians often mis-adjusted charge, and "fixes" sometimes made units worse. Evaluators (including Mowris) reported that technician diagnostic protocols such as the then-standard RCA protocols were themselves error-prone. Lesson: putting diagnostic tools and protocols in technicians' hands did not automatically yield correct repairs. Incentives and verification mattered more. This should be verified in a follow-up.

### F10. Training and knowledge tools (practitioner/vendor)
- ACCA (https://www.acca.org/) now offers "Ask ACCA", an AI assistant over Manual J/S/D (load calculation, equipment selection and duct design). It also trains on A2L refrigerant safety and EPA 608. The standards body is itself deploying AI over its standards. The knowledge was always published; the gap was in application.
- Interplay Learning (https://www.interplaylearning.com/), a VR/3D trades training company, claims 500,000+ learners and "job ready in weeks, not years". Its outcome figures appear as "0%" placeholders on the page, so no usable data.
- Regulatory and inspection layer: plumbing and gas licensing (BLS) and EPA Section 608 refrigerant handling certification (the EPA page was unavailable, 503). Under the AIM Act the US is moving new residential AC to A2L (mildly flammable) refrigerants, R-454B and R-32, from 2025 [dates from memory]. That adds new safety knowledge and procedures, so the knowledge burden on techs is rising, not falling. Supports C6: certification remains a gate, and new rules are adding to it.

## Synthesis

Where the money and time go (HVAC/plumbing residential and light commercial):
1. Getting a qualified person to the site: labour scarcity, dispatch and truck rolls. Aquant: failed visits are 25% of median service cost, and about 1 in 5 cases could be solved remotely. Aquant also says 1 in 7 truck rolls is unnecessary.
2. Diagnosis quality: NIST field data shows most installed systems carry undetected faults and standard field heuristics are unreliable. The cost shows up as wasted energy and callbacks rather than as invoice line items.
3. Parts: in residential HVAC most parts are commodity and distributor-stocked (Bluon's 18M OEM parts and 1B cross-references). The hard part is identification and cross-referencing, which Bluon says generic AI does badly at about 11%. Physical delivery is rarely the dominant cost in these trades. Supply houses are local and techs carry van stock.
4. Commercial capture: software spend and AI investment (ServiceTitan, XOi) mostly target revenue (bookings, close rate, ticket size), which suggests the business owners' pain is demand and labour, not diagnostic knowledge.

Real bottleneck: skilled hands and senior judgement, meaning experienced technicians who can measure correctly on site, plus the incentive and verification to do it right. AI copilots act mainly as a "senior tech in your pocket", cutting escalations (Bluon: 80% fewer calls to the service manager). They do not remove the need for a licensed person with instruments on site. The second bottleneck is proprietary structured data (model-to-parts-to-fixes graphs from support calls and job records), which is where vendors are building moats.

Stance notes:
- C1 (the universal chain): mixed. The steps exist, but in HVAC the dominant failure is that faults are never noticed (systems run degraded for years), and installation quality, not repair, drives much of it. Many visits combine diagnosis, parts and repair in one truck roll from van stock. There is no separate safety inspection for most residential repairs, except permit and inspection for gas, plumbing and installs.
- C2 (knowledge gap): supports, with nuance. There is a 28-point first-time-fix spread between top and bottom organisations, escalations to senior techs are a bottleneck, and NIST documents widespread mis-diagnosis. But the gap is mostly inside firms (junior vs senior) and in procedure and measurement. It is not mainly an OEM monopoly: residential HVAC manuals and parts are fairly open, and the right-to-repair fight is less central here than in autos or ag.
- C3 (AI ends the gap): contradicts the strong form. Bluon's own benchmark shows generic AI is poor at parts identification and procurement (11-12%). Practitioners say "80% of the way". ServiceTitan's 10-K warns of hallucination and slow adoption. Knowledge is moving to owners of curated data, not disappearing.
- C4 (10x cost drop in the four steps): contradicts. The vendor's own model of closing the knowledge gap is about 10-26% of service cost, and failed visits are about 25% of cost. No source shows an order-of-magnitude change.
- C5 (bespoke parts factory): silent or mildly contradicts. HVAC and plumbing parts are largely commodity and cross-referenceable. The pain is identifying the part, not making it. Safety-relevant components (gas valves, A2L-rated parts) carry listing and certification requirements (UL etc.) [general knowledge].
- C6 (inspection persists and must evolve): supports. Licensing, EPA 608 and the new A2L safety training are all expanding.
- C7 (industry built to be inefficient): mixed. There is real structural waste (degraded installs, failed visits, fragmented SMBs with knowledge in a few heads). Counter-evidence: the industry is consolidating fast (PE roll-ups, per the ServiceTitan 10-K), digitising (10.8k ServiceTitan customers, $961M revenue) and adopting AI copilots incrementally. Gross dollar retention over 95% suggests the software layer is valued. That looks like evolution, not a need for a ground-up rebuild. The incentive misalignment (flat-rate pricing that rewards replacement over repair, and upsell-focused AI) is real but is a business-model issue, not a knowledge one.
