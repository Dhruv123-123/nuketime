# h07: Legal and professional services as analog (LegalZoom, TurboTax, travel agents vs real estate agents): when does making knowledge abundant destroy an intermediary and when does licensure keep it?

Method note: the shared WebSearch budget for this workflow run was used up before this agent started, so no searches ran. Findings below come from direct WebFetch of known primary or secondary pages (BLS OOH, FTC, Wikipedia summaries citing primary sources, Utah Innovation Office). Items marked [prior knowledge, not re-verified this session] come from well-known published studies I did not re-fetch. Treat them as leads to verify.

## Findings

### 1. Travel agents: knowledge plus booking abundance roughly halved the occupation, but it did not go to zero
- US travel agents fell from 124,000 (1999) to 88,000 (2006) as consumers booked online (Expedia 1996). Airlines cut agent commissions starting in 1999 in Europe and in 2002 in the US. A collusion suit over the cuts failed (appeal decided 2009). Source: Wikipedia "Travel agency" (summarising industry and BLS data), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_agency
- BLS: 61,500 travel agent jobs in 2025, 0% projected change from 2025 to 2035, median pay $50,160. BLS explicitly names online tools "including artificial intelligence (AI)" as limiting demand. https://www.bls.gov/ooh/sales/travel-agents.htm
- Survivors cluster in complex, high-stakes, or bundled niches (corporate, luxury, cruise, complex itineraries). Same Wikipedia source.
- Mechanism: there was **no licence** and **no liability or safety sign-off**. The supplier (the airline) wanted to cut out the intermediary and controlled the inventory, so it removed the commission. The knowledge (fares, schedules) became public via GDS and OTAs. The task was transactional and the stakes were low. Result: simple bookings disintermediated, and complex or high-variance work kept.

### 2. Real estate agents: information became fully public (MLS on Zillow), but agents and commissions survived for two decades. Licensure plus a cartel rule did it, not knowledge.
- BLS: about 530,600 brokers and agents in 2025, with +2% growth projected to 2035. "Every state requires real estate brokers and agents to be licensed." https://www.bls.gov/ooh/sales/real-estate-brokers-and-sales-agents.htm
- Burnett v. NAR: on Oct 31, 2023 a jury found NAR and the major brokerages liable, with $1.8B in damages (trebleable to $5.4B). NAR settled for $418M in March 2024, and practice changes took effect Aug 17, 2024. Changes: cooperative-compensation rule eliminated, and written buyer agreements required. US commissions run about 6% (roughly $100B a year), versus much lower rates in the UK, Canada and Australia. An expert testified to a 3% buyer commission in 95% of the Missouri sales studied. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnett_v._National_Association_of_Realtors
- [prior knowledge, not re-verified this session] Hsieh & Moretti (2003, JPE), "Can Free Entry Be Inefficient? Fixed Commissions and Social Waste in the Real Estate Industry": when house prices rise, commission rates stay sticky, more agents enter, and productivity per agent falls. The rent is dissipated in excess entry, not passed to consumers.
- [prior knowledge, not re-verified] NAR's Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers shows FSBO at a record low (around 6% or less) and roughly 86 to 90% of buyers using an agent, despite near-total listing transparency since about 2006. Early post-settlement data (Redfin and others, 2024-25) show average buyer-agent commissions moved only slightly, staying around 2.4%.
- Lesson: making information abundant (listings, comps, Zestimates) did NOT destroy the intermediary. What protects it: (a) a gatekeeping rule tied to the coordination layer (MLS cooperative compensation); (b) licensure; (c) the transaction's high stakes, infrequency, and need for emotional and negotiation handholding; (d) liability and escrow mechanics. Only antitrust litigation, not technology, started to move the price.

### 3. TurboTax and the tax-prep intermediary: knowledge was codified into software decades ago. The rent then moved to the software vendor, which defended it by lobbying, not by keeping knowledge scarce.
- The Free File Alliance (organised by Intuit) included an agreement that the IRS would not build its own free tool. The restriction was dropped in 2019-2020. In 2019 only 2.7% of the 104 million eligible taxpayers used Free File, while 34.5 million eligible taxpayers paid Alliance members. ProPublica reported that TurboTax hid its free version from Google search. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_File_Alliance
- Intuit paid $141M in a 2022 multistate settlement over deceptive "free" advertising. In March 2026 the Fifth Circuit vacated a 2024 FTC order (citing Jarkesy). Intuit has lobbied against return-free and pre-filled filing, which is standard in many developed countries. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuit
- IRS Direct File: 140,803 accepted returns in the 2024 pilot (12 states), expanded to 25 states in 2025. 86% of users said it increased their trust in the IRS. The program was ended by the administration in 2025, with Intuit and H&R Block having lobbied against it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRS_Direct_File
- Lesson: when knowledge is fully codified (tax rules are public law), the intermediary does not vanish. It re-forms around **distribution, defaults, and regulatory capture**. Tax prep is not licensed for preparers in most states (IRS PTIN only). The moat is political and default-based, not knowledge-based. The most efficient end state (pre-filled returns, as in Nordic countries) needs the **data holder** (the government, analogous to the OEM) to act. Better knowledge tools alone do not get there.

### 4. LegalZoom: a forms and knowledge intermediary survived UPL attacks by settling into a registered and licensed form, then became a law firm itself
- The North Carolina State Bar issued a cease-and-desist in 2008. A court found LegalZoom's practices were not UPL, and a 2015 settlement allowed such companies to operate if they register with the state and follow consumer-protection procedures. Similar suits elsewhere were settled or dismissed. 2024 revenue was $681.9M. In 2021 LegalZoom became the first public company to own a US law firm, after receiving an Arizona ABS licence. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LegalZoom
- Lesson: abundant templates and knowledge took the commodity layer (simple LLCs, wills, trademarks) from lawyers. But LegalZoom's growth path ran through **becoming licensed** (attorney network, owning a firm). It was not a path around licensure. Licensure was not destroyed. The disruptor absorbed it.

### 5. DoNotPay "robot lawyer": AI claims of replacing professionals hit consumer-protection walls
- The FTC alleged DoNotPay claimed to "replace the $200-billion-dollar legal industry with artificial intelligence." It also alleged DoNotPay never tested whether outputs matched human-lawyer quality and never hired or retained attorneys. The proposed settlement was $193,000 plus a ban on claiming substitution for professional services without evidence (Sept 2024, Operation AI Comply). https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes
- Lesson: "the knowledge gap is gone" is a claim regulators now police directly. You need evidence of parity, and you carry liability.

### 6. Mata v. Avianca: AI knowledge is not yet trustworthy knowledge where errors are costly
- In 2023, lawyers filed a ChatGPT-drafted brief with fabricated cases. SDNY Judge Castel imposed a $5,000 Rule 11 sanction for "subjective bad faith." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_v._Avianca,_Inc.
- [prior knowledge, not re-verified] Hundreds of later AI-hallucination filings have been catalogued (for example, Damien Charlotin's database had several hundred by 2025). Many courts now require AI-use certifications.
- Lesson: the scarce layer moves from *producing* an answer to *verifying and vouching for* it. This is directly relevant to repair, where the analog is a diagnosis or repair instruction that someone must sign off.

### 7. Regulatory sandboxes: licensure reform stalls even with good data
- The Utah legal regulatory sandbox (opened 2020) was narrowed in fall 2024, and its ABS-only portion closed at the end of 2024. It is closed to new applications and sunsets Aug 14, 2027, with an ad hoc committee to assess it. https://utahinnovationoffice.org/
- [prior knowledge, not re-verified] Arizona eliminated ER 5.4 (non-lawyer ownership) in 2021 and has licensed 100+ ABS entities. England and Wales has allowed ABS since 2011 (Legal Services Act 2007), with roughly 1,500 licensed. In 2022 the ABA passed Resolution 402 reaffirming opposition to non-lawyer ownership. California's legislature blocked its own reform effort.
- Lesson: incumbents defend licensure politically even when sandbox harm data is benign. Licensure persists through politics, not because the knowledge is scarce.

### 8. Occupational licensing economics: rents, not quality
- Licensing covered under 5% of US workers in the 1950s and about 22 to 29% by the 2000s-2010s. Kleiner and Krueger found a 15 to 18% wage premium. Kleiner and Soltas (2019, NBER) estimated an average welfare loss of about 12%. There is little evidence of overall quality gains (dentists: higher prices, no better outcomes). There are exceptions where licensing helped, such as early midwifery laws reducing maternal mortality (Anderson et al. 2020). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_licensing
- Lesson: where an intermediary's value is mostly the licence rent, abundant knowledge does not dislodge it. Only legal change does. Where the licence corresponds to real safety risk, it persists for good reason. This maps onto repair inspection and certification: FAA Part 145 and A&P mechanics, electricians, gas fitters, and pressure-vessel inspectors.

### 9. Additional cross-industry analogs [prior knowledge, not re-verified]
- **Stockbrokers**: per-trade commissions went from fixed (until May Day 1975) to about $0 (Schwab, Oct 2019). Trade execution was destroyed as a paid intermediary. But licensed financial advisers (Series 65, RIA fiduciary) grew in number, and fees moved to assets under management. Advice and accountability survived; execution died.
- **Bank tellers after ATMs (Bessen 2015)**: teller employment grew for decades after ATMs because branch costs fell and banks opened more branches. Routine tasks were automated, but the role shifted to relationship work.
- **Radiology**: Hinton (2016) said to stop training radiologists. Radiologist demand and pay rose through 2025, despite hundreds of FDA-cleared AI tools. Liability, reimbursement codes, and sign-off stayed with the licensed human.

## Synthesis: when abundance kills vs when licensure keeps
Kill conditions (travel booking, stock execution, simple forms):
1. The task is transactional, repeatable, and low-stakes, with errors that are cheap and reversible.
2. No legal requirement for a licensed signature.
3. The upstream supplier (airline, exchange) benefits from cutting out the intermediary and controls the data or inventory.
4. A digital channel can complete the *whole* transaction, not just inform it.

Keep conditions (real estate, law beyond forms, CPA audit, radiology, aviation maintenance):
1. A legal sign-off or liability-bearing attestation is required (licence, certificate, Rule 11, return-to-service signature).
2. A coordination-layer rule ties the intermediary to the transaction (MLS co-op comp, Free File non-compete, OEM authorisation).
3. The transaction is high-stakes and infrequent, and consumers buy assurance and accountability, not information.
4. Incumbents have political organisation (NAR, ABA, Intuit lobbying).

Key insight for repair: in every case, abundant knowledge collapsed the **price of information** but not the **price of accountability**. Where the intermediary's revenue was really an information rent, it died (travel). Where it was a liability, licence, or coordination rent, it survived until law or antitrust changed (real estate 2024, tax Free File 2019). Repair has both. Diagnosis and "understanding" are mostly information rent. Return-to-service and inspection sign-off, OEM parts authorisation, and warranty are accountability and coordination rents. So AI attacks C2-type gaps but does not by itself remove the gate. Whoever holds the signature, the data, and the parts channel captures the value.

## Stances
- C1 (universal chain): silent or mixed. The analogs suggest the chain varies. In some domains (real estate, tax) the bottleneck stage is the transaction or attestation, not the knowledge stages.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly causes inefficiency): mixed. Information asymmetry was real (real estate, tax), but removing it did not remove the rent. The persistent inefficiency comes from licence, coordination rules, defaults, and lobbying.
- C3 (AI makes knowledge gap essentially gone): contradicts. DoNotPay (FTC) and Mata v. Avianca show AI knowledge is not yet trusted or liability-bearing. Even fully public knowledge (tax law, listings) did not dislodge intermediaries.
- C4 (order-of-magnitude drop in intermediate steps): mixed. Travel booking costs did drop massively. But real estate commissions stayed at about 5-6% through 20 years of information abundance. Cost drops require the gate to fall, not only knowledge to spread.
- C5 (bespoke parts solved): silent.
- C6 (inspection remains essential and must evolve): supports. Across analogs, the attestation and sign-off layer survives and becomes the scarce, rent-bearing layer (licensed lawyer, licensed broker, radiologist sign-off).
- C7 (structurally built inefficient, needs rebuild): mixed, leaning supports. Real estate (Burnett) and tax (Free File non-compete) show deliberately preserved inefficiency. But the fix came via antitrust and policy, not via a better product. AI-only rebuilds tend to be absorbed, as LegalZoom became a law firm.
