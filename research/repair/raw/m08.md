## Verified findings

### F1. O&M is roughly a quarter of LCOE onshore and fixed-bottom offshore, and less for floating (hard data)
Source: NREL, *Cost of Wind Energy Review: 2024 Edition* (2023 USD), https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2479271 (catalog page https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2479271).

| Reference plant | CapEx $/kW | OpEx $/kW-yr | Net AEP MWh/MW/yr | LCOE $/MWh | OpEx share of LCOE (my calculation) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Land-based, 3.3 MW | 1,968 | 43 | 4,104 | 42 | ~10.5 $/MWh, about 25% |
| Fixed-bottom offshore, 12 MW | 5,411 | 135 | 4,295 | 117 | ~31.4 $/MWh, about 27% |
| Floating offshore, 12 MW | 7,349 | 108 | 3,346 | 181 | ~32.3 $/MWh, about 18% |

The share is OpEx/AEP divided by LCOE, ignoring discounting nuances, so treat it as approximate. Capital cost still dominates LCOE (about 75%).

### F2. Offshore OpEx is mostly vessels, not technicians or parts (hard data, modelled)
Same NREL report (slide 41), produced with NREL's WOMBAT discrete-event O&M simulator using COREWIND (2021) failure rates.

| Line item | Fixed-bottom $/kW-yr | Floating $/kW-yr |
|---|---|---|
| Maintenance (total) | 113 | 69 |
| Labor (technicians) | 5 | 5 |
| Materials | 2 | 4 |
| **Equipment (vessels)** | **105** | **61** |
| Operations | 22 | 38 |
| Port fees | 1 | 17 |
| Insurance | 19 | 19 |
| **Total OpEx** | **135** | **108** |

- Fixed-bottom: vessels are about 78% of total OpEx and 93% of maintenance cost. Technician labor is about 4% of OpEx and materials about 1.5%.
- Floating: the tow-to-port strategy replaces jack-up vessels with port and tug costs, which is cheaper than in situ repair. The model assumes 30 technicians, three CTVs, one cable-lay vessel and one dive-support vessel per project.

This bears directly on the bottleneck question. On these numbers, the "knowledge" steps (diagnosing and understanding) are a small slice of offshore O&M money. The money goes to getting heavy-lift capacity to the turbine.

### F3. UK offshore OPEX breakdown: about £85k/MW/yr, with insurance and logistics prominent (practitioner/industry data)
Source: BVG Associates / The Crown Estate / ORE Catapult, *Guide to an offshore wind farm* (2025 update), https://guidetoanoffshorewindfarm.com/wind-farm-costs

- Typical 1 GW UK project with 15 MW turbines, 75 km from shore: OPEX is about £85,000/MW/yr over a 30-year life, against CAPEX of £3.47m/MW. Net production is 4,200 MWh/MW/yr, so OPEX works out to about £20/MWh (my calculation).
- Operations: £31k/MW/yr. Within that, insurance is £11.2k, offshore logistics £7.2k, admin £7.9k and technical resource £6.6k.
- Maintenance and service: £47k/MW/yr. Turbine is £34.2k, balance of plant £11.6k and statutory inspections only £1.0k.
- The guide notes that wave and tide conditions make access harder, "especially for unplanned service activities in bad weather, adding cost and reducing energy production."
- Statutory inspection is about 1% of OPEX. That is evidence against the idea that certification or inspection is a major cost bucket in wind.

### F4. The IRENA range for O&M share of LCOE is 10–30% onshore and 16–25% offshore (secondary, cited in a peer-reviewed review)
Source: Kestel, Chesterman, Zappalá et al., "Condition monitoring of wind turbine drivetrains: state-of-the-art technologies, recent trends, and future outlook", *Wind Energy Science* 11, 2103 (2026), https://wes.copernicus.org/articles/11/2103/2026/ (citing IRENA 2024 and EPRI 2020).

- "O&M expenses, both fixed and variable, generally accounted for 10 % to 30 % of the total LCoE" (onshore, 2023).
- For fixed-bottom offshore in the G20: "16 % to 25 % of LCoE".
- Global onshore O&M spend is "up to USD 15 billion annually" (EPRI 2020).
- "Offshore O&M costs can be several times higher than those of land-based counterparts" (Carroll et al. 2016).

### F5. A minority of components cause most downtime, and mechanical failures dominate (study synthesis)
Same WES 2026 review.

- A minority of assemblies (cited as 38%) is "responsible for over half of all failure rates and 60 % of total downtime" (Artigao et al. 2018).
- Mechanical failures cause "more than 75 % of total downtime".
- Gearbox: "its breakdown typically results in the longest downtime and highest economic loss" (Olabi et al. 2021).
- By O&M cost, generator and gearbox rank first, then blades (Carroll et al. 2016).
- Offshore, pitch systems have the highest failure rates, followed by generators. Frequent small failures and rare big failures are different problems.

### F6. Main bearings fail at about half their design life, and nobody knows the root cause (hard field data)
- Hart et al., "Main bearing replacement and damage: a field data study on 15 gigawatts of wind energy capacity" (NREL/DOE 2023), https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1992019. The study covered 167 plants and 15.3 GW and recorded 689 main bearing replacements. About 10% of the population is replaced by 10.5 years, and 22–25% are projected to be replaced by year 20, against a 20-year design life. Spalling is the dominant damage mode, but the mechanism split is undetermined.
- Quick, Hart et al., *WES* 11, 493 (2026), https://wes.copernicus.org/articles/11/493/2026/. "No principal root cause of main bearing field failures has yet been identified" (Hart 2023; EPRI 2024). Main bearing failure rates "may grow with the increase in turbine rated power". Wake impingement cuts rating life by about 16% on average.
- Hart et al., "A review of wind turbine main bearings", *WES* 5, 105 (2020), https://wes.copernicus.org/articles/5/105/2020/. Lifetime failure "can be as high as 30 %". Historically, reliability databases "lump it in" under drivetrain. There is "currently no openly available data with which to test such a hypothesis". The authors describe a knowledge gap in failure data, modelling, measurements and design standards. The industry already detects main bearing faults about 5–6 months before failure.

This is a knowledge gap, but not the kind the founder means. Nobody holds the missing knowledge, OEMs included: it is an unsolved physics and data problem. Early detection already exists. What is missing is a cheap fix once a fault is detected, because the main bearing still needs a crane.

### F7. Failure data is locked up, and AI prognostics are not widely adopted in industry (study, practitioner view)
Same WES 2026 drivetrain review.

- Because of "intense industry competition, information on wind turbine operations and failures is strictly protected."
- Barriers include "proprietary data access, privacy concerns, and a lack of cross-platform standardization". Owners fear "the disclosure of sensitive information" and are unsure what value data sharing creates.
- "the widespread adoption of RUL estimation and prognostic methods in industrial settings remains limited". Operators are sceptical of lab-validated models.
- "The 'explainability gap' in AI remains a significant barrier to industrial adoption".
- "Uncertainty quantification remains a major challenge"
- "uncertainty and false alarms remain a risk"
- "fault occurrences are typically rare compared to normal operational data", so labels are scarce.
- Reported ML accuracies are about 80% for typical classifiers, rising above 95% in a few binary setups. Those results are from research datasets, not field deployment.
- Gearbox efficiency loss shows up as a temperature rise about 6 months before failure (Feng 2013). Detection lead time is generally not the problem.

This supports C2 in a weaker form: data, rather than knowledge, is held by OEMs and owners. It contradicts C3: AI has been applied to wind CMS for about a decade and adoption is still limited by false alarms, missing labels and trust.

### F8. A quality-failure cascade at Siemens Gamesa cost more than €1bn and required a state backstop (news/history)
Source: Wikipedia, "Siemens Gamesa", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_Gamesa

- In 2023, an internal review found faulty components across a significant share of the onshore fleet (the article says 15–30% of turbine models).
- Replacement costs topped €1bn. The FY2023 loss was €4.4bn, and the German government backed €7.5bn in guarantees in November 2023.
- In 2024, the 163 MW Odal Vind farm in Norway was partially shut down over blade manufacturing defects.

Many wind "repairs" are serial defects across a fleet, handled as warranty campaigns. They are not one-off breakdowns. The economics are set by OEM balance sheets and contracts, and diagnosis is not the main cost.

### F9. Vineyard Wind blade failure: one defect, months offline, then a regulator stop and a $545m dispute (news/court)
Source: Wikipedia, "Vineyard Wind", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vineyard_Wind

- On 13 July 2024, a GE Haliade-X blade failed at turbine AW-38. GE attributed the failure to "insufficient bonding", a manufacturing defect that factory quality control missed.
- BOEM halted construction and generation. Generation resumed in January 2025, about 6 months later.
- More than 100 blades were inspected, and the plan covered removing blades from up to 22 turbines.
- GE paid Nantucket $10.5m. Vineyard Wind claims about $545m from GE, and GE counterclaims about $300m.

Diagnosis was fast. Most of the six months went to regulator-imposed safety review, fleet inspection and offshore blade-removal logistics. This supports C6, with inspection and regulation acting as a gating step.

### F10. Heavy-lift vessels are scarce and expensive (secondary)
Source: Wikipedia, "Wind turbine installation vessel", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_turbine_installation_vessel

- About 16 vessels existed in 2020, growing to about 23 by 2023, with only 7 able to handle the largest turbines.
- Indicative figures: about $220k/day, about $335m to build, about €90m for a three-year lease. The US Jones Act-compliant *Charybdis* cost about $715m.
- "The fast growth of turbine size challenges even the largest vessels."
- The Jones Act "makes it much more difficult" in the US.

Major component replacement offshore competes with new-build installation for the same few jack-ups.

## Background, not verified this session (check before citing)
- **B1. Onshore cranes dominate major corrective cost.** Large-crane mobilization for a gearbox or main bearing swap typically runs from the low hundreds of thousands of dollars upward, and wait times stretch to weeks or months in busy seasons. Industry practice: replacing a gearbox (about $250–500k for the part on a 1.5–3 MW unit) costs about the same again in crane, labor and lost production. Uptower repair (bearing or HSS stage swaps without a main crane) is a recognised ISP cost-cutting niche. Companies cited for this include Liftra self-hoisting cranes, ConeCranes, and uptower specialists such as Sentient/ZF and Moventas.
- **B2. OEMs vs ISPs.** Wood Mackenzie and GWEC O&M reports have long shown OEMs holding the majority of the global O&M market, around 60–70% by capacity in recent years. ISPs (e.g. Deutsche Windtechnik, Global Wind Service, Ventient-type owner/operators, Vestas-acquired ISPs) and self-perform owners hold the rest. Owners typically move from OEM full-service agreements (FSAs, with availability guarantees of about 97%) to ISP or self-perform after the 5–15-year warranty or FSA period. OEMs have lengthened FSAs to 15–25 years to protect service revenue, which carries the highest margins in OEM portfolios. Vestas service EBIT margins are in the high teens to low 20s %, while turbine-manufacturing margins are thin or negative.
- **B3. OEM control points.** Control points include SCADA and controller access and software lock-in (parameter access, proprietary fault codes), spare-part pricing, and documentation. Warranty conditions can be voided by non-OEM work. Large US owners such as NextEra and Avangrid self-perform and run their own repair shops.
- **B4. Repowering versus repair.** In the US, PTC requalification (from about 2016) drove "partial repowering" of 1.5 MW fleets: new rotors and nacelles on old towers. The tax regime, rather than repair efficiency, decided many repair-or-replace choices.
- **B5. Blade leading-edge erosion** is a major, high-volume O&M item, especially offshore. It is mostly rope-access and weather-window limited, and robotic blade-repair startups such as Aerones, BladeBUG and Rope Robotics target it.

## Reasoning against the hypotheses

- **C1 (universal chain): mixed.** The chain fits a single corrective event. In wind, though, much of the work is planned (scheduled maintenance) or fleet-level (serial-defect campaigns like Siemens Gamesa and Vineyard Wind), and that changes the order: diagnose once, then repair many turbines. Weather windows and vessel or crane booking sit between "source parts" and "repair", and the chain does not capture them.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed, leaning supports in data form.** OEMs control data and software, failure data "is strictly protected", and FSAs lock owners in during the first decade. But the deepest gap (main bearing root cause) is unknown to OEMs too. Large owners and ISPs do have the know-how and win share after warranty. On these numbers, the binding constraint is not knowledge.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicts.** ML CMS has been studied for about 10 years. Industrial adoption of prognostics "remains limited" because of false alarms, missing labels, explainability and uncertainty. Fault detection lead times of about 6 months already exist without new AI.
- **C4 (10x cost drop in diagnose, understand, source, deliver): contradicts for offshore.** In NREL's model, technician labor plus materials is about 5% of offshore OpEx and vessels about 78%. Even free diagnosis would barely move OpEx unless it changes vessel use, for example by batching repairs, moving them to calmer weather windows, or turning a replacement into an uptower repair. The "deliver parts" step does carry the cost, but the cost is heavy lift (cranes and jack-ups), which is a physical asset problem rather than an information problem.
- **C5 (AI parts factory): contradicts.** The failing parts are 10–100 t gearboxes, multi-metre main bearings and 100 m composite blades. Making them takes forging, heat treatment, precision grinding and certified composite layup. Blade bonding defects (Vineyard Wind) show that manufacturing quality, not design availability, is the risk. On-demand bespoke fabrication is not credible for these parts. It may fit small obsolete electronics and controller boards for legacy fleets, though this session did not verify that.
- **C6 (inspection essential, must evolve): supports.** Vineyard Wind shows the regulator halt and fleet inspection as the step that sets the timeline. Drone and robotic blade inspection is already a growing market. Statutory inspection is cheap in cost terms (about 1% of OPEX) but decisive for timing.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.** FSA lock-in, OEM data control and service margins far above manufacturing margins are consistent with rent extraction. But the large costs come from physics and logistics: scarce heavy-lift vessels, weather windows and Jones Act constraints. Capital cost dominates LCOE at about 75%. OEMs are also losing money (Siemens Gamesa), which does not fit a picture of an industry comfortably extracting rents.

## Where the money and time go
- In LCOE terms, O&M is about 18–27% (NREL) or 10–30% (IRENA) of lifetime cost. CapEx is most of the rest.
- Offshore, vessels and heavy lift are about 78% of OpEx in NREL's model, and labor plus materials about 5%. Insurance is the next biggest at about 14%. Weather access sets downtime.
- Onshore, major corrective events (gearbox, main bearing, generator, blade) are dominated by crane mobilization and lost production [background].
- In time terms, waiting for a crane or vessel and a weather window outweighs diagnosis time. For fleet defects, regulator review and inspection campaigns take months.

## The real bottleneck
The scarce layer is heavy-lift access: crane and jack-up vessel capacity, plus weather windows to use them. Two things compound it: early-failing major components with unknown root causes (main bearings), and OEM data and FSA lock-in that keep failure data siloed.

## Company shapes the evidence points at
1. Lift-avoidance and uptower repair: self-hoisting cranes and in-nacelle tooling that turn a main-component replacement into an uptower repair, removing the main crane or vessel. This attacks the largest cost line.
2. Fleet-level failure intelligence and data clearinghouse: anonymized cross-owner failure and SCADA pooling with physics-informed prognostics. Its value lies in batching repairs into vessel campaigns and in planning spares, not in diagnosis alone.
3. Robotic blade inspection and repair (leading-edge erosion, bonding defects) to cut rope-access and weather-window dependence. This ties to C6.
4. Vessel and crane marketplaces or scheduling optimisers that pool heavy-lift capacity across owners.
5. Remanufacture and reverse-engineering of obsolete small parts (controller boards, pitch drives, sensors) for legacy 1–2 MW fleets abandoned by defunct OEMs such as Suzlon in some markets, Clipper, Nordex legacy models and Enron/Zond. This is the only niche where the AI-parts idea plausibly fits.
