## Findings in detail

### 1. DoD cost of NFF/intermittent wiring faults: over $2B a year (practitioner estimate from NAVAIR)
- Muja (NAVAIR) and Lamper (Eclypse), "Automated Fault Isolation of Intermittent Wiring/Conductive Path Systems Inside Weapon Replaceable Assemblies", SAE Int. J. Aerospace 2012, doi:10.4271/2012-01-2241.
- Abstract: NFF and intermittent diagnoses of wiring inside WRAs "are estimated to cost the DoD over $2B annually". The abstract adds that there was "no logistically supportable, standardized capability" to reduce this cost.
- The same over-$2B figure appears in Wikipedia's NFF article, cited to Werner, Aerospace America (AIAA) 2015.
- Kind: practitioner estimate from inside DoD. It is not an audited figure.

### 2. Most "no fault found" boxes in this test were in fact faulty: 93% of F/A-18 radar receiver chassis had latent wiring faults
- Same paper. NAVAIR built an Automated Wiring Test Set for the F/A-18 APG-73 Radar Receiver. They chose it because it was the No.1 avionics mission degrader on that aircraft.
- The test set checks more than 3,400 conductive paths: under 1 minute for a static test, under 30 minutes for a vibration test.
- Result: 93% of all RR chassis tested had latent wiring faults, and only one chassis had none. Of the faults found, 74% showed up in static testing and 26% only under vibration.
- The standard depot tester (CASS) "cannot diagnose wiring/conductive path faults and was not designed to do so."
- **Implication:** in this case "removing good parts" mostly means removing bad parts that the test rig cannot see. NFF is a failure of observation, not proof that the part was healthy. This is hard data, but from one program.

### 3. GAO: intermittent-fault detection gave large, measured savings, and adoption needed a top-down memo
- GAO-20-116, "DOD Depot Maintenance: ... Best Practices and Lessons Learned", January 2020. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-116 (PDF https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-116.pdf, pp. 19-20, 26-27).
- OSD officials said intermittent faults in aircraft electrical systems cost more than **$300M in O&S costs in FY2014**.
- A maintainer at Ogden ALC created the Intermittent Fault Detection and Isolation System (IFDIS). It replicates the in-flight environment to find opens, shorts and poor wiring. Ogden reports **about $62M in cost savings**. F-16 chassis testing alone recovered **138 out-of-service assets worth $42M**.
- At FRC Southwest, F/A-18 generator MTBF rose from **104 to over 400 flight hours**, and repair time fell from **90 days to 30 days**.
- Barriers: depots needed approval from individual program managers before using IFDIS. The Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Materiel Readiness issued a memo, "Addressing Electronics Intermittence Across DOD's Sustainment Enterprise" (11 April 2019), directing the services to adopt it.
- GAO also records that "the procurement of a weapon system does not always include access to all data necessary to maintain the system", because "the contractor retains ownership of the intellectual property needed to repair or optimize the system." This is evidence for an IP/data-rights piece of the knowledge-monopoly claim (C2).
- Kind: regulator/auditor, with depot-reported numbers.

### 4. Vendor case studies: aircraft grounded for months, fixed in hours once wiring intermittence was instrumented
- Universal Synaptics (makes the IFDIS/PIFD testers to DoD MIL-PRF-32516), http://www.usynaptics.com/.
- C-130 EIDS harness: aircraft grounded for over 18 months, "more than 460 wasted man-hours" with traditional methods. The tester isolated the root cause and finished testing within 4 hours.
- C-130 EHSI harness: grounded over 11 months, over 500 wasted maintenance hours, and **more than 30 components replaced** (good parts removed). The PIFD isolated the fault in 30 minutes.
- Claim: the PIFD helped save $63M on the USAF "Frankenjet" (2025). How that figure was calculated is not shown.
- Kind: vendor claims, not independently checked. They are consistent with the GAO figures above.
- **This is the clearest picture of "cost of removing good parts":** the parts cannon. Technicians swap LRU after LRU because the actual fault (a wire or connector) is intermittent and invisible to their tools.

### 5. F/A-18E/F built-in test false alarms: 85,639 wasted maintenance man-hours and 25,881 hours of needless aircraft downtime
- Wheeler, Kurtoglu, Poll (NASA Ames), "A Survey of Health Management User Objectives Related to Diagnostic and Prognostic Metrics", ASME DETC 2009 / IJPHM 2010, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110014213/downloads/20110014213.pdf.
- They cite Bain and Orwig, "F/A-18E/F BIT Maturation Process", NDIA 2000: BIT caused wasted CND maintenance "of the order of 85,639 maintenance man hours and 25,881 hours unnecessary aircraft downtime"; "this issue has plagued the F/A-18E/F and the V-22 Osprey."
- NASA also notes that "diagnostic systems have had a terrible record for costing more than was saved."
- **Implication:** more automated diagnosis can create NFF by producing false alarms. That counts against the idea that adding smart diagnosis automatically cuts cost (C3/C4).

### 6. Commercial airlines: NFF is a large, persistent share of unscheduled removals (14% to over 40% overall; over 50% for particular LRUs)
- British Airways 1992: about 14% of unscheduled removals were NFF, costing £20M a year.
- ATA: about $20M a year for a 200-aircraft airline. Both are quoted in Moon, Kim, Kim, Lee (Korea Aerospace University), KSAA 34(2), June 2026.
- The same paper uses a Korean carrier's LRU data from 2011 to the present. Group NFF rates: **42.86%** (TCAS computer group) and **57.4%** (EIVMU group).
- Scott et al. 2022 (PMC9502349, citing Söderholm): unscheduled LRU removals "can be in excess of 40% No Fault Founds."
- The 1995 ATA figure of $100M a year for 4,500 NPF events works out to about $22k per event in 1995 dollars.
- An Indonesian MRO study (IOP Conf. Ser. 645 012007, 2019, https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1757-899X/645/1/012007/pdf) shows a reliability problem. MROs class shop findings as NFF / RRC / ODF and take "no action" on NFF. If NFF is counted as a failure, the component reliability statistics come out wrong. In other words, NFF also corrupts the data that reliability programmes run on.

### 7. Electronics and industrial analogues: NTF is 30-70% of field returns in appliances, automotive and semiconductors
- Household appliances: NFF is about 30-70% of returned faulty products. Manufacturers often "blame maintenance engineers' misjudgment" and so miss real defects. A physics-of-failure approach could cut NFF by about 50% (Shi et al., CEPREI 2018).
- Electronics bench diagnostics: NTF/CND at 50-60% (LiVolsi et al. 2011). The paper links some NTF to NBTI wear-out in sub-100 nm CMOS: units fail, then reboot normally after an 8-hour rest, and BIT does not catch it.
- PCBA field returns: "4 out of 5 NTF returns" are contamination. Surface residues cause parasitic leakage that appears only in field humidity and voltage conditions (Munson, Foresite, Pan Pacific Symposium 2015, doi:10.37665/pputcky86349).
- Semiconductors: Cypress analysed 70 NTF customer returns with PCA, Mahalanobis distance and SVM. The NTF returns "are not marginal units" and cannot be screened out without a significant yield penalty (IRPS 2020, doi:10.1109/irps45951.2020.9129635). This is negative evidence for the idea that data-driven screening solves NTF.
- Automotive: Thomas, Ayers, Pecht, "The 'trouble not identified' phenomenon in automotive electronics", Microelectronics Reliability 42 (2002). Paywalled. It is widely cited for high TNI rates in warranty returns of automotive modules, but I could not verify its numbers.
- UK CORD field database: NFF accounted for 50-60% of all failures of a system and was the largest outcome class (Jones thesis 2008).

### 8. Causes: mostly physics and test environment, then human factors and organisation. "Knowledge" in the documentation sense is a minor part
- James, Lumbard, Willis, Goble (Goodrich, Westland, Smiths, BAE), RAMS 2003, doi:10.1109/rams.2003.1182029: the UK REMM programme. Later papers say it classified NFF causes into 10 categories, including intermittent faults, inaccurate diagnosis and organisational culture.
- Steadman et al. (Ogden ALC / Universal Synaptics), Autotestcon 2005, doi:10.1109/autest.2005.1609239: a growing share of "bad actor" and NFF boxes in military weapon systems. Causes are cold or cracked solder joints, corroded or dirty contacts, loose crimps and hairline cracks. These appear "under stress but rarely on a room-temperature bench", and shop tests check normal function only.
- Pickthall, Procedia CIRP 22 (2014), doi:10.1016/j.procir.2014.07.013: an engineer survey and interviews. It found that "aircraft test equipment, integrated onboard maintenance systems, and technical manuals often fail to support engineers during diagnosis." This is a knowledge/tooling cause, so it partly supports C2 and the AI angle.
- Ilarslan and Ungar, IEEE I&M Magazine 2016 (Turkish AF F-16 avionics): the causes are complex enough that technicians alone cannot work out mitigations. A wrong LRU call-out from a faulty indication can repeat across a whole fleet.
- Hockley and Lacey, Procedia CIRP 59 (2017): RAF study across seven fleets, 26 recommendations. They found NFF persistently reduces availability.
- Ungar 2014 ("The true nature of false alarms"): systems are often specified to a false-alarm fraction of around 70%, far from what users expect. Inconsistent terminology also leaves metrics unverifiable.

### 9. Removing a "good" part is often the rational local choice
- Ilarslan, Ungar, Ilarslan 2016 (doi:10.1109/autest.2016.7589571): we "cannot tell which UUT is good". Their economic model compares three strategies: return all NFF units to service and accept bad actors, stress-screen them all, or have a technician choose a repair.
- Line maintenance optimises for dispatch. Swapping an LRU takes minutes and avoids aircraft-on-ground penalties. The shop cost lands on a different budget or party (the MRO, the OEM pool, the depot).
- My reading: NFF is partly an accounting split between line and shop, not purely ignorance.

### 10. AI/ML applied to NFF: promising claims, weak validation so far
- "Probabilistic Machine Learning Could Eliminate No Fault Found", Procedia CIRP 2015, doi:10.1016/j.procir.2015.08.092: "achieved 94% consistency rate over all available data", "virtually eliminating NFF (false positives)". Abstract-level claim only.
- "Analysis of Short form Maintenance Records for NFF Using NLP, Phrase Matching, and Bayesian Learning", Procedia CIRP 2017, doi:10.1016/j.procir.2016.10.123. This is text mining of maintenance logs, which is where LLMs would apply.
- KSAA 2026: Weibull plus a Bayesian network predicts which removals will turn out NFF. The authors recommend it only as a human-in-the-loop aid ("보조 지표", a supporting indicator).
- IJISAE 2026, "Deterministic Maintenance in Safety-Critical Aerospace Systems: A Certifiable AI/ML Framework" (https://ijisae.org/index.php/IJISAE/article/download/8354/7339): reports NFF falling from 31% to 8% (-74%) and MTTR falling 57% on a retrospective set of 14,200 avionics events with "representative scenarios". It is a low-tier journal with no named operator, so treat it as weak evidence. Its own analysis still attributes most NFF to "incorrect fault isolation" and intermittents that vanish on the ground but appear in flight data.
- Note: the AI gains that are claimed come from *combining flight or sensor data with history*. That is, they add observations. They do not come from supplying text knowledge alone.

### 11. Hardware instrumentation has the most solid ROI evidence
- IFDIS/AWTS-type testers: GAO-confirmed savings of about $62M, MTBF up 4x, repair time cut 3x.
- Environmental stress screening: a 1987 study (OpenAlex record "Improved Operational Readiness through Environmental Stress Screening") reports a "2:1 reduction in removal/maintenance action rates".
- A US Army study cited by Ambler 1997 estimated maintenance costs could fall 25% if 70-80% of items were discarded rather than repaired. So sometimes the efficient answer is not diagnosing at all.

### 12. NFF affects safety and airworthiness, not just cost
- Khan, Phillips, Jennions, Hockley, RESS 123 (2014): NFF-tagged units "often contain serviceable components that were removed while root-cause troubleshooting failed." Rising system complexity has increased unknown failures in service. The review also covers safety concerns and standards (ARINC 672, 2008).
- The IJISAE paper and Pickthall both describe the same risk: an NFF unit is returned to service with its paperwork showing "serviced", while the fault is still there and recurs ("rogue units").
- **Implication:** inspection and retest is where the system fails (C6).

### 13. The NFF field itself shows weak data and economics
- Singh, Ganguly, Samad, IJQRM 39(5) 2021, doi:10.1108/ijqrm-02-2021-0026: systematic review of 169 papers (1982-2020). Economic analysis and risk analysis are under-represented.
- Erkoyuncu et al., IJPE 173 (2016), doi:10.1016/j.ijpe.2015.12.013: NFF overhead costs are "particularly difficult to quantify". Cost drivers include transport, OEM and customer costs, inventory and lost labour hours spread across the supply network.
- **Implication:** nobody really knows the total NFF cost. Most headline figures ($2B DoD, $100M ATA, £20M BA) are old, single-source estimates that get repeated.

## Reasoning against the founder's claims

- **C1 (one linear chain).** NFF shows the chain is a loop: notice, remove, ship, shop test, NFF, return to stock, reinstall, recur. Diagnosis also happens twice, in different environments (in flight or on the aircraft, then on the bench), and the two disagree. A chain model that treats "diagnose" as one step misses the main failure mode. Verdict: mixed. The stages exist, but the iterations between them are where the cost sits.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly).** Partly supported: GAO on contractor-held IP and data rights; Pickthall on manuals and test equipment failing technicians. But NFF is just as high *inside* OEM and depot shops that hold all the knowledge. The main causes are physical (intermittents, contamination, wear) and test-environment gaps, so the missing information is a *signal* that was never captured. Verdict: mixed.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap).** Contradicted for this domain. LLMs cannot recover a 50 ns intermittent that was never measured. Smarter BIT has historically *added* CNDs (F/A-18E/F). Data-driven screening could not separate NTF semiconductor returns. AI helps triage which removals are likely NFF and mine maintenance logs, but the evidence is thin and self-reported.
- **C4 (order-of-magnitude cost drop in diagnose / understand / source / deliver).** Mixed. NFF is 30-60% of repair actions, so eliminating it would cut a large share of parts shipping, sourcing and shop labour, potentially halving those stages but not cutting them tenfold across the board. Individual cases do show 100x drops (460 hours to 4 hours; 11 months to 30 minutes), but they come from instrumentation hardware, not knowledge.
- **C5 (bespoke parts).** Silent. Parts supply is not the problem here: too many parts are pulled and sourced, not too few.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve).** Supports. Shop retest is the weak point: the bench cannot reproduce the operating environment, NFF units go back to service, and test standards (MIL-PRF-32516, ARINC 672) are evolving.
- **C7 (structurally inefficient).** Mixed, leaning supports. The organisation is fragmented: line and shop budgets are split, each depot runs separate contracts, programme managers must approve fixes, and an OSD memo was needed to push adoption of a proven tester. But much of the "inefficiency" is a rational hedge against aircraft-on-ground costs under real uncertainty.

## Where the money and time go
For electronics-heavy systems, the dominant cost is the **diagnose and verify loop**: removals that should not have happened, shipping and shop testing of units that test fine, repeat removals of rogue units, and aircraft downtime while technicians swap parts. Physical repair is a small share. NFF is 30-60% of avionics repair actions and 50-60% of returns in some field databases, and each event has historically cost about $20k or more. Parts sourcing and delivery costs are mostly *induced* by bad diagnosis: units are sourced and moved because they were pulled, not because they were broken.

## Real bottleneck
**Observability under real operating conditions**, not knowledge. The failure (intermittent wiring or connectors, contamination, thermal or voltage wear) exists only in flight or field conditions and leaves no record. The bench tester (CASS, ATE) runs a static room-temperature functional test that cannot see it. Next is incentives: line maintenance is rewarded for dispatch, while the cost of NFF falls on the shop or pool. A third, smaller factor is that data is held by the OEM/contractor (data rights).

## Company shapes suggested by the evidence
1. **Intermittent-fault detection made general-purpose.** IFDIS/PIFD-class hardware (thousands of channels, nanosecond intermittence detection, vibration and thermal stimulus) shown to have $60M+ payback at DoD. Extend it to commercial MROs, rail, wind turbines, EV and industrial robot harnesses and connectors. Today it is a niche defence vendor market.
2. **Cross-operator serial-number tracking of rogue/bad-actor units.** Follow each serial number across removals, shops and operators, and flag repeat-NFF units before they are reinstalled (Steadman's F-16 statistical system, KSAA Bayesian prediction). The data moat is pooled removal and shop-finding data, which no single airline or depot has.
3. **Bringing flight or field data to the bench.** Pair in-service sensor and fault logs with stress-replay shop tests, so the bench reproduces the in-service conditions the fault appeared under.
4. **NTF reduction for electronics OEMs' warranty returns** (appliances, automotive, industrial electronics, where 30-70% of returns are NTF). Contamination and physics-of-failure analysis as a service, plus return triage.
5. **Removal-decision support at the line.** A probabilistic "is this LRU likely NFF?" check before pulling, with the human in the loop. This is where AI/LLM log mining fits, but it needs real validation.

## Surprises
- The strongest evidence says NFF parts are often *not* good. 93% of "NFF-prone" radar receiver chassis had latent wiring faults. "Removing good parts" is frequently "keeping bad parts the tests can't see".
- More automated diagnosis (BIT) created NFF on the F/A-18E/F: 85,639 wasted man-hours.
- Even at DoD, a proven tester with GAO-documented savings needed a 2019 OSD memo and programme-manager approvals before it spread.
- A Cypress ML study found NTF semiconductor returns statistically indistinguishable from good units.
- Discarding instead of repairing can beat diagnosis (Army: 25% savings by discarding 70-80% of items).
- The headline cost numbers are old (1992, 1995, 2012) and rarely re-measured. The field itself says economics is under-studied.
