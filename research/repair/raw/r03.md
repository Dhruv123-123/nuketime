## Findings

### Wind blades
1. **[FETCHED] Aerones (Latvia), robotic wind turbine maintenance.** The site claims "up to 4x faster operations", projects "4x shorter and with 10 times less idle stay", 25,000 turbines and 75,000 blades serviced, clients representing "over 50 percent of the world's wind power capacity" (NextEra, GE, Vestas, Enel, Siemens Gamesa), and work in 35 countries. These are vendor claims, and the site gives no $ figure. https://www.aerones.com/
   - [BACKGROUND] Aerones raised venture rounds (around $30M in 2023, plus a larger round in 2024/25, reportedly about $60M+). Verify.
   - Reading: this is the most commercially mature robotic repair category. The robot replaces rope-access technicians for leading-edge-erosion (LEE) repair, cleaning, lightning protection system tests, and inspection. The value is mostly fewer **downtime days and weather-window losses**, not knowledge.
2. **[FETCHED] BladeRobots.** Claims "3-4 times faster" (baseline not stated), a fast-curing coat to cut idle time, and that the robot can "quickly learn new blade types" across brands. There are no cost figures, no installed-base number, and the FAQ is still placeholder "Lorem ipsum" text. Early-stage. https://www.bladerobots.com/
3. **[FETCHED] BladeBUG (UK).** Has pivoted to ultrasonic NDT of blades for subsurface defects (wrinkles, delaminations, bondline failures, voids). It is at pilot stage (EDP pilot; proof of concept at the Avel Braz wind farm, France) and gives no cost or time data. It calls itself a "new and emerging" deep tech startup. https://www.bladebug.co.uk/
   - Reading: the early "walking robot that repairs" thesis has drifted toward **inspection/NDT** as the first sellable wedge. This fits C6: inspection is where robots first earn money.
4. **[BACKGROUND] Wind O&M economics.** O&M is roughly 20-30% of offshore wind LCOE and somewhat less onshore. LEE can cost low single-digit % of annual energy production if left unrepaired. Offshore repairs are gated by weather windows, vessel/jack-up availability and technician availability, not by diagnosis. Verify with NREL Cost of Wind Energy Review / ORE Catapult.

### Aero engines (in-situ / on-wing)
5. **[BACKGROUND] Rolls-Royce IntelligentEngine robotics (announced July 2018, Farnborough):** SWARM (collaborative ~10mm robots for visual inspection inside the engine), FLARE (snake robots for temporary patch repairs of thermal barrier coatings), INSPECT (permanently installed borescopes), and remote boreblending robots. RR's claim was that inspection that needs an engine removal could become on-wing/in-situ, and that remote boreblending could be done with a specialist off-site, cutting travel by expert engineers. Several of these items stayed at research/demo stage for years. RR said the remote boreblending tool went into service use around 2019-2020; verify. The 2018 RR press release URL I tried returned 404.
   - **Directly relevant to the founder's airliner example:** RR's own pitch for remote boreblending was that it removes the need to **fly a specialist team to the aircraft**, because the expert operates the robot remotely. So the bottleneck the founder describes is real, and the OEM is attacking it with tele-robotics while keeping control of the expertise (C2 holds, C3 in the "knowledge gone" sense does not).
6. **[BACKGROUND] GE Aerospace.** Sensiworm is a soft inchworm-like robot developed with SRI International (announced around 2024) to crawl inside engines for on-wing inspection and eventually repair. GE has also done on-wing in-situ coating/cleaning (e.g. foam wash, on-wing TBC repair research) and AI-assisted borescope analysis. A LEAP/CFM shop visit costs millions of dollars per event, and anything that defers removal is very valuable. The Sensiworm page URL I tried returned 404. [FETCHED] The GE press release index (Oct 2026) shows no recent robot headlines. The nearest item is a GE/Shield AI/WZL-2 MOU on F110 MRO in Poland (Oct 12, 2026). https://www.geaerospace.com/news/press-releases
   - Reading: in aero, robots are mostly **inspection/diagnosis extenders** (borescope, crawl-in), and the binding constraint is the **regulated shop visit**: parts (LLPs, castings), shop capacity and certification. Robots here are not removing an expensive "understanding" layer. They avoid engine removal.

### Industrial / defense inspection
7. **[FETCHED] Gecko Robotics (US).** U.S. Navy IDIQ of $71M announced Mar 17, 2026, aimed at "80% fleet readiness by 2027", expanded to $300M on Oct 20, 2026, with claimed "savings up to $8m per ship". A Trident partnership (Jan 2026) claims +40% throughput. Customers named include US Navy, US Air Force, ADNOC, Siemens, Anduril, HF Sinclair, NAES. https://www.geckorobotics.com/
   - [BACKGROUND] Gecko raised a $125M Series D in June 2025 at a ~$1.25B valuation. Verify.
   - Reading: the biggest venture outcome in "repair robotics" is an **inspection + data** company (wall-crawling ultrasonic robots plus a software platform). It sells the "notice/diagnose" layer and readiness forecasting, not wrench-turning. That evidence shows the money is in better sensing of the physical state, which is a **data acquisition bottleneck**, not a knowledge bottleneck. It partly contradicts C3: knowing *what to do* was not the scarce part; knowing the *actual condition of the asset* was.

### Hull cleaning
8. **[FETCHED] Economics of fouling.** Fouling can raise drag by up to 60%, cut speed by up to 10%, and need up to 40% more fuel to compensate. The US Navy estimates about $1B/yr in biofouling costs (Vietti 2009, ONR). Global anti-fouling spend was >$5.7B/yr (1998 figure, dated). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofouling
9. **[FETCHED] Regulation is the gate.** IMO 2023 Biofouling Guidelines (MEPC.378(80)). MEPC.1/Circ.918 (April 2025) on "safe and environmentally responsible in-water cleaning services". MEPC approved work on a **legally binding** biofouling framework, with a draft targeted for MEPC 89 (mid-2029). IMO notes that in-water cleaning can damage coatings and release invasive species and biocides. https://www.imo.org/en/OurWork/Environment/Pages/Biofouling.aspx
   - [BACKGROUND] Many ports (e.g. parts of Australia, New Zealand, California, some EU/Asian ports) restrict or require approval for in-water cleaning, which favours capture-equipped robotic systems (HullWiper, Hullbot, Greensea Armach, Jotun HullSkater for proactive "grooming", Fleet Cleaner). Port permits, not technology, decide market access.
10. **[FETCHED] HullWiper.** ROV-based, high-pressure seawater jets with debris capture, diver-free, and able to clean during cargo ops/bunkering. Its site publishes no performance numbers. https://www.hullwiper.co/

### Data centre robots
11. **[BACKGROUND, weak]** Data centre "repair robots" are mostly at pilot stage: automated tape/hard-drive swap robots, inspection rovers (e.g. thermal patrol robots in Chinese/Asian DCs), and hyperscaler internal projects for drive/server swaps. Hyperscalers already run "fail-in-place"/cold-spare designs, where human swap labour per server is low and the binding constraints are spares logistics and hardware supply. I found no hard economic data in this session. This counts as evidence that robotics is **not** the main lever where design for serviceability (hot-swap, modularity) has already simplified repair.

## Cross-cutting reasoning
- **Where robots actually save money:** (a) avoiding **access cost** (rope access, scaffolding, dry-dock, jack-up vessels, engine removal, confined-space entry); (b) avoiding **downtime** (Aerones's "10x less idle stay"; on-wing vs. shop visit); (c) **safety** (removing humans from blades at height, tanks, boilers); (d) **data density** for inspection (Gecko). None of these is mainly about *knowledge*. They are about **physical access, logistics, and regulated return-to-service**.
- **The chain (C1):** robotics cases mostly fit the founder's chain, but notice and diagnose are often fused (a crawler inspects and gives a verdict), and "repair" can be in-situ, with no parts sourced (blending, coating, LEE filler). Some robotic repair skips the parts steps entirely: material-removal repairs (boreblending) and additive repairs (coatings, fillers). Hull cleaning is recurring *maintenance*, not repair, and has no diagnose/parts step. The chain is a useful frame but not universal.
- **Knowledge monopoly (C2):** this domain partly supports C2. OEMs (RR, GE) build the robots and keep the remote expert in the loop, so tele-robotics extends the OEM's expert reach rather than spreading knowledge. Independent players (Aerones, Gecko) won by owning access hardware plus data, not by unlocking OEM knowledge.
- **"AI makes the gap vanish" (C3/C4):** the evidence goes the other way. Even with perfect knowledge, wind repair waits on weather and access, engine repair waits on shop slots and parts, and hull cleaning waits on port permits. Claimed speedups are 3-4x on the *execution* step (vendor claims), not 10x on diagnose-to-delivery.
- **Bespoke parts (C5):** silent in this evidence. In-situ robotic repair (filler, coating, blending) *restores* material instead of making replacement parts, which sidesteps the parts factory question.
- **Inspection (C6):** strongly supported. Inspection/NDT is the first robot product that sells (Gecko, BladeBUG's pivot, RR SWARM/INSPECT, GE Sensiworm). Regulation (IMO, FAA/EASA return-to-service) shapes what robots may do.
- **Built to be inefficient (C7):** mixed. The inefficiency is real (specialist travel, scaffolding, dry-docks), but it comes from physics, safety and regulation, plus OEM aftermarket incentives (aero). Rebuilds are happening one layer at a time (access robots plus data), not through a wholesale redesign.

## Company shapes suggested
- Access-robot-as-a-service in one vertical with a large downtime cost (Aerones model): wind blades, boiler tubes, tanks, ship hulls, transmission lines. Sell per job or per turbine, priced against downtime days.
- Inspection-data platform (Gecko model): robots are the wedge to proprietary condition data, then sell readiness/forecasting to defense and heavy industry. Defense IDIQs are the large-contract path.
- Remote-expert tele-robotics for regulated assets: a robot on site, the expert operating it remotely. This removes the "fly the team out" step in the founder's airliner example. The partner has to be the OEM/MRO or the certifying authority.
- Compliance-first hull cleaning with capture and documented discharge data, positioned for the binding IMO framework (~2029).
