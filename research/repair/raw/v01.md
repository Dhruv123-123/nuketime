# v01: FTC "Nixing the Fix" (2021) report and later FTC actions: what repair restrictions did it find, what evidence did OEMs give, what did it conclude about cost and safety?

Researched 2026-10-20. Primary source: FTC, "Nixing the Fix: An FTC Report to Congress on Repair Restrictions" (May 6, 2021), PDF at
https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf
(full text extracted with pdftotext and read section by section; page numbers below are report pages).

## 1. What the record was (how thin the evidence base is)

- Inputs: July 16, 2019 workshop, plus a Call for Empirical Research. Staff got **22 responses to the Call for Empirical Research and 68 comments** (p.~15). The FTC itself notes "Most of the responses to the Call for Empirical Research did not actually consist of empirical research and were more in the nature of comments." Hard data: the whole record is small and mostly advocacy on both sides.

## 2. Restrictions the FTC catalogued (Section IV, p.~5-6)

Eight categories:
1. Product designs that complicate or prevent repair (glue, proprietary screws, soldered parts)
2. Unavailability of parts and repair information (manuals, schematics, diagnostic software)
3. Designs that make independent repairs less safe
4. Policies/statements steering consumers to OEM networks ("warranty void if removed")
5. Application of patent rights and enforcement of trademarks
6. Disparagement of non-OEM parts and independent repair
7. Software locks and firmware updates (e.g., Apple synchronising iPhone parts to the logic board; used servers that cannot be resold without a firmware service contract; vendor-signed firmware)
8. End User License Agreements: Repair Association reviewed EULAs of 52 products and said "Basically, 100% of manufacturers have restrictions on repair in every one of their [EULAs]" (p.23).

Also: VIN burning of auto electronic modules (LKQ) preventing reuse of salvaged parts.

Note for C2: only some of these are "knowledge" barriers (manuals, schematics, diagnostic info). Many are *legal/technical access* barriers: parts not sold, parts pairing/serialisation, firmware signatures, EULAs, warranty steering, design. Knowledge alone does not defeat a cryptographic parts lock or a part that is not for sale.

## 3. OEM justifications and the evidence they gave (Section V)

OEM arguments: IP protection; safety (lithium batteries, high voltage, appliances in homes); cybersecurity/privacy of connected devices (CompTIA "20 billion connected products"); liability and reputational harm; consumer demand for thin/light designs; superior quality of authorised service; authorised networks already include small businesses (AHAM: "Of the 20,000 servicers... over 87 percent are authorized local independent... service providers").

Evidence OEMs actually supplied:
- **Safety:** "other than citing to the mobile phone thermal runaway occurring in Australia in 2011, manufacturers provided no data to support their argument that injuries are tied to repairs performed by consumers or independent repair shops", even though the Call specifically asked for such data (p.~27). The FTC adds (fn.145) that by withholding parts and manuals "manufacturers may be exacerbating the very safety concerns they have raised", and notes new OEM phones (Galaxy Note 7) also had thermal runaway.
- **Cybersecurity:** "The record contains no empirical evidence to suggest that independent repair shops are more or less likely than authorized repair shops to compromise or misuse customer data" (p.31). Advocates only seek diagnostics and firmware patches.
- **Liability/reputation:** "Manufacturers provided no empirical evidence to support their concerns about reputational harm or potential liability resulting from faulty third party repairs" (p.~33), again despite the data being requested in individual meetings.
- **Quality:** "Most of the support for this argument is anecdotal". Microsoft said, "without citing specific examples or numbers", it frequently received devices returned due to third-party repair failures; it did cite at least 12 "serious overheating incidents" from off-spec power supplies. EDA/AEM 2019 dealer survey: of dealers who saw modified equipment, 45% saw emissions controls disabled, 54% saw OEM safety features disabled, 62% saw changes reducing reliability. FTC dismissed this as "inapposite because it concerns modifications to equipment as opposed to repairs." (This is real evidence that a slice of "repair" demand in ag/heavy equipment is actually tampering/emissions deletes, which the OEMs and EPA care about.)
- **IP:** "at present, the assertion of IP rights does not appear to be a significant impediment to independent repair". But Commissioners Wilson and Phillips noted the report excluded IP analysis, "which may provide legitimate justification for some repair restrictions" (fn.18). So IP is the least-resolved question.
- **Design/consumer demand:** FTC treated it as open: "Whether consumers are willing to trade repairability of devices for other design features is a question that remains open."

Advocate-side evidence cited: Consumer Reports survey that independent-shop users were more satisfied than factory-service users; Auto Care Association: 70-75% of car owners use independent shops (trust, convenience, price); FDA 2018 report: "the objective evidence indicates that many OEMs and third party entities provide high quality, safe, and effective servicing of medical devices."

FTC bottom line: "there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions" (p.6). "The record does not establish that repairs conducted by independent repair shops would be inferior to those conducted by authorized repair shops if independent repair shops were provided with greater access to service manuals, diagnostic software and tools, and replacement parts as appropriate." The conditional is important: equal *access* is the precondition, not knowledge alone.

## 4. Cost, time and equity conclusions

- Cost: "none of the comments or empirical research submitted before or after the Workshop rebuts the right to repair advocates' argument that repair restrictions increase the price consumers pay for repairs."
- Parts competition data: ABPA: alternative collision parts "typically 15-50% less expensive than car company non patented repair parts"; competition reduced OEM auto parts prices ~8% (APICIA/Micra). Remanufactured parts save up to 85% of material and energy vs. new (LKQ).
- U.S. PIRG: repair could cut household electronics/appliance spend by 22% (~$330/yr per household, ~$40B nationally).
- Apple battery: dropping out-of-warranty battery price $79 -> $29 (2018) led to so many repairs that Tim Cook's Jan 2, 2019 investor letter blamed lower iPhone sales partly on it. Hard data point that repair price elasticity is high, and that cheap repair cannibalises new sales: a direct motive for OEM restriction (supports the "structural incentive" part of C7).
- Time: "The record contains scant rebuttal from manufacturers to the argument that a more open repair ecosystem would allow consumers to have their goods repaired more quickly". Nebraska testimony on tractors down 1-2 days in planting/harvest because only the OEM tech can "plug in a USB port and fix their tractor". Military comment (Kunce/Ekman) on contractor-only repair of military gear.
- Equity: restrictions fall harder on low-income and minority communities and on independent shops (many Black/Latinx-owned); Fed data: only 48% of adults earning <$40k could cover a $400 emergency in cash.
- Environment: contested; CTA disputed the 416,000 phones/day figure as >15 years old; FTC found industry e-waste-decline claims unsupported by the studies cited.
- Auto is the exception: "While the car manufacturing industry has taken important steps to expand consumer choice, other industries ... have not followed suit" (2014 auto R2R MOU / Massachusetts law).

MMWA context: Section 102(c) anti-tying since 1975; only 3 waiver requests ever, all denied (piano maker 1976, pool equipment 1976, Coleman 1978). The FTC cannot get civil penalties under the MMWA rules, only injunctions: an enforcement-weakness fact.

## 5. Later FTC actions (2021-2026)

- **July 21, 2021**: Policy Statement on Repair Restrictions, 5-0 vote, committing to prioritise MMWA and Section 5 enforcement and invite complaints. (Same month as Biden competition EO directing FTC on repair.) https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/policy-statement-federal-trade-commission-repair-restrictions-imposed-manufacturers-sellers
- **June-July 2022**: complaints vs Harley-Davidson, MWE Investments (Westinghouse generators) and Weber-Stephen for warranties implying third-party parts/repair voids coverage; final orders Oct 2022 requiring the language "taking your products to be serviced by a repair shop that is not affiliated with [company] will not void this warranty". No money. https://dickinson-wright.com/news-alerts/right-to-repair-orders-issued-by-ftc ; https://lawstreetmedia.com/?p=20257
- **July 2024**: warning letters to 8 companies: aeris Health, Blueair, Medify Air, Oransi (air purifiers), InMovement (treadmills) for tying warranties to specified parts/service; ASRock, Zotac, Gigabyte for "warranty void if removed" stickers. https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/ftc-sends-warning-letters-over-warranty-practices (Earlier, April 2018, similar letters went to six firms incl. Microsoft, Sony, Nintendo, Hyundai, HTC, ASUS.)
- **Jan 15, 2025**: FTC + Illinois + Minnesota (later Arizona, Michigan, Wisconsin) sue Deere under Sherman Act s.2 and FTC Act s.5. Allegation: Service ADVISOR, the only tool able to do all electronic repairs, restricted to dealers; the customer version cannot do all repairs; Deere uses this to "maintain a 100 percent market share and charge supracompetitive prices for restricted repairs". Deere called it meritless. https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/equipment/article/2025/01/15/two-states-join-ftc-federal-lawsuit ; https://www.theregister.com/2025/01/15/ftc_sues_john_deere/
- **June 2025**: Judge Iain Johnston (N.D. Ill.) denies Deere's motion for judgment on the pleadings; aftermarket and ADVISOR allegations plausible; constitutional challenges rejected. https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/equipment/article/2025/06/11/federal-court-rejects-deeres-motion
- **April 2026**: private class action settles: $99M fund plus repair commitments, no admission. https://www.newstribune.com/news/2026/apr/09/deere-settles-right-to-repair-lawsuit-with-99-million-fund-repair-commitments/
- **July 8, 2026**: FTC/5-state stipulated order (Commission vote 2-0, Ferguson and Meador; i.e. the Republican-led FTC kept pursuing it, so repair is bipartisan). 10 years: Deere must give owners and independent repairers repair resources comparable to dealers on "fair and reasonable" terms; future tools must be offered once they reach >50% of the US dealer network; dealers may not retaliate; $1M to states. Resources named: reading/clearing fault codes; reprogramming and **"pairing" new parts**; restarting after emissions "limp mode"; technical manuals, troubleshooting, "DTAC solutions" and product improvement programs. https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-states-secure-settlement-deere-company-advancing-farmers-right-repair ; https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/ftc-settles-right-to-repair-monopolization-case-against-deere

What the Deere remedy tells us: the scarce asset is the OEM's *diagnostic software + parts-pairing authority + accumulated troubleshooting database (DTAC)*. That is partly knowledge (DTAC solutions, manuals) and partly *authority* (the cryptographic or software right to pair a part or clear an emissions derate). The regulator's fix was to mandate access, not to replace the knowledge.

## 6. Related cost data

- U.S. PIRG "Out to Pasture" (2023): farmers lose ~$3B/yr to downtime and ~$1.2B in excess repair cost = $4.2B. Advocacy modelling, not audited. Notable: **downtime is ~70% of the estimated cost, price premium ~30%**. Also cites USDA data that corn/soy repair costs nearly doubled over two decades. https://pirg.org/media-center/report-tractor-right-to-repair-would-save-u-s-farmers-4-2-billion/

## 7. Contrary / critical views

- American Action Forum primer: safety (Li-ion), privacy (data access by shops), brand/quality, IP and R&D incentives, warranty confusion. Gives no quantitative data for these. https://www.americanactionforum.org/insight/the-debate-on-right-to-repair-a-primer/
- NAM/NetChoice: concerns about IP, software controls ensuring *regulatory compliance* (emissions), cybersecurity, safety systems. The compliance argument is the strongest OEM argument and matches the EDA/AEM tampering survey.
- FTC's own caveats: IP not analysed (Wilson/Phillips); consumer design trade-offs "remains open"; the report is not a quantitative cost study and has no numbers for total repair-restriction cost.

## 8. Reading against the founder's hypotheses

- C1 (universal chain): Report is silent on the chain as such, but its categories map onto it: diagnose (diagnostic software locks), understand (manuals), source (parts unavailability), repair (design, tools), and return-to-service gated by software pairing/limp-mode reset. Pairing/reset is a step the chain omits: "authorise/re-commission" in software. Mixed.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): Supports partially. Information/diagnostic access is central (manuals, Service ADVISOR, DTAC). But the FTC lists eight barriers and most are legal/technical control (parts not sold, pairing, firmware signing, EULAs, warranty steering, design). Better framed as an *access and authority* monopoly than a knowledge one.
- C3 (AI removes the gap): Contradicts/does not support. Nothing in the record suggests knowledge is the binding barrier once software locks exist; AI cannot clear a Deere emissions derate or pair a serialised iPhone part. The FTC's remedy was legal compelled access. Also the FTC's "independent repair is as good" finding is conditional on access to OEM tools and parts.
- C4 (10x on 4 steps): Mixed. Price evidence shows 15-50% parts savings and 22% household savings, i.e. tens of percent, not 10x. Downtime is the larger cost (PIRG ag: $3B of $4.2B), which supports focusing on time, not price.
- C5 (bespoke parts solved): Silent/contra. Report notes patents/trademarks and design patents on collision parts; ABPA complains of design-patent misuse. Even if a part can be made, parts pairing and IP block it. Not addressed as solved.
- C6 (inspection essential): Mixed-supports. OEMs argue authorised networks and certifications ensure quality; repair advocates question OEM certifications' value; FTC suggests certifications/training businesses can advertise. Suggests an opening for independent, non-OEM certification of repairers, but no data.
- C7 (built to be inefficient): Supports the "incentive" version: Apple battery case shows cheap repair cannibalises new sales; Deere "100 percent market share" in repair; warranty steering widespread despite 1975 law. But auto shows the market can be opened (70-75% independent share) without a rebuild, via law and data-access agreements. So "rebuild" overstates; "open access" is what regulators did.

## Surprises
- Republican-led FTC (Ferguson) settled Deere with a 10-year compelled-access order; repair is bipartisan in the US.
- The settlement explicitly names part "pairing" and emissions "limp mode" restart: software authority, not knowledge, is the lock.
- Only 22 empirical responses; OEMs offered one safety incident (2011 Australian phone).
- MMWA has no civil penalties for its rules; three waiver requests in 46 years, all denied.
- In ag, downtime cost (~$3B) dwarfs the repair price premium (~$1.2B).
