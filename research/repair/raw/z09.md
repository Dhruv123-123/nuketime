## Findings (verified this session)

### F1. Scale of military cannibalisation (GAO-02-86, Nov 2001). [regulator/data]
Source: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-02-86 (PDF: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-02-86.pdf)
- FY1996-2000: the Navy and Air Force reported about 850,000 cannibalisations, which took about 5.3 million extra maintenance hours. GAO puts that at "more than 500 aviation maintenance personnel working full-time for 5 years."
- Annual counts ran between 154,000 and 176,000. The Navy reported about 468,000 (roughly 94,000 a year).
- The data are understated. Navy figures may be low "by as much as 50 percent", the Air Force underreports, and the Army does not collect figures across the service. "Neither the Department of Defense nor the services know the overall magnitude of the practice."
- Rates:
  - Navy: 8.8 to 9.6 cannibalisations per 100 flying hours.
  - Air Force: 10.6 to 12.7 per 100 sorties (11.6 in FY2000).
  - B-52H, C-5A/B and B-1B: 30 to 85 per 100 sorties.
  - F-15C/E: 22 to 23 per 100 sorties.
- Hours: in FY2000 the Navy spent 441,000 hours and the Air Force 510,000 hours, about 7.2 extra hours per cannibalisation action. "Cannibalizations ... take at least twice as long as normal repairs." A normal repair is 2 steps. A cannibalisation is 4: remove from the donor, install on the recipient, then later re-install a part on the donor.
- Concentration: 5 Navy types (26% of the inventory) produced 42% of Navy cannibalisations. 648,000 hours went to the FA-18 alone over five years.
- Side effects:
  - Collateral damage, such as wiring harness wear from repeated removal.
  - A used part goes in, not a new one, so the component does not get its full life and is more likely to fail early.
  - Morale and retention suffer. More than half of maintainers work over 50 hours a week.
- DoD's response called cannibalisation a "negligible percentage" of maintenance hours. All three GAO recommendations are marked "Closed - Not Implemented."

### F2. Why cannibalisation happens: more than parts shortages (GAO-02-86). [regulator]
- Official view: spare-parts shortages are "the main reason."
- GAO / Navy evidence:
  - "As many as half of all Navy cannibalizations are not caused by supply system problems."
  - A Navy study group estimated that up to half are done because cannibalising is faster "than to wait for a part to be delivered from across base or town." That is a logistics and latency problem, not missing stock.
  - The Navy Inspector General gave three reasons tied to maintenance quality: (1) inexperienced or under-trained maintainers, (2) outdated maintenance manuals, (3) missing test equipment. "Cannibalizations are sometimes done to diagnose a problem ... By removing a suspect part and replacing it with one that is known to be working, a mechanic can identify where a problem lies, even without testing equipment or proper training."
- This is direct evidence for C2: diagnostic knowledge and tooling gaps generate real cost, because swap-to-diagnose burns hours and parts. The same passage is also evidence that the gap is partly physical (test equipment) and partly incentive-driven (readiness metrics).
- Incentives: "Maintenance personnel told us ... they will do whatever is necessary to keep readiness ratings high." Two Army studies concluded readiness "would be significantly degraded" without cannibalisation. Cannibalisation is a rational local optimisation.

### F3. Obsolescence and loss of suppliers drive cannibalisation (GAO-02-86). [regulator]
- "Original manufacturers may no longer make parts, forcing a new manufacturer to produce the item. This can result in long delays."
- F-14 transmitters: the Navy struggled to find a firm to make them, and the one contractor willing to do so "could not produce the transmitters on schedule."
- EA-6B antennas: repair stopped from Dec 1999 to Mar 2000 because the contractor merged. One antenna later failed quality inspection.
- CH-47 cracked gear grounded the fleet in Aug 1999. As of Mar 2001, 75 transmissions were still on back order.
- Fleet average age, FY1980 to FY2000: Navy 11 to 16+ years, Air Force 13 to 20+, Army helicopters 10.2 to 17.6.
- Relevance to C5: a vanished supplier is a real and recurring problem. But a replacement maker's delay comes from qualification and production, not only from knowing the design. The EA-6B antenna failing inspection shows this.

### F4. F-35 global spares pool: pooling at scale did not deliver (GAO-19-321, Apr 2019). [regulator/data]
Source: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-321 (PDF https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-19-321.pdf)
- How it works:
  - The US services, 8 international partners and FMS customers share one global spares pool, managed by the prime contractor (Lockheed Martin).
  - Participants "do not purchase parts directly, but rather purchase access to parts in the shared pool based on how many F-35 aircraft they own and the number of flight hours."
  - Parts are US-government titled when they are not installed on an aircraft.
- Results:
  - May-Nov 2018: fleet "unable to fly 29.7 percent of the time due to spare parts shortages" (S-rate). DoD funded only to a 20% S-rate.
  - Eglin training units could not fly about 56% of the time for lack of parts.
  - Cannibalisation ran at more than 6 times the services' objective. The objective is 5 per 1,000 flight hours and the threshold is 10. "High rates of cannibalization mask even greater parts shortages."
  - Average depot repair time was about 188 days, against a 60-90 day objective. About 4,300 parts were awaiting repair.
  - 44% of parts bought for a Marine Corps deployment package were incompatible with the aircraft taken.
- Governance problems:
  - "No mechanism within the current construct ... for program participants to optimize readiness for certain units by increasing the allocation of parts to those locations, short of deviating from existing program rules."
  - Until recently, one government official oversaw property accountability. "Potentially thousands of F-35 parts ... used within the global spares pool that have not been entered into the registry."
  - DoD has no database of the F-35 parts it owns.
- Lesson: pooling cuts inventory only when the pool is fed by fast repair turnaround, accurate configuration data and clear allocation and ownership rules. Pooling across sovereign owners creates contested priority ("whose jet gets the part?"). It also concentrates dependency on the pool operator, here the OEM, who also controls the technical data. This is a knowledge and data monopoly in practice.

### F5. F-35 sustainment five years later (GAO-23-105341, Sep 2023; GAO-24-106703, Apr 2024). [regulator/data]
Sources: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341 ; https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106703
- March 2023: about 55% mission capable. More than 10,000 components awaiting repair, up from 4,300 in 2018. Problems included a lack of technical data and training at organisational level.
- DoD had not identified the technical data it needs in order to take over sustainment, which is due by Oct 2027.
- Projected sustainment cost rose 44%, from $1.1T in 2018 to $1.58T in 2023. The Air Force cost is $6.6M per aircraft-year against a $4.1M target. GAO has made 43 recommendations since 2014, and 30 (about 70%) are unimplemented, including on spare-parts management.
- Relevance: the technical-data gap is the constraint officials name. That supports C2. It is a legal and contractual gap (IP and data rights), not just a gap in know-how, so AI alone does not close it (contradicts C3).

### F6. Lufthansa Technik scale (company site, fetched Oct 2026). [practitioner/company claim]
Sources: https://www.lufthansa-technik.com/en/ ; https://www.lufthansa-technik.com/en/aircraft-components
- About 5,100 aircraft under contract ("every 5th aircraft"). More than 800 customers. About 22,580 to 24,500 employees. More than 35 subsidiaries.
- Components page: "5,000+ aircraft benefit from our exclusive component contracts". "One of the largest component pools in the world". Global network of local warehouses and workshops, 24/7 AOG Materials Desk.
- The page gives no published savings, service-level or turnaround numbers. Value claims are qualitative.
- Read: the commercial pool winners are big MROs or OEM-affiliated players whose pools get bigger the more aircraft they cover. Pooling is a scale moat, not a commodity.

### F7. Cannibalisation as a general DMS (diminishing manufacturing sources) tactic. [history]
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannibalization_(parts)
- USS Kitty Hawk was kept running with parts from decommissioned USS Independence. HMS Eagle was kept as a spares source for HMS Ark Royal (1972). Union Pacific 838 supplies 844.
- The standard mitigation is a "lifetime buy" at end of production.
- Read: when the supply base is gone, institutions choose donor hulks or lifetime buys over re-manufacture. Re-manufacture is slower and costlier because of qualification. That counts against the claim that C5 is "effectively solved".

## Background (prior knowledge, NOT verified this session; re-check before citing)
- B1. Inventory-pooling maths, the square-root law: if n sites with independent demand pool their stock, safety stock scales roughly with sqrt(n) rather than n. For low-demand, high-value rotables, sharing one spare across several operators can cut the spares investment a lot. The academic literature on airline rotable pooling (e.g. Kilpi & Vepsalainen 2004, "Pooling of spare components between airlines", J. Air Transport Management 10(2); Wong, Cattrysse & Van Oudheusden on lateral transshipment of repairables, EJOR c.2005-2006) reports large savings in modelled stock or holding cost. I could not open these papers to confirm the figures.
- B2. Commercial pool models include:
  - Lufthansa Technik TCS (Total Component Support, flight-hour-priced access to a pool)
  - AFI KLM E&M component support (also flight-hour-priced)
  - AAR, GA Telesis and others
  - OEM programmes: Boeing Global Fleet Care / Component Services Program, Airbus Flight Hour Services
  - Historical airline cooperatives: IATA's former inventory exchange schemes, and the airline consortia ATLAS (Lufthansa, Air France, Alitalia, Sabena, Iberia) and KSSU (KLM, SAS, Swissair, UTA), which split maintenance and spares by type from the 1970s.
- B3. Why pooling is not universal (common practitioner explanations; partly confirmed by F4):
  1. Configuration and part-number/modification-status differences across operators. The 44% incompatibility in F4 shows this.
  2. Regulatory traceability: Form 1 / 8130-3 tags and back-to-birth records; operators distrust another operator's used parts.
  3. Correlated demand at peaks. When an AD (airworthiness directive) hits or a fleet is grounded, everyone needs the same part at once (CH-47 in F3), and pooling has no benefit.
  4. Allocation conflict and priority rules (F4).
  5. Physical latency: a pooled part far away loses to the jet parked next door. That is why half of Navy cannibalisations are a speed choice (F2).
  6. Incentives and accounting: owned spares sit on the balance sheet, and sharing exposes you to others' maintenance quality.
  7. Pool operators are often OEMs or large MROs who gain lock-in from controlling the pool and the data.

## How this maps to the hypotheses
- C1 (universal chain): mostly supports, with a caveat. Cannibalisation shows the chain is not linear. Parts sourcing can run inside the diagnosis step (swap-to-diagnose), and sourcing can mean another asset rather than a supplier. Return to service then creates a new repair on the donor. Verdict: mixed.
- C2 (knowledge gap): supports in part. Navy IG blamed some cannibalisations on training, outdated manuals and missing test equipment, and F-35 sustainment is limited by technical data the government does not hold. But the largest drivers are parts supply, repair-shop capacity (188-day turnaround, 10,000 backlog) and incentives.
- C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicts. F-35 data gaps are about IP and contracts. Repair turnaround is a physical capacity problem. Test equipment is hardware. Incentives to cannibalise (readiness metrics) are organisational.
- C4 (10x cut in diagnose/understand/source/deliver): mixed. Pooling shows sourcing cost can fall a lot through sharing, per the square-root law. The F-35 case shows the gain disappears if repair capacity, configuration data and governance fail. The binding constraint was repair throughput, not the knowledge steps.
- C5 (bespoke parts solved): contradicts. A new manufacturer for an obsolete part (F-14 transmitter, F-35 canopy coating) brings delays and quality failures. Institutions still prefer donor hulks and lifetime buys.
- C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports. Used and cannibalised parts carry life and traceability risk, pooling depends on certification paperwork, and the antenna that failed QA shows inspection catching problems.
- C7 (industry built to be inefficient): mixed. Cannibalisation is rational under readiness incentives. GAO recommendations from 2001 were never implemented, and F-35 recommendations are 70% unimplemented. That is evidence of institutional inertia, which supports the claim. But the inefficiency comes from incentives, ownership and capacity, not lack of "smart."

## Where the money and time go
- Military: the visible cost is labour. Cannibalisation roughly doubles repair labour, adding about 7.2 hours per action and 5.3M hours over 5 years. The bigger cost is downtime: F-35 non-mission-capable for supply about 30% of the time, and a 188-day repair turnaround. Time sits in the repair loop for broken rotables (depot capacity, backlog) and in parts production, not in diagnosis.
- Commercial: the money sits in rotable inventory capital and in AOG downtime. Pooling attacks the inventory-capital term. Proximity, plus pre-agreed pool and exchange terms, attacks the AOG term.

## Real bottleneck
Repair-loop throughput and parts availability, together with who owns and controls the pool and its technical data. Knowledge matters at the edge: swap-to-diagnose and missing test equipment. The scarce layers are depot repair capacity, qualified sources, configuration and traceability data, and governance of who gets the part.

## Company shapes suggested
- Neutral pool and exchange orchestrator for non-aviation fleets that lack TCS-style pools today: wind turbines, rail, mining trucks, data-centre power gear, defence drones. It would pool rotables across operators with configuration-aware matching and digital traceability.
- A cannibalisation and swap-to-diagnose reducer: AI-guided troubleshooting plus portable test equipment to cut "swap until fixed" hours. Military and fleet maintainers are buyers, and GAO data quantifies the waste.
- A repair-loop capacity company: faster turnaround on high-value rotables (component repair shops, automated test stations), since turnaround drives pool size.
- Qualified re-manufacture for diminishing-manufacturing-sources parts, with certification baked in. The hard part is qualification, not CAD.
