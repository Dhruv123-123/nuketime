### 3. ATEC 2026 Pipeline Report (cites Oliver Wyman) [FETCHED]
- Oliver Wyman: a North American shortfall of about 25,000 aviation technical personnel "this year", including 9,100 certificated mechanics. The certificated-mechanic deficit grows to about 20,000 by 2030, about 12% of demand.

The civilian-experience/apprenticeship pathway grew +21%, reflecting "greater engagement by employers to gain more control of their workforce pipelines."
- Barriers to school growth: (1) funding for facilities, tools and equipment; (2) hiring and keeping faculty. These are capital and labor constraints, not information constraints.
- Women are 3.0% of certificated mechanics.

- Churn: across 10 sectors, employers hired 1.18M technicians in 2025 and lost 1.14M, for a net gain of about 32,450. Turnover is highest in collision (60.7%) and lowest in aviation (32.3%). Report: "You cannot solve a shortage if you cannot keep the people you train."
- Tension inside TechForce's own history: the 2022 report said "Technicians are NOT retiring faster than the overall workforce. Example: auto techs 3.5% exit rate vs 4.8% for all US workers. - Economic cost estimate: $7.42B a year in wage output not produced, or $37.09B over 2025-29. This is a wages-only estimate.
- TechForce claims these jobs are AI-resistant: "Unlike many four-year-degree careers now being reshaped by AI, these jobs cannot be fully [automated]... at the engine, the airframe, or the rooftop unit."

Nuclear technicians are a small, highly paid, slow-growing occupation (BLS). The constraint there is qualification/licensing time (NRC-accredited training, years of OJT) more than raw information.

Diagnostic complexity (ADAS calibration, EV high-voltage) raises the skill bar, and here knowledge and labor interact. FTC "Nixing the Fix" (2021) found OEM restrictions on diagnostic data, tools and parts raise costs for independents (supports C2 for auto and ag).

## Reasoning: is labor the real bottleneck rather than knowledge?

- Hard numbers show a large, persistent, measured labor gap. TechForce counts about 126k unfilled technician entries per year across 10 sectors. Oliver Wyman estimates about 9-20k certificated aviation mechanics short in North America. Boeing projects 728k technicians needed globally over 20 years. Retirement pressure is concentrated in aviation (44% of mechanics over 60).
- Information alone is not why there are few technicians. Inputs that AI does not remove:
  - school funding, tools and equipment;
  - faculty;
  - hands-on hours (FAA Part 147 minimums, apprenticeship OJT, GWO and turbine-specific training);
  - certification/licensing time;
  - physical presence at the asset;
  - working conditions and pay.
- But "knowledge" is part of why each technician is scarce and slow. Much of a technician's value is tacit diagnostic skill built over years of OJT. If AI copilots compress the time to competence (e.g. a 2-year tech performing like a 7-year tech), that raises effective labor supply. In this domain, the strongest form of the founder's thesis is labor amplification: AI makes the knowledge cheaper per technician, so the labor gap narrows. It is not "knowledge is gone."
- The evidence contradicts C3 as stated. AI does not substitute for the certificated human signature, the physical hands, or the shop slot. Aviation also shows that regulated pipelines fill demand better (80% vs 9%), so certification is not merely a monopoly tax.
- Parts and capacity (engines, castings, MRO slots) were as binding as labor in aviation 2023-26. In that period, sourcing parts, delivering parts and repair capacity mattered more than diagnosis.
- Flat real wages suggest that part of the shortage is a wage/conditions problem. Employers have not bid up wages the way a true binding labor scarcity would predict, except in unionized airline maintenance.

## Implications for company shapes
- Productivity tools that raise output per technician: AI diagnostic copilots, guided procedures, and remote expert sessions so one senior tech supervises many juniors. These target the real constraint, which is technician-hours.
- Training compression: simulation/AR plus AI tutoring tuned to FAA Part 147's competency-based rules (the 2022 revision allows this) and GWO modules. AMT schools' load factor is 62%, and their binding constraints are faculty and equipment. That points to a faculty-replacement or faculty-leverage product.
- Workforce-marketplace and retention plays (churn is about 1.14M separations a year).
- Be cautious about pitches that assume knowledge is the bottleneck in sectors where the binding constraint is certificated hands or parts.
