# q03: Defense sustainment costs: share of weapon system lifecycle cost spent on operations and support (~70%), F-35 sustainment, depot maintenance spend; GAO numbers

## Findings

### 1. O&S share of lifecycle cost is "at least 70 percent" (DoD figure, cited by GAO) [HARD]
- GAO-10-717 (July 2010), "Defense Management: DOD Needs Better Information and Guidance to More Effectively Manage and Reduce Operating and Support Costs of Major Weapon Systems." Quote: "According to DOD, the O&S costs incurred after a system has been acquired account for at least 70 percent of a system's life-cycle costs and depend on how long a system remains in the inventory." It continues: "Many of the key decisions affecting O&S costs are made during the acquisition process."
- The same report found that the services lacked production-milestone O&S estimates for 5 of 7 aviation systems, had incomplete actual-cost data, and did not update estimates after production for 6 of 7. F-22A life-cycle O&S estimate grew 47% (about $19B) between 2005 and 2009.
- URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-10-717 (PDF: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-10-717.pdf)
- Reasoning: the 70% figure is real but is a DoD rule of thumb repeated by GAO, not a measured DoD-wide statistic. GAO's own point is that DoD cannot measure O&S well. Also, O&S includes personnel, fuel and training, not only repair, so "70% is repair" would overstate it.

### 2. F-35: $1.58T sustainment of about $2T lifecycle (about 78%) [HARD]
- GAO-24-106703 (April 2024): "DOD estimates the F-35 program will cost over $2 trillion to buy, operate, and sustain over its lifetime. Acquisition costs ... comprise about $442 billion, while operating and support costs ... comprise the other $1.58 trillion." That puts O&S at about 78% of lifecycle cost.
- Projected sustainment rose 44%, from $1.1T (2018) to $1.58T (2023). Planned use runs into the 2080s (to 2088).
- Air Force cost per tail per year: $6.6M projected, against an original $4.1M affordability target. In June 2023 the Air Force raised the target to $6.8M rather than cutting cost.
- Costs were held down partly by flying less. Steady-state fleet hours were cut from 382,376 a year to 300,524 (-21%), and F-35A hours from 230 to 187 per aircraft per year.
- "Sustaining support", which captures most contractor costs, was about one-third of total sustainment cost each year 2018-2021.
- Readiness challenges named by GAO: "heavy reliance on contractors, inadequate training, lack of technical data, lack of spare parts, and lack of support equipment." The prime contractor leads 7 sustainment elements, including technical data, supply support and sustaining engineering.
- GAO made 43 recommendations from 2014 on; 30 (about 70%) were unimplemented.
- In November 2023, DoD dropped a planned performance-based logistics contract because of "data quality issues and cost and performance certifications."
- URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106703 ; PDF https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-24-106703.pdf

### 3. F-35 depot repair: 12 years behind, 141-day repair times, and 73% of components sent back to the OEM [HARD]
- GAO-23-105341 (September 2023), "F-35 Aircraft: DOD and the Military Services Need to Reassess the Future Sustainment Strategy."
- Lifecycle cost above $1.7T, of which about $1.3T is O&S. Mission capable rate was about 55% in March 2023, and over 10,000 components were awaiting repair (up from about 4,300 in April 2019).
- DoD was "nearly 12 years behind schedule" standing up organic depot repair. 44 of 68 planned core component workloads had been assigned.
- Average component repair time: 172 days (2017), then 131 (August 2020), then 141 (February 2023), against a 60-90 day goal. Military service depots repaired components in about 72 days on average, "more than two times faster than the original equipment manufacturer." Nearly half the depots met a 30-day target.
- Even so, "DOD was sending 73 percent of all F-35 components back to the original equipment manufacturer." Organic depots repaired only about 27% of all components, with about 65% possible once capability is fully stood up.
- Because of the backlog, the program buys new parts instead of repairing existing ones, which "resulted in higher sustainment costs."
- The lack of depot capacity costs up to 10 points of mission capable rate. DoD's 2022 analysis projected F-35A air vehicle availability of about 75% with depot capacity versus about 60% without.
- GAO named three causes of slow depot stand-up: funding prioritisation, heavy reliance on contractors, and lack of technical data. "Program officials said manufacturers are unwilling to pass proprietary information."
- URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341 ; PDF https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-105341.pdf

### 4. Knowledge monopoly made concrete: technical data and part numbers held by the contractor [HARD]
From GAO-23-105341, organisational-level maintenance (3 sites visited, 15 installations surveyed):
- "The contractor and the original equipment manufacturer of specific components control the technical data necessary to understand, troubleshoot and repair many of the F-35's components."
- Maintainers must call contractor field service representatives for "what maintainers told us were routine troubleshooting activities." They were frustrated at not being able to make simple repairs they had made on other fighter fleets.
- On one deployment, ejection seat issues required a contractor representative to be flown by helicopter to the ship.
- Technical data requests take "up to 60 days" to answer, and complex issues require several requests of 60+ days.
- Part numbers sit in a database "proprietary to the prime contractor. Maintainers do not have access." 7 of 14 installations asked the contractor rep for part numbers about half the time or more; 3 of 14 always had to ask. One installation said it "would not need contractors on the flight line if they simply had access to part numbers."
- Reasoning: this is the cleanest evidence in this question for C2. Note the mechanism, though: the knowledge exists but is legally and contractually withheld (IP plus database access). It is not knowledge that nobody has. An AI model cannot read a proprietary database it has no access to, so C3 does not follow from C2 here.

### 5. DoD-wide data-rights problems: half of 200+ sustainment programs [HARD]
- GAO-25-107468 (September 29, 2025), Weapon System Sustainment: IP and technical data across F/A-18, F-35, LCS, Stryker and Virginia-class.
- A 2022 DoD data call of more than 200 sustainment programs found half reporting data or data-rights deficiencies that blocked competition or organic sustainment. Nearly half said proposed prices for technical data or licences were not fair and reasonable.
- F/A-18: an 80% rejection rate for sampled deliverables with improper markings. Stryker: a sole-source base-vehicle technical support contract of about $534M over 5 years. LCS: about 2.5 weeks waiting for the OEM to repair a crane motor. Maintainers cannibalise grounded aircraft and submarines for parts.
- DoD's technical data manual dates from 1993 and had not been updated as of September 2025. DoD has unlimited rights to operation, maintenance, installation and training (OMIT) data, but statute excludes detailed manufacturing or process data, which is "a source of disputes." GAO asked Congress to clarify this.
- 10 U.S.C. 2466 (the 50/50 rule) caps contractor share of depot maintenance funds at 50%.
- From sibling note v10 [HARD per that note]: officials said reverse engineering, additive manufacturing and deferred ordering are "generally less effective and efficient" than buying rights early.
- URL: https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107468/index.html

### 6. F-35 2026: mission capable rate down to 44%, $13.7B reset, incentive fees paid for adjusted metrics [HARD + NEWS]
- GAO-26-108113 (June 11, 2026), "F-35 Sustainment: Actions Needed to Ensure Updated Strategy Improves Persistent Readiness Challenges."
- Mission capable rate fell from 67% to 44% (FY2021-FY2025), and full mission capable from 38% to 25%. Lifetime sustainment is $1.6T (2024 estimate). The fleet is over 800 aircraft, with about 1,700 more planned.
- The Global Sustainment Solution (GSS) Reset needs $13.7B more than planned through FY2031: about $2.2B for the reset itself and about $11.5B for the gap between budgeted and required funding. There is a projected gap of more than $1B a year against affordability goals by the mid-2030s.
- Military Times summary: the program depends on industry for more than $7B in additional parts. A 2025 Lockheed study found 48 parts the supplier base cannot produce in sufficient quantity. Lockheed was paid more than $114M of about $269M in available incentive fees (2020-2023). In 19 of 39 periods the FMC rate was adjusted upward; GAO estimates Lockheed would have earned about half as much on raw rates. GAO has made 46 recommendations since 2014, of which 14 were implemented by March 2026.
- URLs: https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-26-108113 ; https://www.militarytimes.com/industry/techwatch/2026/06/12/only-1-in-4-f-35s-is-fully-mission-capable-gao-finds/
- Reasoning: the binding constraints are supply-base capacity (parts that cannot be made in quantity), contract incentives, and depot capacity, alongside data rights. Diagnosis is not the bottleneck.

### 7. Aviation fleet-wide: mission capable goals rarely met, about $54B a year in O&S [HARD]
- GAO-23-106217 (2022/23): of 49 aircraft types, only 4 met their annual mission capable goal in a majority of years FY2011-2021, and 26 never met it. In FY2021 only 2 of 49 met their goal. Total O&S was about $54B in FY2020, of which maintenance grew $1.2B over the period. Causes cited: aging aircraft, maintenance challenges, and parts and supply problems.
- GAO-21-101SP: of 46 aircraft types FY2011-2019, 3 met goals in most years and 24 never did. O&S was above $49B in FY2018.
- URLs: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106217 ; https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-101sp

### 8. Ships: O&S of $17B a year for 151 ships, with more delays and more cannibalisation [HARD]
- GAO-23-106440 (January 2023): 10 ship classes and 151 ships, with O&S of about $17B in FY2020, up $2.5B over FY2011-2020 ($1.2B of that from maintenance). Depot maintenance delay days, cannibalisations and casualty reports all increased.
- URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106440

### 9. Shipyards and submarines: physical capacity and labour, not knowledge, are the bottleneck [HARD]
- GAO-19-229: attack submarines lost 10,363 days to idle time and delays FY2008-2018, costing more than $1.5B. USS Boise sat idle for over 2 years waiting for a shipyard slot. Public yards had been operating above capacity, with workforce and facilities shortfalls.
- GAO-26-109256 (2026): more than 15,000 operational days were lost over 10 years, at about $3.4B. Inactive idle time is growing: 15 submarines face more than 14,000 idle days and about $3.1B FY2026-2030, waiting for dry-dock capacity to defuel.
- GAO-20-588: 38 of 51 carrier and submarine maintenance periods (75%) finished late FY2015-2019, for 7,424 delay days. Unplanned work added more than 4,100 days and workforce problems more than 4,000. Unplanned work led to a 36% underestimate of the personnel needed. Shops ran 25-32% overtime.
- Reasoning: "unplanned work" is partly a diagnosis and inspection problem (discovering hull and system condition only after opening the ship). Better condition knowledge before an availability is a real lever. But execution capacity (dry docks, welders) dominates.
- URLs: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-229 ; https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109256 ; https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-588

### 10. Depot infrastructure: poor, old, and costly to fix [HARD]
- GAO-19-242 (April 2019): 21 depots employ more than 80,000 civilians. 12 of 21 had poor facility condition, and at 15 of 21 the average equipment age exceeded its useful life. The services invested over $13B FY2007-2017 and have a $6.5B restoration backlog that GAO thinks is understated. Air Force aviation depot on-time completion fell from about 98% (FY2007) to about 81% (FY2017). Shipyard delay days rose 45%. The 6% rule requires depots to invest at least 6% of average maintenance workload value.
- GAO-23-106067 and GAO-26-107830: the Shipyard Infrastructure Optimization Program (SIOP) rose from $21B over 20 years (2018 estimate) to more than $200B and 50+ more years (GAO, September 2026). The Portsmouth dry dock went from $528M to $2.2B, and Pearl Harbor from $6.1B to $16B. Without shipyard improvements, the Navy cannot support almost a third of planned carrier and submarine maintenance periods through 2040.
- GAO-22-105032: a $1.8B deferred depot maintenance backlog, of which $1.2B was tied to early decommissioning. 9 ships lost 34 years of service life.
- URLs: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-242 ; https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106067 ; https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107830 ; https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105032

### 11. Information systems that make repair worse: ALIS [HARD]
- GAO-20-316: F-35 sustainment of about $1.2T over a 66-year lifecycle. ALIS data problems ("inaccurate or missing data sometimes leading ALIS to ground flight-ready aircraft").
- Sibling note v10 (GAO 2019): one Air Force unit spent the equivalent of more than 45,000 hours a year on manual ALIS workarounds.
- URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-316
- Reasoning: digital and "smart" logistics systems can create new contractor-controlled lock-in and new failure modes. Software alone did not fix sustainment.

### 12. Policy fight over military right to repair, 2025-2026 [NEWS]
- In April 2025, Secretary Hegseth directed the Army to include right-to-repair terms in new contracts. In May 2025, Army Secretary Driscoll pledged the same.
- The Warren-Sheehy Senate FY26 NDAA provision required contractors to provide data needed to operate and sustain equipment. The AIA said it would "cripple innovation." Industry preferred a House "data as a service" model. (Roll Call, November 17, 2025.)
- The provision was stripped in conference (December 2025). It was replaced by a requirement for DoD to catalogue the data it already has, "request options" from contractors (with no enforcement), and audit contracts that lack data rights. Field examples: a generator in Korea a mechanic could not fix without voiding the warranty, Marine engines shipped to the US and offline for months, and 6 of 8 ovens broken on USS Gerald R. Ford. (Fox News, December 12, 2025.)
- May 12, 2026 SASC hearing: Warren said a Black Hawk cockpit knob costs about $15 to replace, while a contractor charges $47,000 to replace the whole screen, with about 4 knobs failing a month. She also cited $900 per page for Air Force maintenance manual updates under data-as-a-service. Driscoll said lacking repair access "could be the decisive point" in an Indo-Pacific conflict. (Warren press release; these are advocate figures.)
- URLs: https://rollcall.com/2025/11/17/defense-contractors-fight-back-against-ndaa-repair-language/ ; https://www.foxnews.com/politics/trump-backed-military-right-repair-plan-stripped-from-congress-final-defense-bill ; https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/at-hearing-warren-army-secretary-calls-for-congress-to-authorize-the-military-right-to-repair-debunks-defense-contractors-claims-undermining-reforms

### 13. Spare parts pricing power: TransDigm [UNVERIFIED this session; verified in sibling notes o05, v08, v10]
- DoD IG DODIG-2019-060 found excess profit on 46 of 47 sole-source parts (about $16.1M on about $26.2M of sales), with margins up to 4,451%. DODIG-2022-043 found about $20.8M excess on 105 parts. About 90% of TransDigm sales come from proprietary products.
- URLs: https://media.defense.gov/2019/Feb/27/2002094522/-1/-1/1/DODIG-2019-060.PDF ; https://media.defense.gov/2021/Dec/13/2002908344/-1/-1/1/DODIG-2022-043.PDF

### 14. Additive manufacturing qualification is slow [from sibling note v10]
- NAVAIR flew a V-22 with a 3D-printed flight-critical titanium part in 2016. A decade later (Velo3D CRADA, June 2025), qualification of metal AM for flight-critical parts is still in progress. Ships may print only "low criticality" parts with commanding-officer approval.

## Unverified / gaps
- DoD-wide annual depot maintenance spend: commonly cited as roughly $35-45B a year for depot, and about $90B a year for all DoD maintenance. I could not verify these from a primary source this session (no search). They should be checked against DoD's annual 50/50 report to Congress or GAO depot work.
- The "70%" figure is a DoD heuristic. Some programmes are higher (F-35 at about 78%) and some lower (short-lived munitions). There is no DoD-wide measured value.

## Stance reasoning
- C1 (universal chain): Mixed. Defense sustainment does follow notice, diagnose, repair, inspect and return to service at the organisational and depot levels. But the dominant real loops are queueing (waiting for a dry dock or depot slot), unplanned work discovered mid-repair (diagnosis interleaves with repair, it is not a discrete step), and cannibalisation. Much of the cost is also not repair at all: personnel, fuel and training sit inside O&S. Supply-base capacity and contracting are steps the chain omits.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): Supports strongly, but the monopoly is legal and contractual (IP, data rights, proprietary part-number databases, OEM-only repair), not cognitive. Evidence: 73% of F-35 components go back to the OEM even though government depots repair twice as fast; half of 200+ programs report data-rights deficiencies; part numbers sit in a contractor database; and industry lobbying killed the right-to-repair provision.
- C3 (AI removes the gap): Contradicts, mostly. The gap is enforced by contract and statute, and AI cannot read data it has no right to access. Distribution of data deliverables is also a problem (80% rejection on markings). AI could help with reverse engineering and parsing the large volumes of deliverables DoD already owns (GAO says programs cannot track them), and with troubleshooting where data exists. But DoD officials say reverse engineering and AM are "generally less effective and efficient" than buying rights.
- C4 (10x cost drop in four steps): Mixed, leaning contradicts. The largest costs and delays come from depot and shipyard capacity, workforce, parts production (48 F-35 parts the supplier base cannot make in quantity, and more than $7B of parts needed), infrastructure ($200B+ SIOP), and contracting. Diagnosis, understanding and sourcing info do matter, though: 60-day waits for technical data answers, a database lookup for part numbers, and 45,000 hours a year of ALIS workarounds. Those are real but would shave days and FTEs, not 10x the total O&S bill. Organic repair at about 72 days versus OEM at about 141 days shows roughly 2x from changing who repairs, not 10x.
- C5 (AI parts factory solves bespoke parts): Contradicts for safety-critical defense parts. Qualification and certification take years (AM flight-critical work still in progress 10 years after the V-22 demo). Detailed manufacturing data is legally excluded. The supplier base cannot make 48 F-35 parts in quantity. There is an opening for low-criticality and obsolete parts.
- C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): Supports, indirectly. Certification is the gate for AM parts, and unplanned work found at inspection is the biggest single delay driver in shipyards. Readiness metrics themselves were gamed (FMC rates adjusted upward for incentive fees), so independent measurement matters.
- C7 (structurally built to be inefficient): Mixed, leaning supports. The incentives are structural: the prime is paid fees on adjusted metrics, sustainment contracts are annual, IP is withheld, the lobby defeated reform, 70% of GAO recommendations went unimplemented, and the 1993 data manual was never updated. But much of it is deliberate rent design and political economy, not lack of smarts. Rebuilding requires contract, statute and capital (dry docks), which a software company cannot rebuild alone.
