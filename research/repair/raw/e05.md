# e05: Robotics and automation equipment repair in factories (FANUC, ABB robot service, PLC obsolescence like Allen-Bradley/Siemens S5): how plants keep legacy automation alive

## Method note and limits
The shared WebSearch budget for this workflow run (200 calls per turn) was already used up when this agent started, so no new web searches ran. I fetched known primary URLs directly with WebFetch. Five fetches worked: the Siemens/Senseye True Cost of Downtime 2022 PDF, IFR, the FANUC Europe service page, and Wikipedia's Simatic S5 and PLC pages. Several guessed URLs returned 404/403/503 (Rockwell lifecycle pages, ABB lifecycle page, Radwell, FANUC America). Findings are tagged:
- [VERIFIED]: read in a fetched source during this run.
- [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED]: from the agent's prior knowledge. Plausible and widely cited, but check before quoting.

## Findings

1. [VERIFIED, data] Siemens/Senseye "True Cost of Downtime 2022". Unplanned downtime costs Fortune Global 500 firms about 11% of turnover, almost $1.5tn a year, up from $864bn (8%) two years earlier. An average large plant loses 25 hours a month and $129m a year to unplanned downtime. An hour of downtime costs more than $2m in automotive (up from $1.3m in 2019-20) and almost $500k in oil and gas. Hourly cost rose at least 50% in every sector. The share of firms with predictive maintenance teams rose from 11% to 38%. Source: https://assets.new.siemens.com/siemens/assets/api/uuid:3d606495-dbe0-43e4-80b1-d04e27ada920/dics-b10153-00-7600truecostofdowntime2022-144.pdf
   Implication: a plant pays for time-to-restore, not for the repair itself. A $500 board that takes three days to find costs more in downtime than the board is worth. This is the economic engine of the legacy-parts and repair-house market.

2. [VERIFIED, data] IFR World Robotics. The 2023 operational stock was 4,281,585 industrial robots, with 541,302 installed that year (552,946 in 2022). China had about 1.8m. With robot lives of 12-15+ years, a large and growing base is running past its OEM "active" phase. Source: https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/record-of-4-million-robots-working-in-factories-worldwide

3. [VERIFIED, oem_view] FANUC Europe service page. FANUC promises to supply spare parts "for the entire operational life of your products". It also sells remanufactured parts ("like-new, 100% OEM quality") and a "regeneration" program for older products. Source: https://www.fanuc.eu/eu-en/service
   This contradicts C7 in part: at least one dominant OEM makes lifetime support a selling point, and it captures that support as revenue. The knowledge stays inside the OEM, but the OEM does not leave customers stranded.

4. [VERIFIED, history] Wikipedia, FANUC. FANUC holds about 65% of the global CNC control market, and revenue was ¥795.3bn in 2023. System 6 controls were still running decades after manufacture. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanuc
   Concentration means that much of the world's machine-tool fleet uses one vendor's diagnostic codes, parts and knowledge.

5. [VERIFIED, history] Wikipedia, Simatic S5. Launched in 1979 and maintained until about 2000. "Automation systems based on Simatic S5 are still functional worldwide." STEP 5 7.2 can run "under certain conditions" on Windows 10. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simatic_S5_PLC
   [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED] Siemens ended S5 spare-parts delivery and repair around October 2020, after a phase-out announced years earlier. Plants now rely on gray-market, refurbished and repair-house stock.

6. [VERIFIED, history] Wikipedia, PLC. GM's first Modicon 084 ran for "nearly twenty years of uninterrupted service". Ladder logic programs are not portable between vendors, and sometimes not between models from the same vendor. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programmable_logic_controller
   Non-portability is a structural lock-in. Migration means re-engineering, not swapping a part.

7. [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED, oem_view] Rockwell Automation discontinued PLC-5 hardware around 2017 and SLC 500 (1747) chassis and processors around 2023-2024. Rockwell's lifecycle tiers are Active, Active Mature, End of Life and Discontinued. After discontinuation, repair through Rockwell's Remanufacturing/Repair Services continues for a limited, "best effort" period. Rockwell runs migration programs: conversion kits that keep field wiring, and PLC-5 to ControlLogix code translation tools. The OEM business model funnels obsolescence into migration revenue.

8. [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED, oem_view] Siemens' S7-300 product cancellation began around October 2023, with spare parts promised for about 10 more years (around 2033). Siemens and Rockwell both publish phase-out timelines years in advance. Obsolescence in industrial automation is announced and planned, not a surprise. The gap is that plants defer migration because of capex, downtime windows and revalidation.

9. [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED, practitioner_view] An ecosystem of third-party repair and surplus houses (Radwell, Precision Zone, EU Automation, Industrial Automation Co, many local board-repair shops) stocks and repairs discontinued drives, PLC cards, HMIs and FANUC/ABB robot boards and servo amplifiers. Their pitch is fast shipping of obsolete parts and component-level board repair with 1-2 year warranties. They make up the de facto "sourcing and delivery" layer for legacy automation. Their scarce assets are physical inventory, test benches and technicians with tacit knowledge, not information alone.

10. [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED, practitioner_view] Common r/PLC and integrator complaints: the original integrator is gone, the PLC program on the controller has no comments or symbol table (uploaded code loses tags and documentation in older platforms), the programming software only runs on old laptops (Windows XP/DOS, serial or proprietary cables such as the Siemens PG and the A-B 1784-PCMK card), and the person who understood the line has retired. Here the knowledge gap is real (supports C2). Much of it is undocumented, site-specific tacit knowledge, not OEM secrets. AI can help reverse-engineer uncommented ladder logic, but only if someone can get the code off the device and validate changes on a running line.

11. [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED, regulator] In regulated plants (pharma/food under FDA 21 CFR Part 11, GAMP 5, and machine safety under ISO 13849/IEC 62061 and the EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230), replacing a controller or changing code triggers revalidation or recertification of the safety function. The revalidation, not the part, often dominates migration cost and is why plants prefer like-for-like used parts over modern equivalents. Supports C6: inspection and validation remain binding, and they slow upgrades.

12. [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED, news/security] Legacy PLCs carry security risk. Stuxnet (2010) targeted Siemens S7-315/417. Many legacy controllers have no authentication. Keeping legacy automation alive (C7) has a cost in security debt, which matters to a founder in security tech.

13. [BACKGROUND, UNVERIFIED, oem_view] OEM AI and remote diagnostics already exist: FANUC ZDT (Zero Down Time, IoT-based failure prediction, piloted with GM from about 2016), ABB Connected Services/Ability (condition monitoring of robot gearboxes and controllers), and Siemens Senseye predictive maintenance. FANUC's ZDT claims to have prevented many unplanned downtimes at GM. These are OEM-controlled and keep data in OEM clouds. AI is so far reinforcing the OEM position rather than dissolving it, which contradicts C3's "gap is gone".

## Reasoning against the claims
- C1: mostly holds. Two differences: in factories "notice" is increasingly automated (alarms, condition monitoring), and a "migration/retrofit" branch replaces "source part" when parts are gone. So the chain is not always the same; a repair can turn into a re-engineering project.
- C2: mixed. OEM knowledge is concentrated (FANUC alarm codes, proprietary software, licensed tools). But most legacy pain is tacit plant-specific knowledge (uncommented code, retired staff) and physical scarcity (no new boards made), not an OEM monopoly. OEMs often publish manuals; the bottleneck is parts, tools and validated people.
- C3: contradicted. AI can read manuals and help interpret ladder logic, but it cannot create obsolete ICs, get code off an air-gapped S5, or sign off a safety function. OEM AI (ZDT, Senseye) entrenches OEM data control.
- C4: mixed. Diagnosis time could fall a lot, and downtime costs $39k-$2m/hr, so even modest cuts in mean time to repair are valuable. "Sourcing and delivering" a discontinued S5 card is bounded by global used stock and logistics, not information. Order-of-magnitude cuts are plausible for diagnosis and parts search, not for scarcity and validation.
- C5: contradicted for electronics. Obsolete ASICs, firmware-locked boards and servo amplifiers cannot be "printed". Board repair (component-level) and reverse-engineering clones exist, but they are slow and legally and technically fraught. Mechanical brackets, grippers and gears can be made by additive manufacturing or CNC; electronics and firmware are the binding constraint.
- C6: supports. Machine safety and GxP revalidation dominate migration decisions.
- C7: mixed. The system is fragmented and slow. But OEMs publish lifecycle roadmaps years ahead, FANUC promises lifetime parts, and a functioning secondary market exists. The inefficiency is partly a rational choice: plants trade obsolescence risk against capex, downtime windows and revalidation cost. "Built to be inefficient" overstates it; "built around OEM lock-in and deferred capex" fits better.
