# v06: Medical device servicing: FDA 2018 report on servicing by third parties, ISOs vs OEMs for imaging equipment, the 2020 ventilator repair crisis; cost and safety evidence

Researched 2026-10-20. Primary document read in full where possible: the FDA FDARA 710 report (May 2018, downloaded and text-extracted), and the U.S. PIRG Ed Fund "Hospital Repair Restrictions" report (July 2020, text-extracted). Other items are from trade press, court/law-firm summaries, SEC filings and vendor material, flagged as such.

## Findings

### 1. FDA 2018 servicing report (primary, regulator)
Source: FDA, "FDA Report on the Quality, Safety, and Effectiveness of Servicing of Medical Devices" (FDARA s.710), May 2018. https://www.fda.gov/media/113431/download?attachment
- Conclusions (verbatim bullets): the "currently available objective evidence is not sufficient to conclude whether or not there is a widespread public health concern related to servicing, including by third party servicers"; "many OEMs and third party entities provide high quality, safe, and effective servicing"; "a majority of comments, complaints, and adverse event reports alleging that inadequate 'servicing' caused or contributed to clinical adverse events and deaths actually pertain to 'remanufacturing'"; "The continued availability of third party entities to service and repair medical devices is critical to the functioning of the U.S. healthcare system."
- MAUDE review (all MDRs since 1992 to June 2017): 4,301 MDRs mentioned a third-party servicer: 40 deaths, 294 serious injuries, 3,791 malfunctions, 176 other. 4,240 of the 4,301 were filed by manufacturers (i.e., OEMs reporting on rivals' work); 25 from user facilities. Only 3 deaths could be definitively tied to servicing: two were field service engineers killed while servicing a CT and an MRI scanner, one a patient lift rail reinstalled incorrectly. A fourth death came from an uncleared remanufactured imaging system (camera fell on patient).
- Complaints to CDRH since 2009: 68 relevant; 28 were remanufacturing; of the 40 servicing complaints, 29 alleged inadequate servicing by OEMs vs 10 by ISOs; only one reported actual injury (infusion pump repaired by ISO with allegedly defective parts).
- Market structure: an estimated 16,520-20,830 firms service medical devices in the US (D&B SIC extrapolation; 4,791 directly found).
- Healthcare establishments named three decision factors for choosing a servicer: quality, cost, timeliness. 186 docket comments.
- Planned actions: promote quality management, clarify servicing vs remanufacturing, cybersecurity, foster evidence. No new regulation.
- Context: CMS (2011/2013 memos) requires hospitals to follow manufacturer-recommended maintenance or a documented Alternative Equipment Maintenance program; TJC requires 100% completion of high-risk PM and (from 2018) a library of service manuals for all devices. So the inspection / PM schedule is itself regulated and OEM-anchored.

### 2. ECRI analysis inside the FDA report (study)
Same source, s.5.3. ECRI searched 2,114,303 MAUDE records 2006-2015 (capital equipment), 528 HDA tracker records and 692 ECRI accident investigations. Servicing-attributable: 86 MDRs (0.004%), 4 HDA reports (0.8%), 6 investigations (0.9%). ECRI: "we do not believe that a safety problem exists with the servicing, maintenance, and repair of medical devices by either third-party organizations or OEMs." (PIRG rounds this as 0.005%.)

### 3. OEM / industry counter-view (oem_view)
- AdvaMed at the FDA workshop: untrained personnel, parts of unknown provenance can adulterate a device, no compliance with servicing standards; "lack of mandatory MDR reporting by ISOs creates a gap in MDR data, masking adverse events." Supported subjecting servicing to parts of the QS regulation. (FDA report p.15-16)
- MITA: third-party servicers operate in a "regulatory grey area", should register with FDA and report adverse events (Medical Device Servicing, Safety, and Accountability Act, 2017); developed NEMA/MITA servicing standard for imaging. A MITA representative said the report "merely proved that there wasn't enough evidence being collected." https://radiologybusiness.com/topics/healthcare-policy/mita-agrees-new-fda-report-medical-device-servicing-calls-congress-act ; https://www.raps.org/resource/cdrh-turns-down-industry-call-for-regulatory-contr.html
- Assessment: the absence-of-evidence critique is legitimate (ISOs have no MDR obligation), but the evidence that does exist (complaints) skews toward OEM servicing problems, and the MDR evidence is overwhelmingly OEM-filed.

### 4. FDA remanufacturing guidance, May 10, 2024 (regulator)
Final guidance "Remanufacturing of Medical Devices" (draft June 2021): servicing (maintenance/repair restoring to original spec) remains outside FDA device-manufacturer requirements; remanufacturing (significantly changing performance, safety specs or intended use) requires full QS compliance, including for third parties. Self-assessment by servicers via flowchart and examples. https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/05/fda-clarifies-distinction-between-device-remanufacturing-and-servicing-in-final-guidance ; https://fda.gov/media/181931/download
Relevance to C5: making a bespoke non-OEM replacement part that changes spec can tip a repair into "remanufacturing" with full manufacturer obligations. The bottleneck for an "AI parts factory" in medical is regulatory classification and validation, not fabrication.

### 5. PIRG 2020 "Hospital Repair Restrictions" survey (campaigner, survey)
Source: Proctor & O'Reilly, U.S. PIRG Ed Fund, July 2020. https://publicinterestnetwork.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Hospital_Repair_Restrictions_USPEF_7.8.20.pdf
- n = 222 biomedical professionals, distributed via biomed associations (self-selected, advocacy framing).
- 91.8% had been denied service information for "critical equipment (defibrillators, ventilators, anesthesia machines, imaging equipment, etc.)"; 16.9% "most of the time", 47.5% "somewhat frequently".
- 88.7% reported OEMs refused to sell spare parts (4.5% most of the time, 36.2% somewhat frequently, 48.0% infrequently).
- 30.4% had equipment that could not be used due to restrictions on parts/info.
- 69.5% handle ventilator repair in-house; of those, 29.2% had ventilators they currently could not use for lack of parts/service info; 51.9% had ventilators they could not service on-site if they broke; 24.2% denied ventilator repair info since March 2020; 48.8% denied "critical repair information, parts or service keys" since March.
- Restriction mechanisms: password/service keys to read diagnostics; manuals withheld or tied to service contract; calibration software required to activate new spare parts but not released (Drager Evita example; "common ... especially for imaging equipment" per IAMERS); ultrasound with tamper switch locking machine until OEM resets it; GE required a 4-day in-person course to access ventilator service info (waived in pandemic after pressure); Siemens forbids trained techs training colleagues; one device's training cost $28,000 vs ~$35,000 device price (~80%); GE reportedly has a "dumbed-down" manual for hospitals and full manual for internal use.
- Geisinger (Barbara Maguire, 80,000+ devices): three trained in-house techs returned 50 stored ventilators to service in one weekend; OEM-only parts routes mean waiting for a dispatched OEM engineer. Dallas-area clinical engineering director: CT down, OEM delayed, biomeds "not allowed to work on the equipment."
- Biomeds rely on online forums and Frank's Hospital Workshop (Tanzania) for manuals; Drager and Tecme sent takedowns; Steris sent iFixit takedown (May 2020).

### 6. Later PIRG surveys (campaigner)
"More than three out of four" biomeds denied parts or manuals in prior three months; 8 in 10 had equipment on site they could not service due to restricted service keys/parts (2021 follow-up); 67% of medical repair professionals say they or their team fixed a device the manufacturer could not. https://pirg.org/articles/hospital-technicians-renew-urgent-call-for-right-to-repair-medical-equipment/ ; https://pirg.org/media-center/two-thirds-of-surveyed-medical-equipment-repair-people-say-they-have-fixed-machinery-the-manufacturer-could-not/

### 7. Courts: aftermarket foreclosure is a recurrent, litigated pattern (court/history)
- Eastman Kodak v. Image Technical Services, 504 U.S. 451 (1992): 18 ISOs servicing Kodak copiers/micrographics; Court held lack of power in the equipment market does not preclude market power in parts/service aftermarkets; on remand, $71.8M treble-damages verdict reviewed by 9th Cir. (125 F.3d 1195, 1997). https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/504/451
- Red Lion Medical Safety v. General Electric (E.D. Tex., April 2017): jury found GE violated antitrust law in GE anesthesia machine servicing by refusing to train ISO technicians on new models (training being precondition to service materials) and by moving parts sales to a sole distributor (Alpha Source) in 2011; $43.8M actual damages trebled to $131.4M; 17 plaintiffs, most with 2-4 employees. https://www.competitionpolicyinternational.com/us-mckool-smith-secures-131-4-million-antitrust-verdict-against-ge/
- Philips North America v. Summit Imaging (W.D. Wash. 2019-): Philips sued an ISO under DMCA anti-circumvention for software that accessed log files/enabled features on Philips ultrasound; Summit's antitrust counterclaims were dismissed; settled. Summit's 2021 Copyright Office filing says Philips filed "numerous lawsuits against ISOs stating DMCA claims." https://healthimaging.com/topics/healthcare-policy/philips-alleges-imaging-company-hacked-machines ; https://www.copyright.gov/1201/2021/comments/Class%2012_InitialComments_Summit%20Imaging.pdf

### 8. Copyright Office DMCA s.1201 exemption for medical device repair (regulator)
Granted 2021, renewed Oct 28, 2024 (37 CFR 201.40(b)(15)) allowing circumvention to access diagnostic/repair data. FDA letter (June 21, 2024) supported it only for "data access for the purpose of diagnosis, maintenance, or repair" not modification. AdvaMed opposed renewal. MITA challenged it in court; D.C. Cir. (June 2024) allowed APA challenge; a later summary (Sept 2026, unverified) says the appeals court upheld the exemption. https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20240701-copyright-development-challenge-to-medical-device-right-to-repair-permitted-under-apa ; https://copyright.gov/1201/2024/USCO-letters/2024-DMCA-Copyright-Engagement-Cover-Note-6-21-2024-Final.pdf
Imaging ISOs argued in renewal petitions that "access to software-generated error logs and diagnostic data is often critical to accurately diagnosing equipment failures." Key point: the diagnostic knowledge sits behind a technical/legal lock on the machine's own data, not just in a manual.

### 9. 2020 ventilator crisis: what actually happened (news/history)
- Medtronic released PB560 manuals, design docs and schematics (Mar 30, 2020) to let others manufacture. https://news.medtronic.com/2020-03-30-Medtronic-Shares-Ventilation-Design-Specifications-to-Accelerate-Efforts-to-Increase-Global-Ventilator-Production
- iFixit built a crowd-sourced medical repair manual database with hundreds of volunteers; Steris threatened copyright action; EFF refused on iFixit's behalf. https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/06/medical-device-repair-again-threatened-copyright-claims
- GE, Medtronic, Fisher & Paykel released some manuals and waived in-person training after 43,915 petition signatures (pandemic-time-limited in GE's case).
- Strategic National Stockpile: maintenance contract (Vyaire) lapsed late Aug 2019; award to Agiliti protested, stop-work, re-awarded Jan 24, 2020; ~2,109 ventilators unavailable, HHS accelerated restoration to April 30. Root cause was procurement/contracting, not knowledge. https://doggett.house.gov/media/in-the-news/new-york-times-ventilator-stockpile-one-hitch-thousands-do-not-work
- By Aug-Sep 2020 the US had a glut: 95,713 ventilators in the stockpile vs 15,057 distributed; HHS cancelled ~38,000 units; clinicians used ventilators as last resort. The binding constraints were clinical demand and ICU staffing, not repair. https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2020/may/10/becoming-king-of-ventilators-may-result-in-unexpec/ ; https://www.pressreader.com/usa/milwaukee-journal-sentinel/20200903/281655372471051
- Wyden-Clarke "Critical Medical Infrastructure Right-to-Repair Act" (Aug 2020): would have required info/tools on fair terms, shielded copyright/DMCA liability during COVID, and let owners fabricate patented parts non-commercially; did not pass. https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/press-releases/wyden-and-clarke-introduce-bill-to-eliminate-barriers-to-fixing-critical-medical-equipment-during-the-pandemic-
- Isinnova (Italy, Mar 2020) reverse-engineered and 3D-printed Venturi valves when supply ran out; reports that the OEM refused CAD and threatened suit were denied by Intersurgical; later the Italian Health Ministry reportedly told Isinnova not to share the Charlotte valve file on certification grounds. https://www.dezeen.com/2020/03/19/3d-printers-valve-ventilators-hospital-coronavirus/ Shows bespoke part fabrication is fast, but certification/liability governs whether it is allowed.

### 10. Cost: OEM service is a high-margin profit pool (data)
- GE HealthCare FY2024: services sales $6,597M of $19,672M total (33.5%); services gross margin ~51.6% vs products ~36.7% (computed from cost of services $3,196M). https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1932393/000193239325000004/release4q24earnings.htm
- Siemens Healthineers reports ~55% recurring revenue (2016 deck); third-party estimate of ~EUR 6.8bn service revenue FY2024 (unverified).
- Imaging service contracts run roughly 10% of acquisition price per year for a ~$1.5M MRI/CT (IMV/AuntMinnie); 23% of 401 IMV survey respondents said service agreements cost too much. https://www.auntminnie.com/clinical-news/molecular-imaging/nuclear-medicine/article/15595112/rising-service-costs-prompt-hospitals-to-examine-new-options
- ISOs up to 30% cheaper than OEMs (Millennium Research Group via HealthExec; methodology unseen). Vendor claims 20-40%. TRIMEDX (vendor case study): nine-hospital system spent >50% of repair budget on OEM contracts; eliminating 56% of OEM contracts via in-house training saved >$10M/yr. Stanford reportedly saved $1M by making in-house team first responders on ultrasound.
- No peer-reviewed controlled cost or uptime comparison of ISO vs OEM imaging service was found. This is itself a finding: evidence is thin on both sides.

### 11. Downtime cost (data, partly vendor)
Siemens Medicare-based calculator (via Becker's): CT downtime ~$5,972/day, MR ~$14,618/day. Vendor estimates run far higher ($10k-$100k/day for MRI). https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/hospital-management-administration/cost-cutter-or-value-killer-6-questions-hospitals-need-to-ask-for-equipment-service-cost-savings.html

### 12. OEM remote diagnostics already automate "diagnose" (oem_view)
Philips: ~50% of corrective maintenance cases diagnosed and resolved remotely, ~88% first-time-right fix rate; older page 82% of cases received remote support, uptime guarantees up to 98%. https://www.philips.com/healthcare/services/maintenance-services/remote-services (vendor claims). Implication: the OEM holds the telemetry and the remote-fix capability; AI-driven diagnosis currently strengthens whoever owns the machine data.

### 13. End-of-service-life (EoSL) letters and parts (practitioner_view, vendor-sourced)
OEMs issue end-of-support notices, withdraw contracts, service keys and thin parts inventory; third-party refurbished/harvested parts can keep popular models running for years; but "there does come a point where old equipment is really end of support"; new CT tubes can cost nearly as much as the machine is worth; rural hospitals may close imaging after EoSL. https://blockimaging.com/end-of-life-and-end-of-service ; https://www.christieinnomed.com/not-your-last-images-outliving-your-end-of-support-letter ; https://www.radiologytoday.net/news/enews_0720_1.shtml

### 14. AI tools for biomeds are arriving (study/oem_view, 2026)
- TRIMEDX "technician agent" (May 2026) in its CMMS: natural-language Q&A over error codes, repair histories, alert remediation, cybersecurity guidance. Vendor-reported, no independent evaluation. https://www.trimedx.com/resource/trimedx-introduces-new-agentic-ai-capabilities
- arXiv 2601.16967 (Jan 2026): LLM support platform for biomeds in LMICs; on a Philips HDI 5000 ultrasound proof of concept, 100% precision on error-code interpretation, 80% accuracy suggesting corrective actions. Small, single device. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16967v1
- AAMI offering "AI Agents for HTM Professionals" training (July 2026), framing the problem as thousands of pages of unstructured manuals plus disconnected CMMS logs.
- 80% corrective-action accuracy is useful as a copilot but not "gap gone" for life-support equipment.

### 15. Labor is a binding constraint (data)
AAMI 2021: 47% of HTM professionals over 50; 2022 survey (n>1,400): 60% over 45, >6% past retirement age; at least 30 BMET programs closed in five years, 23 states with no BMET program; ~400 formal graduates/yr vs ~7,300 projected openings/yr (secondary figure). AAMI/TechNation State of HTM 2025 (n~1,000): recruiting shortfalls and retirements are top challenges. https://aami.org/news/the-state-of-htm-2025-report-signals-changing-health-tech-workforce/ ; https://www.dotmed.com/news/story/55239

## Reasoning / synthesis

- The medical-device case is the cleanest test of C2 vs. an alternative "access monopoly" hypothesis. The FDA and ECRI evidence says independents and in-house biomeds can repair safely; the PIRG and court evidence says what they lack is not general know-how but (a) the device's own diagnostic data behind service keys/passwords/DMCA, (b) calibration/activation software for new parts, (c) OEM-gated training, (d) parts sold only to OEM channels. These are contractual, technical and legal locks. AI can compress (e) generic knowledge (manuals, error-code interpretation, tacit tricks) but cannot unlock (a)-(d). So C2 is supported but reframed: knowledge is monopolised by access control, and C3 is contradicted in its strong form.
- Diagnosis is being automated, but by OEMs (remote service ~50% resolved remotely at Philips). AI raises the value of telemetry ownership, which can deepen the monopoly unless right-to-repair law forces data access. The 1201 exemption is the legal hinge.
- Where the money goes: hospital capital equipment service is mostly labor + OEM contract margin + parts (tubes, coils, boards) and downtime. Service is ~1/3 of GE HealthCare revenue at ~52% gross margin, versus 37% on products, so the aftermarket is the profit centre. ISO discounts of 20-40% suggest the cost compression available from competition is tens of percent, not 10x. Downtime cost (~$6-15k/day per scanner) and waiting for an OEM engineer dominate in the anecdotes.
- C4 order-of-magnitude: no evidence. Plausible savings from opening access: 20-40% on service spend, large reductions in time-to-repair in specific cases (50 ventilators in a weekend). Safety inspection/PM is regulated by CMS/TJC at 100% completion for high-risk equipment and cannot be skipped.
- C5: the 2020 episodes (Isinnova, PB560, Wyden-Clarke fabrication clause) show fabrication capacity exists; the binding constraints are IP/copyright, FDA's servicing-vs-remanufacturing line and certification/liability. For imaging, the hard parts (X-ray tubes, detectors, MRI magnets/coils) are not things a generic AI parts factory makes.
- C6: strongly supported. CMS/TJC PM and inspection regimes, FDA quality management push, and MITA's call for registration/MDR reporting. The real evolving piece is evidence generation: no one systematically measures servicing outcomes; ISOs have no MDR duty. An AI-era inspection/traceability layer (service records, parts provenance, MDR-like reporting for ISOs) is a gap that both sides say is missing.
- C7: mixed. The industry is not "built to be inefficient" by accident; it is built to capture aftermarket rent (Kodak 1992, Red Lion 2017, Philips DMCA suits) with safety as the stated rationale. Evidence supports structural rent extraction, not that the whole thing must be rebuilt: FDA explicitly says the current mixed OEM/ISO/in-house system works and is "critical." Also the 2020 ventilator "crisis" turned out to be a procurement lapse plus a demand overshoot, not a repair-knowledge collapse.
- C1: the chain fits the hospital workflow (CMMS work order, troubleshoot, parts, repair, performance verification/electrical safety test, return to service), but medical adds a parallel continuous scheduled-PM loop (inspection triggered by calendar, not failure) and a regulatory classification step (is this servicing or remanufacturing?). Also "notice" is increasingly done by OEM remote monitoring before the user notices.

## Surprises
- 4,240 of the 4,301 MDRs mentioning third-party servicers were filed by manufacturers.
- FDA complaints alleging inadequate servicing named OEMs (29) roughly three times as often as ISOs (10).
- Two of the three servicing-attributable deaths were service engineers killed on CT/MRI, i.e., worker safety, not patient safety.
- A device training course cost ~80% of the device ($28k vs $35k).
- The US ended 2020 with ~96k stockpiled ventilators and cancelled ~38k units; ~2,109 SNS ventilators were offline due to a contract protest, not lack of knowledge.
- OEMs already resolve ~half of imaging faults remotely; AI diagnosis may entrench OEMs.
