## Findings

### F1. Frontdoor (American Home Shield): the P&L of a repair aggregator. [data, 10-K FY2025]
- Revenue **$2,093M** (+14%, of which ~$188M came from the 2-10 HBW acquisition).
- Cost of services rendered **$936M** (45% of revenue). Gross profit $1,157M (**55% gross margin**).
- Selling and administrative expense **$669M** (32%):
  - sales and marketing $315M
  - customer service $117M
  - G&A $236M
- Net income $255M. Adjusted EBITDA $553M.
- Volume:
  - ~**3.8M home warranty service requests a year**
  - ~**2.1M active warranties**
  - "almost two claims per year, on average" per customer
- Network: ~**17,000 independent contractor firms**. About 4,200 of them are "preferred" and completed **84%** of service requests. No contractor accounts for more than 5% of cost of services.
- Parts: direct supplier spend is ~**22% of cost of services**. Six national suppliers each take more than 5% of supplier spend. Contractor-bought parts are excluded from that figure.
- 76% of revenue comes from renewals, and 84% of customers are on monthly auto-pay. Customer retention in 2024 (excluding 2-10 HBW) was 78.5%.
- Derived: cost of services ÷ requests ≈ **$246 per service request** (an upper bound, because the builder-warranty costs are included). Revenue per warranty is roughly $900 a year.
- Interpretation: a ~$900/yr subscription pays for ~2 dispatches at ~$250 each. The margin comes from **pricing, claim mix, weather and contractor rate negotiation**, not from cheaper diagnosis.

### F2. Frontdoor's gross margin swings with weather, inflation and pricing, not technology. [data, 10-K FY2023 and FY2025]
Gross margin by year:

| Year | Gross margin | Revenue | Cost of services |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 49% | $1,602M | $818M |
| 2022 | **43%** | | $952M |
| 2023 | 50% | | $895M |
| 2024 | 54% | | |
| 2025 | 55% | | |

- 2022's squeeze came from inflation and supply-chain parts costs. The filing says: "industry-wide parts and equipment availability challenges and inflation can impact our ability to estimate the cost to settle claims."
- The 2025 explanation for flat claims cost: "continued process improvement initiatives, a lower number of service requests per customer, a favorable weather impact of $7 million and higher trade service fees, offset, in part, by inflationary cost pressures."
- Read: what moves the economics is **parts prices, labor rates, weather-driven HVAC volume and customer-paid trade fees**. The knowledge layer barely registers.

### F3. Frontdoor already runs remote and AI diagnosis, and it is incremental, not transformative. [data and company view]
- The 10-K describes "video chat capabilities, augmented reality, computer vision and machin[e] learning to offer real time help to homeowners from our Virtual Experts who may be able to guide homeowners to fix the repair, reducing the need for an in-home visit."
- Frontdoor owned this through Streem (acquired around 2019).
- The FY2023 10-K says that in 2022 it "began to shift the focus of our Streem technology platform … to primarily concentrate on integrating the technology into our core product offerings, including the Frontdoor app." It also said it intended to **discontinue the third-party SaaS business by end-2024**.
- The 2023 Frontdoor app launch "required a substantial marketing investment". By the FY2025 10-K the app appears only in a risk factor about rebranding initiatives that may not work, and virtual support now sits inside the American Home Shield app.
- No volume of visits avoided is disclosed. If remote diagnosis were removing a large share of truck rolls, an incumbent with ~3.8M dispatches a year would be expected to disclose it.
- This is the most direct counter-evidence to C3/C4 in this domain: a scaled incumbent with the data, the motive and the tools has had AR/ML remote diagnosis for ~6 years. Its cost of services still tracks labor and parts.

### F4. Angi: a lead-selling marketing business with a 95% gross margin, not a repair business. [data, 10-K FY2025]
- Revenue:
  - 2023: $1,359M
  - 2024: $1,185M
  - 2025: **$1,031M** (−13%)
- Gross margin 95%.
- Selling and marketing:

| Year | Selling and marketing | Share of revenue |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 | $765M | ~56% |
| 2024 | $602M | 51% |
| 2025 | $508M | **49%** |

- Operating income: 2023 −$26M, 2024 $22M, 2025 $65M.
- U.S. revenue is lead fees "paid by Pros for consumer matches (**regardless of whether the Pro ultimately provides the requested service**)", plus advertising and memberships.
- In 2025, total service requests were **15.5M (−10%)** and leads were **20.2M (−17%)**. Network-channel leads fell **−74%** after Angi introduced "homeowner choice" in January 2025.
- Average monthly active Pros fell from 152K to **122K (−19%)**. Acquired Pros fell from 141K to **90K (−36%)**.
- Angi launched a new online acquisition funnel for Pros "in expectation of establishing a new channel for **Pro capacity growth** in 2026". The binding constraint is pro supply, not consumer knowledge.

### F5. Angi's service-request volume has been shrinking for years. [data, 10-K FY2022]
- Service requests:
  - 2020: 33.9M
  - 2021: 33.5M
  - 2022: 29.5M (−12%)
- Transacting service professionals fell from 251K to **220K** in 2022.
- Revenue per request rose through price increases ("price increases implemented during the second quarter of 2022").
- The definitions changed after 2023, so the 2025 figure of 15.5M is not strictly comparable.

### F6. When Angi tried to own fulfillment (fixed-price "Angi Services" and Roofing), it lost money and retreated to being a lead broker. [data, 10-K FY2022]
2022 segment Adjusted EBITDA:

| Segment | 2022 Adjusted EBITDA |
|---|---|
| Ads and Leads | **+$169.0M** |
| Services | **−$52.1M**, on $381M revenue |
| Roofing | **−$21.4M**, plus a $26M goodwill impairment |

- Services cost of revenue rose $64M, "primarily [from] a $58.1 million increase in payments to third-party professional service providers."
- From 1 January 2023, Angi changed its terms so that "the service professional, rather than Angi, Inc., has the contractual relationship with the consumer to deliver the service", and moved Services to net revenue accounting.
- Angi later reported a single U.S. segment. The FY2025 10-K lists Handy only as a brand.
- Read: owning the dispatch and the job brings labor cost, quality risk, warranty and guarantee liability, and misclassification exposure. Angi decided the job was not worth owning.

### F7. Sears Home Services: halving of the largest in-house appliance-repair fleet in the U.S. [data, Sears Holdings 10-Ks]

| | FY2007 | FY2017 |
|---|---|---|
| Service technicians | **over 10,000** | **~5,200** |
| Service calls per year | over 13M | over 5M |
| Households with an active relationship | 43M+ | ~30M |
| Calls per technician per year | ~1,300 | ~960 |

- In FY2017 Sears was still "the nation's No. 1 provider of appliance and product repair services", through Sears Parts & Repair and A&E Factory Service (a Whirlpool JV). It also ran searspartsdirect.com, which sold DIY parts with instructions.
- Derived: calls per technician fell about 26%. Assuming ~250 working days, that is roughly **5.2 → 3.8 calls per technician per day**. Utilization fell as density fell, which is the classic route-density death spiral: fewer Sears appliance sales meant fewer protection agreements, sparser jobs per territory, more drive time per job and higher cost per call.
- The 10-K also notes that fuel for "the vehicles used by technicians" is a material cost exposure.
- Sears Holdings filed Chapter 11 on 15 October 2018. ESL bought the assets in February 2019, and they went into Transform Holdco (Transformco). [secondary: Wikipedia, Sears Holdings]
- ServiceLive.com (launched 2008) was an early Sears contractor marketplace charging 10% of the contract price, with 40,000+ listed contractors. [secondary]
- Lesson: an OEM- and retailer-captive repair network depends on the retail installed base for **density**. Its decline was driven by retail collapse and density loss, not by a knowledge advantage eroding.

### F8. Gig-model home-service marketplaces repeatedly failed on unit economics, disintermediation and worker classification. [secondary: Wikipedia, Homejoy and Handy]
- Homejoy (YC 2010) raised ~$38M and shut down in July 2015. Reasons given:
  - worker-classification lawsuits
  - "difficulties in maintaining profitability"
  - workers leaving to arrange jobs directly with clients
  - $19 first-clean promotions against a ~$85 market price
  - in the UK, a £13/hr price with cleaners paid £7–9.5
- Handy raised ~$107M. It was sued in 2014 over contractor misclassification and sold to ANGI Homeservices in October 2018 for **$165.5M**, close to its total capital raised. It was then folded into Angi Services, which lost money (F6).
- Angi's FY2025 10-K still lists worker-classification law as a principal risk.
- Read: dispatch is hard to scale because:
  - repeat jobs **disintermediate** (once the homeowner has a good plumber's number, the platform is bypassed)
  - local liquidity is needed trade by trade and town by town
  - labor law limits how much control a platform can exert over technicians

### F9. Thumbtack: a large valuation on lead revenue, now repositioning toward AI agents. [secondary: Wikipedia, citing Fast Company and Forbes]
- Valued at $3.2B in July 2021, after a $275M round.
- ~**$400M revenue in FY2024**.
- ~300K active pros (June 2025).
- Took $75M of debt from SVB in July 2024.
- Became an OpenAI Operator research partner and one of the first ChatGPT app integrations in January 2025, and partnered with Alexa in February 2025.
- Read: the AI play for these marketplaces is in **demand capture and matching**, protecting their position as AI search replaces Google SEO and paid search. It is not about technician capability.

### F10. Angi itself names AI as a threat to its model, through DIY and search disruption. [company view, 10-K FY2025]
Quote: "AI could be utilized to better educate homeowners on how to perform their own home improvement projects, thereby reducing the need for our business, or AI could eventually transform the way search engines currently work."
- Frontdoor's FY2025 risk factors likewise list "technological developments in artificial intelligence" and generative-AI search effects on paid search.
- This **partly supports C2/C3**: the information and matching layer, where these firms earn their margins, is exposed to AI.
- It is a threat to **lead-generation rent**, not to the cost of fixing. The money at risk is Angi's ~$500M a year of marketing-funded matching, not the plumber's labor.

### F11. ServiceTitan: the value pool sits in contractor operating software, and dispatch optimization is already AI-assisted. [data, 10-K FY2026]
- Gross transaction volume (GTV) invoiced through the platform: **$82.1B** in FY2026, up from $68.5B.
- ~10,800 Active Customers (each with more than $10K of annualized billings).
- Products:
  - "Dispatch Pro" uses AI on technician history (sales, average ticket, memberships, reassignment) "to choose the best-matched technician for a job, while also optimizing technician routes"
  - "Atlas", an agentic AI layer launched in FY2026
  - "Second Chance Leads" and an AI contact center
- Risk factors cite "the increased prevalence of marketplaces for contractors, supply chain issues, tariffs … and labor shortages."
- Read: dispatch and scheduling intelligence is being commoditized inside contractor ERP and field-service software. The remaining constraint is the **technician's hours and travel**. The software's selling point is revenue per technician (selling memberships, upsells), which is not the same as cheaper repairs for owners.

### F12. FTC v. HomeAdvisor: lead-quality misrepresentation to small trades firms. [regulator, as described in Angi's FY2022 10-K]
- The FTC filed an administrative complaint on 11 March 2022, alleging that HomeAdvisor's practices on leads sold to service professionals, and its mHelpDesk product, were unfair or deceptive. Summary decision was denied in August 2022, and a consent order was negotiated in December 2022.
- [recall, not re-verified this run] The final order in 2023 required ~$7.2M in payments and changes to how lead quality is represented.
- Read: the marketplace "inefficiency" is partly **misaligned incentives**. The platform is paid per lead, not per completed repair, which is a structural inefficiency (supports C7 in kind). But the problem is incentive design, not missing knowledge.

## Why technician dispatch is hard to scale (synthesis)
1. **Labor-hour bound.**
   - Each repair needs a licensed or skilled person on site.
   - Throughput per technician is ~4–5 calls a day: Sears ~5.2/day in 2007 and ~3.8/day in 2017.
   - Drive time and parts-return trips use up the day.
   - Software (ServiceTitan Dispatch Pro, routing) optimizes at the margin.
2. **Density economics.**
   - Cost per call depends on jobs per square mile per trade.
   - Sears lost density as its retail base shrank and halved its fleet.
   - Marketplaces have to build liquidity trade by trade and metro by metro.
3. **Disintermediation.**
   - Homeowners re-hire good technicians directly (Homejoy).
   - Warranty firms prevent this with a prepaid subscription and a trade service fee; marketplaces fight it with lead fees charged regardless of outcome.
4. **Supply scarcity.**
   - Angi's active pros fell 19% and acquired pros 36% in 2025, and it is building a "Pro capacity" funnel.
   - ServiceTitan cites labor shortages.
   - Frontdoor depends on 4,200 preferred firms for 84% of jobs.
5. **Liability and labor law.**
   - Owning the job (Angi Services, Handy, Homejoy) brings misclassification suits, guarantee expense and quality liability.
   - Angi pulled back to net, lead-only accounting in 2023.
6. **Customer acquisition dominates opex.**
   - Angi spends ~50% of revenue on selling and marketing.
   - Frontdoor spends $315M on sales and marketing plus $117M on customer service, against $936M of claims cost.
   - Much of the "inefficiency" is the cost of **finding and convincing customers**, not of diagnosing.

## Where the money goes (home repair at platform scale)
- **Frontdoor:** roughly 45% of revenue goes to contractor labor plus parts and equipment (direct parts ~22% of that 45%), ~15% to sales and marketing, ~6% to customer service, ~11% to G&A, and ~26% is EBITDA. Diagnosis is not a separate line: it is bundled into the dispatch. The trade service fee (~$100–125 per call, from recall) is in effect the price of a truck roll.
- **Angi:** ~5% cost of revenue, ~49% selling and marketing. The consumer then pays the pro separately for labor and parts, outside Angi's P&L.

## Stance assessment
- **C1 (one universal chain): mixed.** Warranty claims do run notice → request → dispatch → diagnose → parts → repair. But the chain misses two stages that are costly in this domain:
  - **customer acquisition and matching**: about half of Angi's revenue
  - **coverage adjudication**: authorize or deny, then repair versus replace
  
  There is also no formal safety inspection for most consumer repairs; licensing and permits stand in for it.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly drives inefficiency): mostly contradicts.**
  - The scarce inputs are technician hours, density and customer acquisition.
  - Frontdoor and Angi make money from **aggregation, risk pooling and lead brokerage**, not from proprietary repair knowledge.
  - Appliance OEM service knowledge was a real but minor factor: Sears ran a DIY parts-plus-instructions site for years.
- **C3 (AI closes the knowledge gap): contradicts as stated, though partly true for the information layer.**
  - Frontdoor has had AR/CV/ML virtual diagnosis since about 2019 and scaled back the standalone product.
  - Its cost of services still tracks labor, parts and weather.
  - AI threatens the **lead-gen rent** (Angi's own risk factor), not the cost of the fix.
- **C4 (an order-of-magnitude drop in diagnose, understand, source and deliver costs): contradicts.**
  - Those steps are a minority of cost per request. Labor time on site plus parts dominates.
  - Parts sourcing is already consolidated: six national suppliers, scale purchasing.
  - Even cutting diagnosis and sourcing to zero would leave the ~$250 truck roll and repair mostly in place.
- **C5 (an AI parts factory solves bespoke parts): silent.**
  - Home warranties favor **replacement** over custom fabrication.
  - The only related evidence is Frontdoor's risk factor that finding alternate sources if a supplier discontinues "would be time consuming, difficult and costly."
- **C6 (inspection remains and must evolve): mixed and mostly silent.**
  - Quality assurance is a real cost and control point: preferred-contractor scoring, Angi's guarantee expense, the FTC lead-quality case.
  - Formal safety inspection is largely absent from consumer home repair outside permitted HVAC and electrical work.
- **C7 (structurally inefficient, needs a rebuild): mixed.**
  - **Supports:** incentives are misaligned. Leads are paid "regardless of whether the Pro ultimately provides the requested service", warranty firms profit by minimizing claim cost, and the FTC acted.
  - **Contradicts:** every tech-first rebuild that took on fulfillment failed or retreated (Homejoy shut down, Handy sold at about capital raised, Angi Services −$52M EBITDA and moved to net accounting, the Frontdoor app was de-emphasized). The incumbents that survive are capital-light aggregators. The "rebuild" that is working is contractor-side software (ServiceTitan, $82B GTV) that raises technician productivity.

## Surprises
- Angi, the best-known "repair marketplace", spends ~half its revenue on marketing and has a 95% gross margin. It is a media and lead business.
- Sears' technician fleet halved in a decade even as it remained #1. The cause was density loss, not a lost knowledge edge.
- Frontdoor had AI/AR remote diagnosis in-house and chose to fold it into the core rather than build a business on it.
- Angi's 2025 move to "homeowner choice" cut network leads by 74%, showing how much prior volume was low-intent lead resale.

## Company-shape implications (for a YC founder in machines, energy or robotics)
- Not a better lead marketplace: thin, ad-funded, and exposed to AI search.
- Possibly **technician productivity**: AI-guided diagnosis and parts-correct-on-first-visit for field techs. That attacks repeat truck rolls, the real cost, but ServiceTitan and OEM portals are crowding the space.
- Possibly **risk pricing and warranty underwriting** using connected-device telemetry: predict failures, cut claims cost, and dispatch pre-emptively at better density.
- Possibly **robotics or remote-hands** to stretch scarce licensed-technician hours, for example one licensed tech supervising several junior or robotic workers. That attacks the actual bottleneck.
