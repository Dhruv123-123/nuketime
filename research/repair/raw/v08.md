# v08: Antitrust history of aftermarket lock-in (Kodak v Image Technical Services 1992, Xerox, IBM tying, Apple parts pairing) and what courts and economists (Shapiro, Borenstein) found about aftermarket monopolies

Researched 2026-10-20. Sources: court opinions, regulators (FTC, FDA, DoD IG), academic economics, practitioner law-firm and trade-press writing. Each finding is tagged as hard data, legal holding, or opinion.

## Bottom line

Aftermarket lock-in is a real, well-documented economic mechanism, and the evidence does support the founder's C2. But the scarce thing being monopolized is mostly **access** (parts supply, diagnostic software and authorization keys, manuals, IP rights, approval or certification gates). It is rarely **know-how** in the sense of "understanding how to fix it". The Kodak ISOs, the Xerox CSUs, SIS for EndoWrists, Deere's independent shops and PMA makers already had the know-how. In Kodak, ISOs were often cheaper and sometimes better. What they lacked was parts, software keys or legal permission. So AI closing the knowledge gap (C3) does not by itself break the lock. The binding constraints are legal, contractual, cryptographic and regulatory. The 30+ years of litigation also show that courts are a slow, unreliable remedy: Kodak was filed in 1987 and the jury verdict came in 1996. Defendants won almost all the Kodak-style cases that followed. Recent change has come from regulation and settlements: the FTC/Deere 2026 settlement, the $99M Deere class settlement, Oregon's parts-pairing ban and the EU Right to Repair Directive.

## Findings

### 1. Kodak (1992): the Supreme Court accepted that aftermarket monopolies exist even without power in the equipment market. [legal holding + record facts]
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, 504 U.S. 451 (1992), decided 6-3 (Blackmun for the majority; Scalia dissented, joined by O'Connor and Thomas).
- Record facts: Kodak provided 80-95% of service for Kodak machines and controlled nearly 100% of the parts market. The ISOs (18 respondents) offered service at "substantially lower" prices, and some customers found ISO service higher quality. Kodak's lack of equipment-market power was assumed for the case.
- Holding: whether information costs and switching costs "foil the simple assumption" that equipment and aftermarket are disciplined together is a question of fact. Lifecycle pricing is "difficult and costly", and high switching costs can lock in customers and allow supracompetitive aftermarket prices. Kodak's justifications (service quality, inventory cost, free-riding) did not win as a matter of law.
- Quote cited by commentators: "[m]uch of this information is difficult -- some of it impossible -- to acquire at the time of purchase."
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/504/451
- Relevance: the ISOs had the technical knowledge. What they lacked was parts. The monopoly was over the **input**, not over understanding.

### 2. Kodak on remand: the jury found liability, damages were trebled, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed despite patents. [court + news]
- A San Francisco jury found for the ISOs on Sept. 18, 1995/96. The base award was about $23.9M, trebled to about $71.7M (Diagnostic Imaging trade report; the Ninth Circuit's 1998 fee opinion describes a verdict "exceeding $23 million", trebled).
- Image Technical Services v. Eastman Kodak, 125 F.3d 1195 (9th Cir. 1997), affirmed Sherman Act sec. 2 liability. It accepted an "all parts" market in which many parts were unpatented, and held that IP protection was not a complete business justification for refusing to deal.
- The litigation ran about 10 years from filing (1987) to verdict, and about 11 to appellate affirmance.
- Sources: https://diagnosticimaging.com/view/kodak-loses-decision-lawsuitover-third-party-service ; DOJ IP hearings paper https://www.justice.gov/atr/public/hearings/ip/chapter_1.pdf

### 3. Xerox (Fed. Cir. 2000): the opposite result. A unilateral refusal to sell patented parts or license copyrighted manuals is lawful. [legal holding]
- In re Independent Service Organizations Antitrust Litigation (CSU v. Xerox), 203 F.3d 1322 (Fed. Cir. 2000); cert. denied 2001.
- Xerox refused to sell patented parts and copyrighted service manuals to ISOs unless they were end users. The court held that absent illegal tying, PTO fraud or sham litigation, a patent holder may refuse to sell or license, even when the refusal affects a second market, and that the holder's intent is irrelevant.
- This created a circuit split with the Ninth Circuit's Kodak remand. In practice, OEMs that put patents or copyrights on parts, software and manuals can lawfully withhold them.
- Sources: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/203/1322/474824/ ; https://robinskaplan.com/newsroom/insights/federal-circuit-closes-the-door-on-antitrust-refusal-to-deal-claim ; https://arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2001/04/antitrust-liability-for-unilateral-refusals-to-l__
- Relevance: this is the legal root of software-locked repair. Diagnostic software and manuals are copyrighted, and parts can be patented. The **service manuals** (the "knowledge") are themselves IP-protected, so a knowledge monopoly is partly a legal artefact, not only an information gap.

### 4. Post-Kodak lower courts: "twenty years and not much to show for it". [practitioner analysis]
- Jonathan Gleklen, ABA Antitrust magazine (Fall 2012): "with the exception of the Kodak case itself on remand, defendants have prevailed in almost all of the reported Kodak-style cases alleging anticompetitive conduct in aftermarkets."
- Cases against Digital Equipment (7th Cir., Easterbrook), Alcatel, Honeywell (PSI Repair Services v. Honeywell, 6th Cir. 1997) and others failed. Courts required a **change in policy after lock-in**: if the aftermarket restriction was disclosed or in place at the time of sale, there was no Kodak claim. Newcal v. IKON (9th Cir. 2008) was a rare plaintiff-friendly exception.
- AAI Working Paper 15-02 (Kozen, 2015): "most lower courts have ignored the Kodak holding and reasoning... As a result, there has been little, if any enforcement action involving aftermarkets."
- AAI's Keurig example: Keurig held 86-89% of the K-Cup aftermarket, charged a 50% margin on cartridges and was able to raise prices 9% in 2011. Only 22% of revenue came from brewers. 43% of brewers are bought as gifts, so lifecycle pricing by buyers is implausible.
- Sources: https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2012/12/the-iso-litigation-legacy-of-eastman-kodak-co-v-__ (PDF) ; https://www.antitrustinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/Working-Paper-No-15-02.pdf

### 5. Borenstein, MacKie-Mason and Netz: aftermarket prices exceed cost even when the equipment market is perfectly competitive. [study, theory]
- "Exercising Market Power in Proprietary Aftermarkets", J. Economics & Management Strategy 9(2) 2000, 157-188 (1996 working paper). Also "Antitrust Policy in Aftermarkets", 63 Antitrust L.J. 455 (1995).
- In all three equipment-market structures studied (perfect competition, differentiated duopoly, monopoly), the aftermarket price is above marginal cost. Aftermarket prices can even be **higher** when the equipment market is more competitive, which contradicts the "brand competition disciplines the aftermarket" defence. When firms can price-discriminate, erect entry barriers or affect upgrade timing, aftermarket monopoly is more likely to persist.
- The authors note that interbrand competition can dissipate some service profits through cheaper equipment, so part of the rent is competed away up front. That is a welfare transfer and a distortion, not necessarily a pure loss.
- Sources: https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/924047b8-0bd4-438f-9ec5-0ecdca730d76 ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jemstr/v9y2000i3p157-188.html ; working paper https://ftp.unpad.ac.id/orari/library/library-ref-eng/ref-eng-1/application/e-commerce/exercising-market-power-in-proprietary-aftermarkets-10-1996.pdf

### 6. Carl Shapiro (1995) and Shapiro and Teece (1994): harm is real but narrower, concentrated in surprise policy changes and poorly informed buyers. [study / economist view]
- Shapiro, "Aftermarkets and Consumer Welfare: Making Sense of Kodak", 63 Antitrust L.J. 483 (1995). Shapiro and Teece, "Systems competition and aftermarkets: an economic analysis of Kodak", Antitrust Bulletin 39(1) 1994.
- Shapiro's argument (summarised from my prior knowledge of the article; the full text was not retrieved in this session, so verify before quoting): (a) significant consumer harm from aftermarket power arises mainly when a firm **opportunistically changes policy** to exploit an installed base already locked in, or when many buyers cannot do lifecycle costing. (b) Where buyers are sophisticated and aftermarket terms are known in advance, a high aftermarket margin is largely offset by lower equipment prices, so net harm is small. (c) Courts should screen cases accordingly. This is essentially the "change in policy" filter the lower courts adopted (finding 4).
- Citation confirmed in AAI amicus brief, SIS v. Intuitive: https://www.antitrustinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/AAI-TSAC-SIS-v.-Intuitive.pdf
- Relevance: the economics debate is about **who bears the rent and whether it is anticipated**, not whether lock-in exists.

### 7. Cabral (2009-2011): aftermarket power need not hurt consumers, but it entrenches incumbents. [study, theory]
- Luis Cabral, "Aftermarket Power and Basic Market Competition" (NYU Stern working paper). With constant returns in the aftermarket, social and consumer surplus do not change with aftermarket power. With increasing returns, more aftermarket power gives higher social and consumer surplus **but** greater concentration in the basic market and higher barriers to entry. Large firms benefit and small firms lose.
- Source: https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lcabral/workingpapers/aftermarketsSep09.pdf
- This contradicts a simple reading that aftermarket monopoly always equals inefficiency. Its main cost is dynamic: entrenchment and barriers to entry for new OEMs.

### 8. IBM (1936, 1956): the earliest aftermarket tying cases. [legal history]
- IBM v. United States, 298 U.S. 131 (1936): IBM leased tabulators on condition that customers use only IBM punch cards. The Court held this violated Clayton Act sec. 3 and rejected patent and goodwill defences. IBM could set card specifications but could not require its own cards.
- The 1956 consent decree (S.D.N.Y., Jan. 25, 1956) required IBM to sell (not only lease) machines and to offer service and parts to owners on reasonable terms, cutting IBM to no more than half of US punched-card capacity. It was later extended to computers. My knowledge, not verified this session: the decree required IBM to sell parts and provide repair information so that independent maintenance could exist. It was phased out from 1996 to 2001.
- Sources: https://www.casemine.com/commentary/us/ibm-v.-united-states:-upholding-the-clayton-act's-prohibition-on-monopolistic-tying-clauses/view ; https://appliedantitrust.com/22_tying_arrangements.htm
- Relevance: the "consumable/part tie" (razor and blades) model is at least 90 years old, and the remedy then, as now, was compelled access to parts and service.

### 9. Intuitive Surgical (EndoWrist): the live, high-stakes medical-robotics version, revived in Aug 2026. [court]
- SIS alleged Intuitive tied da Vinci service to its EndoWrist instruments and blocked repaired or reset EndoWrists, which carry a usage-counter chip. SIS sought $140M, trebled to $420M.
- In January 2025 the district court granted judgment as a matter of law mid-trial for Intuitive, relying on Epic v. Apple's Kodak-factor requirements.
- On Aug 13, 2026 the Ninth Circuit (No. 25-1372) reversed. It held that a standard foremarket/aftermarket tying claim does not require Kodak/Epic lock-in factors when the defendant has near-100% share in the foremarket (surgical robots) and has gained 100% of the aftermarket. The evidence was sufficient for a jury to find that Intuitive's third-party approval process was "illusory".
- A parallel hospital class action (Larkin, Franciscan, King County/Valley Medical, Kaleida) won class certification.
- Sources: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/08/13/25-1372.pdf ; https://paulweiss.com/insights/client-news/intuitive-surgical-wins-high-stakes-antitrust-trial-regarding-da-vinci-surgical-system ; https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2513263 ; Intuitive 10-Q https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1035267/000103526725000209/isrg-20250930.htm
- Relevance for robotics founders: a chip-based usage limit plus control of FDA-style clearance or "approval" is the modern lock. The bottleneck was a counter chip and regulatory clearance, not knowledge of how to repair a wrist instrument.

### 10. Deere: the FTC's first big repair monopolization case, settled in 2026. [regulator + settlement]
- FTC v. Deere (N.D. Ill., Jan. 2025; Commission vote 3-2). Deere allegedly holds a "100% market share" for repairs that require the fully functional dealer tool, Service ADVISOR. The farmer version, Customer Service ADVISOR, cannot do all repairs. PIRG found it withholds "payload files" needed to authorise parts replacements electronically. Dealers almost always use Deere parts. Restrictions delay planting and harvest.
- June 2025: the motion to dismiss / judgment on the pleadings was denied, including Deere's argument that no cognizable aftermarket was pleaded.
- April 2026: Deere agreed to a $99M class settlement covering anyone who paid Deere or its dealers for large ag equipment repairs since Jan. 10, 2018, plus injunctive relief on repair resources. Deere denies wrongdoing.
- July 8, 2026: FTC and five states (AZ, IL, MI, MN, WI) settled. For 10 years Deere must give owners and independent shops the same diagnostic software, manuals and **parts-pairing tools** dealers have, and dealers may not retaliate. Court approval is pending.
- Deere economics (secondary or anecdotal): parts and service are about 20-28% of revenue but a larger share of profit. One dealer interview: service about 70% gross margin, parts about 35%, equipment sales about 5-6%.
- Sources: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-states-sue-deere-company-protect-farmers-unfair-corporate-tactics-high-repair-costs ; https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/equipment/article/2025/06/11/federal-court-rejects-deeres-motion ; https://news.wttw.com/2026/04/07/illinois-based-deere-co-agrees-pay-99-million-settle-right-repair-lawsuit ; https://lasvegassun.com/news/2026/jul/08/john-deere-owners-will-get-the-right-to-repair-the/ ; https://inpractise.com/articles/deere-dealership-economics-adobe-vs-figma-copart-verra-mobility-cellavision
- Relevance: the dealer model is built so that equipment is a low-margin loss leader and service and parts are the profit engine. That supports C7's "built to be inefficient" in the sense of incentives, though it is a rational profit design, not a lack of "smart".

### 11. Apple parts pairing: the software version of the parts lock. [regulation + firm statements]
- Apple serialises and pairs parts (displays, batteries, cameras, Face ID/Touch ID). Apple's hardware chief John Ternus (Apr 2024) said pairing is needed to authenticate biometrics and for calibration, and that third-party parts are not "blocked" (non-biometric ones work, with warnings). iFixit cut the iPhone 14's repairability score from 7/10 to 4/10 over pairing.
- Oregon SB 1596 (signed March 2024) is the first US ban on parts pairing that blocks or degrades function, for devices made after Jan 1, 2025. Apple lobbied against it, citing security, safety and privacy.
- From 2024, Apple accepts genuine **used** parts, including biometric ones, but still uses pairing to verify genuineness. A "Parts and Service History" screen shows non-genuine parts.
- EU Right to Repair Directive 2024/1799: manufacturers may not use contractual terms or hardware/software techniques that unjustifiably impede repair, including hindering used or compatible third-party parts. Member-state transposition was due by July 31, 2026. It covers consumer goods within Ecodesign categories, not industrial goods.
- Sources: https://www.macrumors.com/2024/04/11/apple-responds-to-parts-pairing-criticism/ ; https://appleinsider.com/articles/24/03/27/despite-apple-pushback-oregon-has-passed-its-right-to-repair-bill-banning-parts-pairing ; https://Engadget.Com/iphones-will-soon-be-repairable-with-genuine-used-components-but-parts-pairing-persists-142958993.html ; https://twobirds.com/en/insights/2024/germany/recht-auf-reparatur-richtlinie-veroeffentlicht-auswirkungen-fuer-wirtschaftsakteure-in-der-eu
- Relevance to C5: even a perfect AI-made replacement part may not **work** if the device demands a cryptographic handshake. The lock is the signature, not the geometry.

### 12. FTC "Nixing the Fix" (2021): little evidence for OEM safety and security justifications. [regulator]
- The May 2021 report to Congress found "scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions". It found no empirical evidence that independent shops misuse data more, or that giving them diagnostics and firmware creates cybersecurity risk. It catalogued restrictions: adhesives, limited parts, unavailable diagnostic software and software locks. A July 2021 policy statement prioritised enforcement, which led to Deere.
- Source: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P194400-Nixing-the-Fix-California-Senate-Judiciary-Committee-Testimony.pdf ; https://www.dotmed.com/legal/print/story.html?nid=54775

### 13. FDA (2018) on medical device servicing: third-party servicers are "critical"; there is no evidence of a widespread safety problem. [regulator]
- The FDARA sec. 710 report (May 2018) found many OEMs and third parties give high-quality, safe servicing. Most adverse events attributed to servicing actually related to **remanufacturing**. Objective evidence was insufficient to justify new rules. Third-party service is "critical to the functioning of the U.S. healthcare system." AdvaMed disputed this and wanted registration and adverse-event reporting for third parties.
- Source: https://www.fda.gov/media/113431/download ; https://www.thefdalawblog.com/2018/05/fda-issues-report-on-medical-device-servicing-declining-to-impose-new-regulatory-requirements-for-now/
- Relevance to C6: the inspection and certification layer is both a genuine safety function and a battleground that OEMs use to justify exclusion. The evidence gap cuts both ways.

### 14. Aerospace: sole-source spare-parts monopolies with extreme margins, and PMA as the "alternative parts factory". [regulator data + investor view]
- DoD IG (2019): on 46 of 47 sampled TransDigm parts, about $16.1M in excess profit, with margins of 17% to 4,451%. DoD IG (DODIG-2022-043, Dec 2021): about $20.8M in excess profit on 105 parts, with the highest markup about 3,850%. More than 75% of TransDigm net sales come from sole-source products. The business model is to buy sole-source part makers and raise prices. TransDigm disputes the cost methodology.
- Sources: https://media.defense.gov/2021/Dec/13/2002908344/-1/-1/1/DODIG-2022-043.PDF ; https://oversightdemocrats.house.gov/committee-activity/hearings/dod-inspector-general-report-on-excess-profits-by-transdigm-group-inc ; https://www.pogo.org/analysis/spare-parts-contractor-profits-from-broken-system
- FAA PMA (Parts Manufacturer Approval) is the regulated route for non-OEM replacement parts. HEICO, the largest PMA maker, sells at an estimated 25-50% (some say 40-70%) below OEM price. These are investor estimates. PMA remains a small share of total parts demand (HEICO says it holds about 2% of the replacement-parts market, about half of PMA). OEMs push back by making warranties conditional on OEM parts.
- Sources: https://www.flightglobal.com/parts-paradise/56980.article ; https://eaglepointcapital.substack.com/p/heico-a-compounding-beast ; https://aviationweek.com/aerospace/manufacturing-supply-chain/heico-sees-no-dip-commercial-demand
- Relevance to C5: making an alternative airliner part is technically solved and has been since the 1950s. What takes years and costs millions per part family is **FAA certification** (test data, design approval, production approval), plus airline and lessor acceptance. That bottleneck is regulatory proof, not fabrication.

### 15. Lexmark (2017): patent exhaustion helps remanufacturers. [legal holding]
- Impression Products v. Lexmark, 581 U.S. (2017), 8-0 on domestic sales: an authorized sale exhausts patent rights regardless of post-sale restrictions. This killed Lexmark's attempt to use patents to stop refilling of "return program" cartridges.
- Source: https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2017/05/supreme-court-expands-patent-exhaustion
- Relevance: the patent route to aftermarket lock-in narrowed, which pushed OEMs toward **software and chip** locks (printer firmware "dynamic security", EndoWrist counters, Deere payload files, Apple pairing) and toward copyright and DMCA sec. 1201.

## Synthesis and reasoning

1. **What is monopolized.** In every major case, the excluded rivals already had the skill: ISOs for Kodak, CSU for Xerox, SIS and Restore for EndoWrists, independent ag shops for Deere, HEICO for aircraft parts. What they lacked was (a) physical parts, (b) proprietary diagnostic or authorization software, (c) legal rights to manuals and IP, or (d) a regulator or OEM approval stamp. The "knowledge monopoly" is real only in the sense that OEMs **legally and technically enclose** documentation and authorization, through copyright on manuals, DMCA 1201 on software and cryptographic pairing. AI cannot dissolve those. It can only reduce the cost of reverse-engineering, which is often legally risky.
2. **Economics.** There is consensus that aftermarket prices sit above cost even with competitive foremarkets (Borenstein et al.). There is disagreement on welfare: Shapiro and Cabral say harm is narrower or ambiguous when anticipated, and the main costs are entrenchment, entry barriers and exploitation of an installed base after policy changes. So the inefficiency is real, but much of it is a **transfer** (high service margin, low equipment price), not pure waste.
3. **Remedy history.** Antitrust litigation is slow (Kodak took about 10 years to verdict) and mostly lost (Gleklen 2012). Wins have come lately and via regulation and settlement (Deere 2026, Oregon, EU directive, Ninth Circuit SIS 2026). This is a moving legal environment in 2026 and favours startups selling to independent repairers and owners in ag, medical robotics and electronics.
4. **For C5 (AI parts factory).** Making the part is the easy half. The hard half is (i) authorisation to work in a paired or chipped system, (ii) regulatory approval where safety matters (FAA PMA/STC, FDA 510(k) for remanufacturing), and (iii) IP clearance (Xerox lets patent holders refuse; copying a patented part is infringement unless the patent is expired or the use is repair, which exhaustion and the repair doctrine permit only partially).
5. **For C6.** The FDA and FTC both found OEM safety claims weakly evidenced, yet certification gates (PMA, FDA clearance, Intuitive's "illusory" approval process) are where lock-in now hides. An AI-native inspection and certification layer that produces trusted evidence could be the actual unlock.

## Company shapes suggested by this evidence
- An evidence and certification engine for non-OEM parts: AI-assisted generation of compliance packages (FAA PMA test plans and data, FDA 510(k) for remanufactured instruments, IEC/UL). Certification is the slow, costly gate.
- A repair-access compliance platform that profits from new mandates (Deere settlement tools parity, Oregon and EU pairing bans, state ag and medical R2R laws): independent diagnostic tools, authorized pairing as a service, and documentation.
- An independent service network for robotics and medical robotics (EndoWrist-type instruments, surgical and industrial robots) as the SIS ruling reopens the market. Lock-in exists, and courts are now pricing it.
- PMA-style alternate parts for machines that are not yet regulated as tightly as aircraft (ag, mining, energy equipment, industrial robots), where sole-source markups resemble TransDigm's.
