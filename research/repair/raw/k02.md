## Findings (fetched and verified)

1. **AI raises novices toward expert level but barely moves experts. The gap narrows; it does not disappear.** Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work", NBER w31161 (https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161). 5,179 customer-support agents: +14% issues resolved per hour on average, +34% for novice/low-skill agents, minimal effect for experienced agents. The mechanism is that the AI passes along the practices of top workers. Data. Reading: AI spreads codified best practice. It supports C2/C3 only for *informational* knowledge, and only in a text-only domain with no physical step. It also needs a corpus of expert transcripts to learn from. Repair has no equivalent corpus for most equipment.

2. **Physical and repair tasks are the least-exposed work.** Eloundou et al., "GPTs are GPTs" (https://arxiv.org/html/2303.10130). "Manual work is not exposed to LLMs or even LLMs with additional systems integration for the time being." Science and critical-thinking skill importance is "strongly negatively associated with exposure" (coefficients -0.11 to -0.35). The inspector example task is rated E0 because the model "does not have access to any kind of physicality". Study.

3. **Actual AI use in repair work is the lowest of any skill measured.** Anthropic Economic Index paper (https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04761). "Installation, Equipment Maintenance, and Repairing showed the lowest prevalence" among skills. Occupations involving physical labor were the least present. Computer/math occupations make up 37.2% of conversations, against 0.23% for Job Zone 1 occupations. Data. This contradicts C3 as an observed fact today: the knowledge channel is not being used for repair at scale.

4. **Moravec's paradox.** Moravec (1988): it is easy to give computers adult-level performance on tests or checkers, and "difficult or impossible to give them the skills of a one-year-old when it comes to perception and mobility" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravec%27s_paradox). History/theory. Wikipedia also records Narayanan's counter-view that the paradox reflects what researchers choose to work on rather than an intrinsic limit.

5. **Robot dexterity has barely improved in deployment.** Rodney Brooks, Predictions Scorecard 2025 (https://rodneybrooks.com/predictions-scorecard-2025-january-01/). "we have not actually seen any improvement in widely deployed robotic hands" in about 40 years. For real work robots need "four, five, or six nines" of reliability, and "We are a long way from that." He expects dexterous hands no earlier than 2030, hopefully by 2040. Practitioner view (founder of iRobot and Rethink Robotics).

6. **Touch, the missing data modality.** Brooks, "Why Today's Humanoids Won't Learn Dexterity" (https://rodneybrooks.com/why-todays-humanoids-wont-learn-dexterity/). The human hand has about 17,000 low-threshold mechanoreceptors, about 1,000 per fingertip. In Johansson's experiment, people with anesthetized fingertips took about 4x as long to light a match. Video and teleoperation data leave out force and touch, and touch has no capture/replay pipeline comparable to audio or images. He puts profitable humanoid deployment more than 10 years out. Practitioner view, partly grounded in neuroscience studies. Relevance: much repair skill (thread feel, torque, seating a connector, feeling bearing play, listening to an engine) is tactile and tacit.

7. **The best robot foundation models work on household tasks under lab conditions.** Physical Intelligence pi0 blog (https://www.pi.website/blog/pi0). Normalized scores *with partial credit*: shirt folding 1.0, bussing 0.875 to 0.971, grocery bagging 0.786, toast 0.75. The company itself says generalist policies are "still in their infancy" and that dexterous tasks may need fine-tuning on high-quality task data. Data from an interested party. A partial-credit score of 0.79 is far from the "five nines" a safety-critical repair needs.

8. **Force-sensing research is still at the basic level.** FACTR, arXiv 2502.17432 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17432). Contact-rich learning needs custom force-feedback teleoperation rigs to collect data. The paper reports a 43% generalization gain on unseen objects for tasks like lifting boxes and rolling dough. Study. Shows that the data needed for physical manipulation is scarce and expensive to collect.

9. **Certification is gated on time spent doing physical work, not on knowledge.** 14 CFR 65.77 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/65.77). An A&P mechanic certificate needs either an FAA Part 147 school document or "at least 18 months of practical experience" (30 months for both ratings). Regulator. Even if AI supplies the knowledge, the right to sign off on the work rests with a licensed human who has logged hands-on experience. AI cannot hold a certificate.

10. **The labor bottleneck is people, not knowledge.** Boeing Pilot & Technician Outlook (https://www.boeing.com/commercial/market/pilot-technician-outlook) projects 728,000 new maintenance technicians needed globally over 20 years. BLS (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/aircraft-and-avionics-equipment-mechanics-and-technicians.htm) counts about 159,800 US aircraft/avionics mechanic jobs in 2025, with about 13,100 openings a year through 2035, mostly replacement. Oliver Wyman (https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2024/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2024-2034.html) puts MRO at $104B in 2024, rising to $124B by 2034, and cites "raw material and labor shortages" and OEMs "pushing their production capacity to the limit". Data, OEM and consultant views. The binding constraints are hands and parts supply, not diagnosis.

11. **Execution and verification failures happen even with full knowledge.** NTSB, Alaska 1282 door plug (https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/DCA24MA063.aspx). Four bolts were removed during rework and never reinstalled, and the parts-removal process "was not consistently followed". Probable cause: Boeing's inadequate training, guidance and oversight. FAA oversight was a contributing factor, and the NTSB issued 19 recommendations. Regulator. Boeing is the OEM and holds all the knowledge, yet the failure was in procedure, documentation and physical execution. Knowledge was not the failure mode, which supports C6: inspection and process discipline are the hard part.

12. **Access is locked by software and law, not knowledge: Deere.** FTC v. Deere, January 2025 (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-states-sue-deere-company-protect-farmers-unfair-corporate-tactics-high-repair-costs). Service ADVISOR is "the only fully functional software repair tool capable of performing all repairs", and only dealers get it. The customer version cannot perform all repairs. Regulator. Even with perfect AI diagnosis, a farmer cannot complete the software-gated step (calibration, authorization, ECU programming). The barrier is a *permission* monopoly, not a knowledge monopoly.

13. **Parts pairing.** iFixit (https://www.ifixit.com/News/69320/apple-parts-pairing-iphone). Serialized parts lose features (Face ID, Touch ID) or show warnings unless the OEM re-pairs them. The workarounds are microsoldering or EEPROM reprogramming. Campaigner view. Same point as finding 12: AI-supplied knowledge cannot defeat cryptographic authorization.

14. **Counter-evidence: OEM safety and liability arguments are weak.** FTC, "Nixing the Fix" (2021) (https://www.ftc.gov/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions). "there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions". "The record contains no empirical evidence to suggest that independent repair shops are more or less likely than authorized repair shops to compromise or misuse customer data." Regulator. This *weakens* the liability leg of the steelman. Much "safety" gating is pretextual, which supports C2/C7, though the gap it describes is about access and permission rather than knowledge.

## Background items [BG], not re-fetched this session

- Polanyi, The Tacit Dimension (1966): "we can know more than we can tell". This is the classic statement of why some skill resists codification and is the root of the tacit-knowledge objection.
- Dell'Acqua et al. (HBS/BCG, 2023), "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier": 758 consultants. Inside the frontier, about 12% more tasks completed, about 25% faster, about 40% higher quality. On a task outside the frontier, AI users were about 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. Relevance: confident wrong answers on edge cases, and repair diagnosis is mostly edge cases.
- Moffatt v. Air Canada (BC Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024): the airline was held liable for wrong information from its chatbot. Liability for AI advice falls on the deployer, which makes OEMs and MROs cautious about AI-issued repair instructions.
- Mata v. Avianca (SDNY 2023): lawyers were sanctioned for citing cases ChatGPT hallucinated. This is the canonical verification-failure precedent.
- GE LEAP fuel nozzle: the first FAA-cleared 3D-printed part in a commercial engine (the T25 sensor housing in 2015, then the LEAP nozzle). It took years of material and process qualification. Additive manufacturing qualification (NASA-STD-6030, FAA PMA/STC routes) needs qualified materials, process control, and part-specific test and inspection. A bespoke AI-designed part for a regulated asset still needs approved design data, which is the main counter to C5.
- EASA AI Roadmap 2.0 and Concept Paper Issue 2 (https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/research-innovation/ai): guidance so far covers only Level 1 and 2 ML (assistance and human-AI teaming). Fully autonomous Level 3 AI is still on the regulatory horizon. Verified on the page: "Guidance for Level 1 & 2 machine-learning applications."

## Reasoning / synthesis

- The steelman splits into four distinct gates, and AI moves each one differently:
  1. **Codified knowledge** (manuals, fault trees, bulletins). AI helps a lot, *if it can get the data*. Most service manuals, fault-isolation manuals and OEM telemetry are proprietary, and LLM training corpora contain little of them (finding 3, plus the Deere and pairing cases). The data-access problem is legal and commercial, not technical.
  2. **Tacit and embodied skill** (feel, sound, smell, dexterity). AI as text barely helps. Robotics is 10 or more years from reliable dexterous field repair by credible estimates (findings 5 to 8), and reliability targets of five nines are far beyond today's partial-credit lab scores.
  3. **Authority and permission** (software unlock, licensed signoff, approved data, warranty). Untouched by AI. These are legal constructs (findings 9, 12, 13).
  4. **Verification and liability.** AI makes errors more fluent. In safety-critical repair someone must still bear liability, which pushes demand toward inspection and certification. That supports C6, and finding 11 shows execution errors happen even with full knowledge.
- Counterweight: the FTC finds OEM safety justifications mostly pretextual. So the gap is partly manufactured (C7-ish), but as a *permission* monopoly rather than a *knowledge* monopoly. AI does not dissolve permissions, while regulation (right to repair) can.
- On C4 (10x cost reduction across diagnose, understand, source, deliver): the evidence suggests diagnosis and understanding could fall a lot for codified faults. Sourcing and delivery are bounded by physical supply chains and OEM capacity (Oliver Wyman). The labor and execution step (728k technicians needed) and certification are outside those four steps and may dominate total cost and downtime, so a 10x cut in the four steps yields much less than 10x end to end (Amdahl's law).
- The 14% / 34% result suggests the realistic AI effect is compressing the time it takes a novice to reach competence. That is valuable given the technician shortage, but it is a skill multiplier, not a gap eliminator.
