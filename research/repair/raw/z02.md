## Findings

### 1. The FTC record says warranty and repair restrictions steer consumers into OEM networks, and OEMs offered "scant evidence" for their justifications (V, regulator)
- Source: FTC, *Nixing the Fix: An FTC Report to Congress on Repair Restrictions* (May 2021). https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf
- Quote (p.6): "it is clear that repair restrictions have diluted the effectiveness of Section 102(c) and steered consumers into manufacturers' repair networks or to replace products before the end of their useful lives ... there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions."
- Conclusion (p.54): "Although manufacturers have offered numerous explanations for their repair restrictions, the majority are not supported by the record."
- Dissent caveat (fn 18): Commissioners Wilson and Phillips noted that the report leaves intellectual property out of scope, and that IP "may provide legitimate justification for some repair restrictions."

### 2. Illegal warranty tying is widespread: 45 of 50 companies (V, study cited by regulator)
- U.S. PIRG Education Fund (Oct 2018) reviewed the warranties of 50 companies and found that 45 "appeared to violate Section 102(c)" of the MMWA. The FTC cites it at p.8 of *Nixing the Fix*.
- SEMA told the FTC it "regularly receives complaints that automobile dealerships void automobile warranties if the dealership finds a specialty part (e.g., custom wheels) had been installed ... regardless of whether the specialty part caused the automobile to malfunction."
- The Automotive Oil Change Association submitted evidence that "many consumers are still unaware of their rights under the MMWA" (fn 28).
- Reading: the warranty lever works mainly through **consumer belief and dealer practice**, not through the law. The law already bans most tying.

### 3. The lever is weak in law but works through deterrence: repeated FTC enforcement waves (V for 2018 and 2024; M for 2022 details)
- **April 2018:** FTC staff sent warning letters to six companies selling automobiles, cellular devices and video game systems: Hyundai, Asus, HTC, Microsoft, Sony and Nintendo. Some letters told the companies to stop using "warranty void if seal removed" stickers. Sony and Nintendo then updated their warranty statements.
  - https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2018/04/ftc-staff-warns-companies-it-illegal-condition-warranty-coverage-use-specified-parts-or-services
- **June 2022 (M):** FTC consent orders against Harley-Davidson and MWE Investments (Westinghouse generators) for conditioning warranties on authorised parts and dealers. Weber (grills) followed in 2022-23.
- **July 2024:** FTC warning letters to eight companies, four of them air purifier sellers, over warranty practices that hinder repair (V via Wikipedia, MMWA page).
- Reading: 50 years after the 1975 Act, the regulator still has to keep re-teaching the same rule. Warranty language is a cheap deterrent that firms keep using because it changes behaviour.

### 4. The MMWA has a structural hole: it covers only written warranties, only consumer products, and only the warranty period (V, regulator)
- *Nixing the Fix* p.53 says a revised MMWA Interpretation would only help "products that are sold with a written warranty and only during the duration of the warranty period. Thus, manufacturers could entirely avoid the anti-tying provision by refraining to offer written warranties. Second, consumers would be left without repair rights during the time period when they are most likely to use them—after the warranty has expired. Finally, because the MMWA only applies to consumer products ... [it] would not address repair restrictions imposed on owners of products that fall outside the scope of the MMWA."
- **Implication for B2B (energy, machines, aircraft):** the MMWA does not reach this world at all. Control there runs through contracts: service agreements, lessor return conditions, OEM certification of shops, and type-certificate data. That control is harder to attack with consumer law.

### 5. Warranty law explicitly lets OEMs deny claims for damage caused by third-party repair (V, regulator guidance)
- FTC, *Businessperson's Guide to Federal Warranty Law*: tie-ins are "generally" not allowed. But a warrantor may exclude "damage caused ... by you or any non-authorized third party." It may also require specified parts or service when they are "provided free of charge under the warranty," or when it obtains an FTC waiver.
  - https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/businesspersons-guide-federal-warranty-law
- Reading: the legal line sits at **causation**. When causation is disputed, it becomes a fight over diagnosis. The AOCA testimony in the FTC report (p.33) describes automaker Technical Service Bulletins that tell dealers "to treat certain aftermarket parts as the de facto cause of problem engine symptoms ... The dealers forgo technical analysis." Diagnostic authority is the real choke point, and the warranty is the enforcement tool.

### 6. Product liability and reputation as justification: OEMs assert it but produced no data (V, regulator, record of OEM views)
- OEM and trade-group claims (*Nixing the Fix* pp.32-33):
  - CTA: OEMs suffer "reputational risk and the expense of defending lawsuits that result from improperly repaired devices."
  - CompTIA: "If an OEM's brand and warranty are to stand behind repair work and assume product liability, it is only reasonable that the repair facility demonstrates competency."
  - AHAM: authorised networks let "liability claims and determinations ... be more easily assessed" and leave a "record of the repairs, which can assist in insurance claims."
- FTC finding: "Manufacturers provided no empirical evidence to support their concerns about reputational harm or potential liability resulting from faulty third party repairs." This was despite specific staff requests.
- Kyle Wiens (iFixit) argued the opposite: manufacturers create liability by *withholding* repair information. His example was forklift manuals released to limit liability.

### 7. Evidence that independent repair is not worse (V, practitioner and consumer data cited by regulator)
- Consumer Reports (2014): "consumers who used independent repair shops were more satisfied with the repairs than those who used factory service."
- Auto Care Association: about 70-75% of car owners use independent shops once the warranty expires.
  - Both are in *Nixing the Fix* p.38.
- Reading: once the warranty lever ends, the market moves sharply to independents. That suggests the warranty period is when authorised-channel capture is strongest.
- Counter-evidence submitted by industry (an OPEI survey on equipment modification): 54% of modifications removed safety features and 62% could reduce reliability. The FTC called this "inapposite" because it concerned modification, not repair.

### 8. The same pattern in heavy equipment, where software, not warranty, does the gatekeeping (V, regulator and court filing)
- FTC, with the Illinois and Minnesota attorneys general, v. Deere & Co. (N.D. Ill., filed 15 Jan 2025, case 3:25-cv-50017; Commission vote 3-2).
- Service ADVISOR, available only to dealers, is "the only fully functional software repair tool capable of performing all repairs on Deere equipment." Deere dealers hold "a 100% market share" of repairs that need that tool.
- Deere's Production & Precision Ag segment had **$26.8B in net sales and $7.0B in operating profit in FY2023**. The complaint ties the restrictions to parts revenue. Its figures for generic-part discounts and dealer parts margins are redacted.
  - Press release: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-states-sue-deere-company-protect-farmers-unfair-corporate-tactics-high-repair-costs
  - Complaint: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/DeereCoREDACTEDComplaintCaseNo325-cv-50017.pdf
- The complaint also describes how ECU "payload files," "parts pairing" and ECU "flashing" require the dealer tool. Repair is gated by **authorisation to complete the job** (software unlock), not by knowledge alone.
- Context (V via Wikipedia): Deere signed a January 2023 MOU with the American Farm Bureau Federation, keeping protection for trade secrets and emissions settings. Colorado passed the first agricultural right-to-repair law in 2023.

### 9. Liability cuts both ways: shops that deviate from OEM procedures face large verdicts (M, court)
- *Seebachan v. John Eagle Collision Center* (Dallas County, TX, 2019): a jury awarded about **$42M** against a body shop. It had glued rather than welded a replacement roof on a Honda Fit, contrary to Honda's repair procedure, and the occupants were severely injured in a later crash. Not re-verified this session.
- Effect reported in the collision trade press (M): a rush toward OEM repair procedures, OEM-certified shop networks, and mandatory pre- and post-repair diagnostic scans.
- Reading: **tort liability on repairers**, more than on OEMs, is a strong force pushing repair toward OEM-documented procedures and certified shops. This is real evidence that liability shapes channels. Notably, what it enforces is *procedure fidelity*, which is information that can be published, not channel exclusivity.

### 10. Insurance does not always push toward OEMs (M, practitioner)
- In US auto collision repair, insurers use Direct Repair Programs (DRP) to steer claimants toward shops that agree to insurer labour rates. They often specify aftermarket, recycled or non-OEM parts to cut cost. OEMs and some shops oppose this through OEM "position statements" on OEM parts.
- Reading: insurance is a **channel-control** force, but its direction depends on who pays. A payer that bears the cost pushes toward cheaper, non-OEM channels. A risk-bearer worried about liability pushes toward certified procedures. This contradicts any single "liability = OEM capture" story.
- Analogue (M, history): Hartford Steam Boiler (founded 1866) bundled insurance with mandatory inspection. It is the archetype of an inspection-as-insurance business, relevant to C6.

### 11. Aviation: the control comes from certification and contracts, not knowledge alone (M, practitioner and investor view)
- The FAA PMA route (14 CFR 21) lets non-OEMs make approved replacement parts (V, via Wikipedia). There was an "explosion of PMA parts" after the FAA's 1990s enforcement programme.
- Commonly reported adoption barriers (M), all channel-control levers of the warranty and liability type:
  - Lessor return conditions and lease contracts that restrict PMA parts and DER repairs in engines.
  - OEM long-term service agreements, such as engine "power-by-the-hour," that require OEM parts.
  - Residual-value worries.
- Engine OEMs (GE/CFM, Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce) earn the bulk of their profit in the aftermarket (M; widely reported in earnings commentary).
- Reading: in aviation, the bottleneck is **certified authority to sign the return to service and the acceptability of the part to financiers**. It is not diagnostic knowledge.

### 12. Medical devices: the regulator found no evidence either way (M, regulator)
- FDA's May 2018 *Report on the Quality, Safety, and Effectiveness of Servicing of Medical Devices* concluded that the objective evidence was "not sufficient to conclude whether or not there is a widespread public health concern" about servicing. It noted that both OEMs and third parties provide "high quality, safe, and effective servicing." Hospitals rely heavily on in-house and independent servicers.
- The FDA URL could not be fetched (401). Not re-verified this session.
- During COVID in 2020, hospitals struggled to get ventilator maintenance (V via Wikipedia). iFixit then published a large repository of medical repair manuals.

### 13. Policy is flipping warranty from a lever *against* repair into a lever *for* repair (V via Wikipedia)
- EU Right to Repair Directive 2024/1799, adopted 30 May 2024; member states must transpose it by **31 July 2026**.
  - When a consumer chooses repair over replacement, the legal guarantee is **extended by one year**.
  - Manufacturers may not use contract terms or hardware/software techniques that hinder repair unless "objectively justified."
  - An EU online repair platform is due to be fully operational by 1 January 2028.
- Massachusetts: the 2012 automotive data law led to a national 2014 MOU from model year 2018. 2020 Question 1 extended it to telematics. NHTSA in June 2023 told OEMs it was federally pre-empted, which shows **safety and cyber regulators** acting as a counter-lever.

## Synthesis

Where the control actually sits:
1. **Consumer goods.** Warranty tying is mostly illegal already. It persists because consumers believe it (45/50 warranties appeared non-compliant, and enforcement waves came in 2018, 2022 and 2024). The effect is strongest during the warranty period. Afterwards about 70% of auto owners go independent.
2. **B2B and heavy machines.** The MMWA does not apply. Control is exercised through software authorisation (Deere Service ADVISOR, parts pairing), service contracts, dealer agreements and lessor conditions.
3. **Liability.** OEMs invoke it but provide no data (FTC). The strongest *real* liability force falls on the **repairer** (the John Eagle verdict). That pushes shops toward OEM procedures and certification, which is information plus attestation.
4. **Insurance** pushes both ways, depending on who pays.
5. **Certification and return-to-service authority** (aviation, medical, emissions) is the hard core that knowledge alone does not dissolve.

### Implications for the founder's claims
- **C2 is partly right but mis-specified.** The monopoly is less about *knowledge* than about *authority*: tools that unlock software, warranty or contract terms, signatures on return to service, and acceptance by financiers and insurers. Knowledge gaps exist, but OEMs enforce exclusivity through legal and technical locks, so knowing how does not equal being allowed to.
- **C3 is contradicted in its strong form.** AI can close the information gap (manuals, diagnostic reasoning). It cannot close the gaps in authorisation, liability allocation, warranty causation disputes or certification. The Deere case is the clean example: farmers know the fix but need the dealer's software to complete it.
- **C6 is supported.** Liability and insurance both need a trusted record of the repair (AHAM's "record of the repairs ... insurance claims"; the John Eagle verdict). Whoever provides independent attestation, procedure compliance and an auditable repair record holds the lever that currently justifies OEM channels.
- **C7 is mixed.** The inefficiency is less "not built for smart" and more *built for rent capture* through legal and contract design, which regulators are actively dismantling. That points to a regulatory tailwind more than a technology vacuum.
