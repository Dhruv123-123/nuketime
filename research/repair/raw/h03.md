## Findings

### A. Telephone equipment (Hush-A-Phone -> Carterfone -> Part 68 -> Computer II -> divestiture)

1. **Hush-A-Phone (D.C. Cir. 1956): the "network harm" argument for monopoly was shown to be a pretext in a mechanical case.** AT&T's tariff barred any device "not furnished by the telephone company"; the FCC sided with AT&T. The court reversed, holding that users may use their phones in ways "privately beneficial without being publicly detrimental." The device had been sold for about 20 years with no reported problems before AT&T acted. (kind: court) Source: Wikipedia, Hush-A-Phone Corp. v. United States, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hush-A-Phone_v._United_States

2. **Carterfone (FCC, 1968, 13 F.C.C.2d 420): any lawful device could connect electrically if it did not harm the network.** This opened the customer-premises equipment (CPE) market. In practice, for years devices had to go through a protective coupling arrangement *rented from AT&T*, so the incumbent kept a toll gate. The "any lawful device" principle is credited with the spread of answering machines, fax machines and modems. (kind: regulator) Source: Wikipedia, Carterfone, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carterfone

3. **The real unlock was a standard plus a certification regime, not knowledge.** Part 68 of the FCC rules (registration program, phased in roughly 1975-1977 [UNVERIFIED-memory on dates]) set technical standards so customer equipment could connect "without causing harm" and *without requiring protective circuit arrangements* in the carrier's network. Later (around 2000-2001 [UNVERIFIED-memory]) the FCC privatized the technical criteria to the industry body ACTA and let third-party certification bodies approve terminal equipment. Europe's equivalent is TBR21. (kind: regulator) Source: Wikipedia, Part 68, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Part_68
   - Reasoning: the monopoly was broken when inspection/certification moved from the incumbent ("we must own and approve everything for safety") to a neutral published standard plus independent testing. This is directly relevant to C6: certification was the bottleneck AND the lever.

4. **Computer II (FCC, decided 1980; CPE detariffed for new equipment from 1983 [UNVERIFIED-memory on dates]) barred carriers from bundling telecom service with CPE**, which "created a new competitive market for telephones and modems." Carriers also had to sell inputs to rival enhanced-service providers on the same terms they gave themselves. (kind: regulator) Source: Wikipedia, Second Computer Inquiry, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Computer_Inquiry

5. **Under the Bell lease model, repair was bundled and equipment was built to last decades.** Phones stayed Bell property; a monthly fee bundled into service covered repair; returned sets were refurbished; this pushed Western Electric toward reliability to minimize service calls, and helped keep basic local service under about $10/month. A customer wanting a non-Bell phone had to transfer it to the Bell company, which leased it back for a monthly charge plus rewiring fee. (kind: history) Source: Wikipedia, Western Electric, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Electric
   - Counterpoint to C7: the monopoly was not "built to be inefficient" in an engineering sense; it was built for durability and low service calls. The inefficiency was in pricing/lock-in and slow innovation, not in repair execution.

6. **After liberalization, consumers became responsible for inside wiring and equipment, and basic service prices rose; many customers kept paying lease fees long after they exceeded purchase price** (QLT Consumer Lease Services kept leasing old Western Electric phones). Liberalization shifted repair burden onto the owner; inertia kept rents flowing to the incumbent's lease book for decades. (kind: history) Source: Wikipedia, Western Electric, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Electric
   - [UNVERIFIED-memory] Press reports (e.g. 2000s) found some elderly customers had paid hundreds of dollars over years for leased phones worth tens of dollars.

7. **Fate of the incumbent: AT&T was broken up, its manufacturing arm lost the captive buyer and became unprofitable.** Settlement finalized Jan 8, 1982; divestiture effective Jan 1, 1984; AT&T's book value fell about 70%. AT&T found Western Electric unprofitable without guaranteed Bell customers and spun it off in 1995 (Lucent -> Alcatel-Lucent -> Nokia). AT&T's push into computers failed; SBC bought AT&T Corp in 2005. (kind: history) Source: Wikipedia, Breakup of the Bell System, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakup_of_the_Bell_System
   - Note: the equipment opening (Carterfone 1968) took ~15 years and an antitrust suit to fully play out. The incumbent did not die from the equipment market opening alone.

8. **Prices: handset prices collapsed to commodity level after CPE was opened [UNVERIFIED-memory: cheap imported phones $10-$30 in the mid 1980s vs. years of rental fees].** Modem market: the Hayes Smartmodem (1981) and successors became possible only because direct connection was legal; modem prices fell steeply through the 1980s-90s [UNVERIFIED-memory]. I could not retrieve hard price series in this session.

### B. IBM plug-compatible peripherals and mainframes

9. **The 1956 IBM consent decree forced IBM to sell (not only lease) machines and to sell parts and service information to independent maintainers [UNVERIFIED-memory].** This is the closest historical analog to a right-to-repair mandate: it created the third-party maintenance (TPM) industry for IBM gear. DOJ moved to terminate it in the mid-1990s, with phase-out completed around 2001 [UNVERIFIED-memory]. (kind: court) Attempted source: DOJ, https://www.justice.gov/atr/case-document/file/485671/download (401 in this session)

10. **Plug-compatible peripherals arrived fast once the interface was knowable.** Telex offered the first IBM plug-compatible tape drives and controllers in 1965; Memorex entered plug-compatible disks in 1968; CDC, Itel and Storage Technology followed. US GSA procurement of plug-compatible gear helped the market grow. Eventually third-party alternatives existed for most IBM peripherals and main memory. (kind: history) Source: Wikipedia, Plug compatible, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plug_compatible
    - Supports C2 partially: once the interface spec (the "knowledge") was reverse-engineered, rivals entered. But the barrier was an interface/standard, and the buyer (GSA) mattered.

11. **IBM's response: price cuts, new leasing terms, and interface changes; Telex v. IBM.** [UNVERIFIED-memory] In 1971 IBM introduced the Fixed Term Plan (longer leases at lower prices) and cut peripheral prices by roughly 15-35% on targeted products, and changed interfaces (integrating controllers into CPUs) in ways rivals called predatory. A district court in 1973 awarded Telex about $352M (trebled ~$259.5M, figures vary in sources), but the 10th Circuit reversed in 1975, finding IBM's price cuts and product changes lawful competition; the case was settled. Lesson: incumbent price cuts captured much of the consumer gain and crushed weaker plug-compatible firms (Telex, Memorex, Itel later struggled or failed).

12. **Plug-compatible mainframes: big price/performance gain, but the incumbent survived and the challengers mostly died.** Amdahl 470V/6 (first ship 1975) was about twice as fast as IBM 370/168 at about the same price, one-third the floor space, no water cooling. Amdahl won roughly 8% of worldwide mainframe business (peak claims to 24% vary). IBM initially refused to maintain Amdahl-connected systems, but was forced to after Amdahl became profitable maintaining IBM hardware. In late 1981 eight PCM companies offered 36 IBM-compatible models. But Amdahl lost hundreds of millions per quarter in the early 1990s, was absorbed by Fujitsu (1997), and quit IBM-compatible mainframes in 2000 when IBM's 64-bit zSeries would have required ~$1B+ to match. Hitachi left the same year. IBM sued and then bought PSI (2007-2008), ending emulation. StorageTek's attempt at its own processor nearly bankrupted it. (kind: history) Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amdahl_Corporation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plug_compatible
    - Key insight: the incumbent re-closed the market through architecture changes, software licensing (the "knowledge" moved into software IBM licensed) and continuous R&D cadence. Openness is not permanent.

13. **Repair/maintenance was a weapon: refusal to maintain mixed systems.** IBM refusing to service systems with Amdahl components (finding 12) mirrors today's OEM tactics (warranty voiding, parts pairing). It was overcome by a competitor building its own maintenance business plus antitrust settlement terms giving Amdahl customers reasonable software licenses. (kind: history) Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amdahl_Corporation

## Synthesis

- **How monopolies broke:** never by knowledge diffusion alone. Each break required (a) a legal/regulatory act (court ruling, FCC order, consent decree, antitrust settlement), (b) a published interface or technical standard (Part 68, IBM channel interface), (c) a neutral certification or registration regime replacing the incumbent's "safety" veto, and (d) a large buyer willing to take the risk (GSA for plug-compatibles). Knowledge (reverse-engineering the interface) was necessary but the gating item was permission and certification.
- **Prices:** terminal equipment went from rental-for-life to commodity; modems/fax/answering machines became mass products. In IBM peripherals, the incumbent's own price cuts captured much of the gain.
- **Incumbent:** AT&T was dismantled, but that took an antitrust case, not the equipment opening. IBM survived and in mainframes re-consolidated control; most plug-compatible challengers failed. Incumbents with continued R&D and control of software/interfaces can re-close an opened market.
- **Repair specifically:** under the Bell monopoly, repair was efficient in an operational sense (bundled, durable equipment, refurbishment). Liberalization moved repair cost and responsibility to the owner and made equipment cheaper and more disposable. Cheaper often meant replace-not-repair rather than better repair.

## Stance on hypotheses
- C1: silent (these cases are about who may supply and connect, not the stage chain).
- C2: mixed. Interface knowledge mattered (plug-compatibles needed IBM's interface; IBM tried to change it), but the binding monopoly was legal/contractual (tariffs, lease-only, refusal to maintain), not know-how.
- C3: contradicts by analogy. Rivals had the knowledge by 1965-1975; the monopoly persisted until regulators acted, and IBM re-closed mainframes later.
- C4: mixed. Large price drops happened in equipment, but driven by competition and standards; history says the incumbent captures much via price cuts and the gain shows up as cheaper replacement, not cheaper diagnosis.
- C5: silent/weakly contradicts: building compatible parts needed millions in R&D (Amdahl) and continual tracking of incumbent changes; compatible makers mostly failed.
- C6: supports strongly. Part 68 registration and later third-party certification bodies were THE mechanism that broke the network-harm veto. Certification regime design is the lever.
- C7: mixed. The structure was built to extract rent (lease-only, foreign attachment bans), but not operationally inefficient at repair; it was rebuilt by regulation, and over ~15-30 years.

## Company shapes suggested
- A neutral, AI-assisted conformity/certification body or test lab for third-party parts and repairs in a regulated domain (the "Part 68 + TCB" of e.g. aviation PMA parts, grid equipment, robots, EV batteries).
- An interface/standards-first parts company (plug-compatible) that targets a big institutional buyer (government, fleets) the way GSA anchored plug-compatibles.
- An independent third-party maintenance (TPM) operator for a high-value installed base where OEM refuses mixed-system service, the Amdahl-maintains-IBM move.
