# e06: Security tech and critical infrastructure equipment repair (SCADA/ICS spares, legacy control systems, secure supply chain requirements): constraints on who can repair

Method note: the shared WebSearch budget for this run was used up before this agent's first search, so no searches ran. Findings come from direct WebFetch of known primary URLs (GAO, CISA, NERC, DOE, Wikipedia summaries of public incidents). Items marked [PRIOR, unverified this session] come from the agent's background knowledge and should be checked before anyone cites them.

## Verified findings (fetched 2026-10-20)

1. GAO-12-375 (2012), "DOD Supply Chain: Suspect Counterfeit Electronic Parts Can Be Found on Internet Purchasing Platforms". https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-12-375
   - GAO asked online platforms for quotes on rare or obsolete parts. 396 vendors replied: 334 in China, 25 in the US, 37 elsewhere. Vendors usually replied within a day and parts arrived within about two weeks.
   - All 16 parts bought were bogus. All 12 tested parts were "suspect counterfeit" (re-marked, wrong metal composition, altered date codes). The other 4 had made-up part numbers, and vendors still supplied them.
   - Reasoning: sourcing obsolete parts is fast and cheap. The scarce thing is provenance, meaning trust that the part is what it claims to be. Fast sourcing of obsolete parts is the main counterfeit vector, so making the sourcing step cheaper without authentication makes things worse. This cuts against C4 and supports C6.

2. NERC CIP-013-2, Supply Chain Risk Management (mandatory for the North American bulk electric system). https://www.nerc.com/pa/Stand/Reliability%20Standards/CIP-013-2.pdf (text extracted locally)
   - R1 requires a documented plan to manage risk from "procuring and installing vendor equipment and software" and from "transitions from one vendor(s) to another vendor(s)".
   - The plan must cover: vendor incident notification; notice when vendor "remote or onsite access should no longer be granted"; vendor disclosure of known vulnerabilities; "verification of software integrity and authenticity of all software and patches provided by the vendor"; and coordination of "vendor-initiated remote access".
   - Plans must be reviewed at least every 15 months.
   - Reasoning: regulation itself ties repair to vetted vendors and vetted software provenance. Switching vendors is named as a risk event. The barrier to who can repair is a compliance and trust barrier, not mainly a knowledge barrier. This contradicts C2 and C3 for this sector.

3. CISA alert (Nov 28, 2023), Unitronics PLCs exploited at US water utilities. https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2023/11/28/exploitation-unitronics-plcs-used-water-and-wastewater-systems
   - Attackers got in through internet exposure and the default password "1111". The utility "switched to manual operations".
   - CISA's recommendations are basic hygiene: MFA for all remote access, take PLCs off the internet, back up logic and configurations, know the factory reset and redeploy process, and update firmware.
   - Reasoning: much of the failure in small utilities is basic know-how and staffing (capacity), not secret OEM knowledge. AI could help here (partial support for C3), but the fix also needs hands-on work and access that is itself a security risk. Remote access for maintenance is the attack surface.

4. CISA AA24-038A (Feb 2024), Volt Typhoon. https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa24-038a
   - PRC state actors pre-positioned in US communications, energy, transport, and water networks. Some had footholds for "at least five years". They used end-of-life Cisco and NETGEAR routers as a botnet and tested OT access using "default vendor credentials".
   - CISA tells operators to plan "end of life" for technology past the manufacturer's support, and to isolate and monitor anything that stays in service.
   - Reasoning: in critical infrastructure, an end-of-life device is a security liability, not just a repair problem. Keeping legacy kit running with grey-market or third-party fixes is exactly what regulators push against. This complicates C5 and C7: some of the "inefficiency" is deliberate risk control.

5. DOE, Large Power Transformers and the US Electric Grid (2014 update). https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2014/04/f15/LPTStudyUpdate-040914.pdf (text extracted locally)
   - 2010 lead times: 5-12 months from domestic producers, 6-16 months from foreign ones. Under high demand: 18-24 months, and "beyond 20 months" if key parts are short. HV bushings alone take up to 5 months and are usually custom.
   - Cost: $2M-$7.5M for 75-500 MVA units, plus 25-30% for transport and installation. Units weigh up to 410 tons. 70% of LPTs are 25 years or older. Raw materials are 57-67% of cost. Domestic producers met about 15% of US demand.
   - [PRIOR, unverified] Wood Mackenzie and NREL reported in 2024-25 that LPT lead times had stretched to roughly 2-4 years, with prices up 60-80% since 2020 and about 30% of US power transformer demand unmet in 2025.
   - Reasoning: for the largest grid assets, the bottleneck is physical manufacturing capacity (grain-oriented electrical steel, winding, testing, heavy haul). It is not diagnosis or knowledge. An "AI parts factory" cannot make a 400-ton custom transformer. This contradicts C4 and C5 at the heavy end.

6. INL Aurora Generator Test (2007), Wikipedia summary. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora_Generator_Test
   - A cyber command destroyed a 2.25 MW generator in about 3 minutes. Replacing a destroyed generator or transformer "could take more than a year" because many are custom-built.
   - Reasoning: a fault can be caused in minutes and take more than a year to repair. The repair chain is gated by manufacturing.

7. Stuxnet, Wikipedia summary. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuxnet
   - The likely entry vectors were contractor USB drives, engineer laptops, and field equipment brought in by contractors who maintained Siemens Step 7 systems.
   - Reasoning: the maintainer is the classic attack path into air-gapped ICS. That is why operators restrict who can repair, and why "anyone with AI knowledge can now fix it" meets structural resistance in security-sensitive settings. This is a strong counter to C2 and C3: the monopoly is partly a security perimeter.

8. Colonial Pipeline (2021), Wikipedia summary. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Pipeline_ransomware_attack
   - Six-day shutdown and a $4.4M ransom. A later PHMSA review found Colonial "had likely not adequately planned for manually shutting down and restarting the pipeline".
   - Reasoning: recovery and return to service, plus manual-operation readiness, cost the most time. Diagnosis or parts did not. This supports the idea that return to service and verification are the real bottleneck (C6).

9. 2015 Ukraine grid attack, Wikipedia summary. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Ukraine_power_grid_hack
   - About 230,000 people lost power for 1-6 hours. 30 substations were switched off. UPSs, modems, and RTUs were disabled or destroyed, and KillDisk wiped servers.
   - [PRIOR, unverified] The E-ISAC/SANS analysis found that firmware on serial-to-Ethernet converters was overwritten and bricked, and that operators ran substations manually for months afterwards.

10. Dragos OT Year in Review landing page. https://www.dragos.com/year-in-review/
    - Dragos tracks 26 OT threat groups and counts about 3,300 industrial organisations hit by ransomware.
    - Only about 30% of OT networks have visibility, 56% cannot see below the IT/OT boundary, and 88% struggle with detection and response.
    - Reasoning: "notice" and "diagnose" are weak in OT because instrumentation is missing, not because knowledge is missing. AI needs data it doesn't have. This partly supports C1 (noticing is a real stage) and tempers C3.

## Prior-knowledge items (not verified this session; check before citing)
- Senate Armed Services Committee report (May 2012) on counterfeit electronic parts in the DoD supply chain: about 1,800 cases, over 1 million suspect parts, most traced to China. Obsolescence and diminishing manufacturing sources were key drivers. Examples included P-8A Poseidon and C-130J displays. This led to NDAA FY2012 s.818 and DFARS 252.246-7007/7008, which require buying from OEMs or authorised distributors, or from "trusted suppliers" with testing.
- NRC 10 CFR Part 21 and 10 CFR 50 App. B: nuclear safety-related parts must come from QA-qualified (N-stamp) suppliers, or a licensee must "commercial-grade dedicate" a commercial part. Dedication often costs several times the part price. The supplier base shrank sharply after the 1980s. Analog-to-digital I&C upgrades take years of licensing review. Reverse-engineered or additively manufactured replacement parts are now being qualified, but slowly: early AM parts were installed at TVA Browns Ferry (2021) after multi-year qualification.
- FCC Covered List and NDAA FY2019 Section 889 ban Huawei, ZTE, Hikvision, Dahua, and Hytera equipment in federal procurement and FCC-authorised equipment. "Rip and replace" of covered telecom gear was estimated at about $4.98B, with the reimbursement program underfunded by about $3B for years. This shows security rules can force replacement over repair.
- EO 13920 (May 2020) barred bulk-power-system equipment from foreign adversaries. It was suspended in Jan 2021 and replaced by an RFI process. In 2025, Reuters reported undocumented communication devices in Chinese inverters and batteries.
- Rockwell PLC-5 (discontinued 2017) and Siemens S5 are end-of-life, yet still in very wide service. Hardware support comes from grey-market and refurbisher firms (e.g. Radwell, PLC Center, EU Automation) and from third-party repair houses. A large legacy ICS spares aftermarket exists, which shows that independent repair already works for many non-regulated industrial sites.
- US Copyright Office DMCA s.1201 exemptions (2015, 2018, 2021, 2024) widened repair rights for vehicles, appliances, and medical devices. In 2024 the Office declined a broad exemption for industrial equipment.
- IEC 62443-2-4 sets security requirements for IACS service providers, including integrators and maintainers. Large owners increasingly require it, which formalises "who may touch the system".

## Synthesis against hypotheses
- C1 (universal chain): mostly holds. CI adds stages the chain misses: authorisation and vetting of the repairer, supply-chain provenance checks (CIP-013 R1.2.5), and cyber-cleanliness checks of maintenance tools (the Stuxnet lesson). "Notice" is often the weakest stage, given the Dragos visibility numbers.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. OEMs do hold proprietary engineering tools, firmware, and licences. But the main constraint on who can repair in CI is regulated trust: vetted vendors, provenance, remote-access controls, and nuclear QA. Its stated purpose is security, and real attacks (Stuxnet, Volt Typhoon) show it is not pure rent-seeking.
- C3 (AI removes the gap): contradicts for CI. Knowledge isn't the binding constraint. Data for diagnosis is missing (30% visibility), and AI-guided repair by unvetted parties increases attack surface. AI could help understaffed small utilities with hygiene (the Unitronics case).
- C4 (10x cheaper diagnose/understand/source/deliver): contradicts at the heavy end. For transformers and generators, months to years go to manufacturing. Cheaper sourcing of obsolete chips worsens counterfeit risk (GAO: 16 of 16 bogus).
- C5 (AI parts factory solves bespoke parts): contradicts for CI. Electrical steel, 400-ton transformers, and qualified nuclear parts are bound by qualification and material supply. Opportunity is narrow and real: qualified reverse-engineering, AM of obsolete mechanical spares, and emulation of obsolete electronics, if paired with dedication or certification.
- C6 (inspection/certification essential and must evolve): strongly supports. Provenance verification, commercial-grade dedication, software integrity checks, and return-to-service validation are the bottleneck and a growth area.
- C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed. Some friction is deliberate security and safety design. Some is real dysfunction: transformer capacity, underfunded rip-and-replace, and orphaned legacy PLCs.

## Where money and time go
For CI heavy equipment: manufacturing and lead time (transformers 18-24+ months historically, longer since 2022), then qualification and commissioning. For ICS electronics: obsolete-spares procurement with authentication, and engineering effort for migration (rewriting logic, re-validating). For cyber incidents: recovery and return to service, plus manual-operations readiness (Colonial: 6 days).

## Company shape ideas
- Provenance and authentication service for obsolete ICS and electronic spares: test, certify, and keep chain of custody. It turns GAO-style counterfeit risk into a trusted supply.
- AI-assisted commercial-grade dedication and qualification packages for nuclear and utility spares, including reverse-engineered and AM parts. It shortens the certification step, which is where the time goes.
- Vetted, recorded maintenance-access and tooling platform: AI-guided field technicians with verified-clean engineering workstations and CIP-013/IEC 62443-2-4 evidence generated automatically.
- Legacy-controller emulation and migration: drop-in replacements for PLC-5/S5-era I/O with automatic logic translation and validation.
