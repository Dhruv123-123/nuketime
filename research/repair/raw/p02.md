## Findings

### 1. AOG Technics: scale and mechanism (hard data, UK prosecutor)
Source: SFO press release, "SFO secures 4-year prison sentence for aircraft parts fraud", GOV.UK, 23 Feb 2026. https://www.gov.uk/government/news/sfo-secures-4-year-prison-sentence-for-aircraft-parts-fraud
- Director Jose Alejandro Zamora Yrala, 38, pleaded guilty to fraudulent trading in Dec 2025. He was sentenced to 4 years 8 months at Southwark Crown Court.
- The fraud ran Jan 2019 to Jul 2023 from a home office in Surrey. It sold more than **60,000 engine parts worth GBP 6.9m**, mostly for the CFM56. Revenue was more than GBP 7.7m over four years, about **90% of it from fraud**.
- Estimated losses were **more than GBP 39.3m**, falling on airlines (including American and Ethiopian) and manufacturers.
- Mechanism: he altered genuine **Authorised Release Certificates** (EASA Form 1 / FAA 8130-3 type airworthiness tags) on a home computer. He also forged shipment memos showing the parts were bought from OEMs such as Safran, and invented staff (a "quality manager" and sales managers) who signed documents.
- How it was found: in 2023 **an airline contacted Safran to verify one part**. Safran found the certificate was fake and alerted the authorities. The UK CAA, FAA and EASA then issued safety alerts covering every AOG part, and aircraft were grounded worldwide.
- Reading: the scheme beat the system with **paperwork, not engineering**. One person with a PC defeated a document-based trust chain for 4.5 years. It came to light only because one buyer checked back with the OEM by hand. The OEM is the sole verification authority, so verification works as a trust monopoly.

### 2. AOG Technics: further context
Source: Wikipedia, "CFM International CFM56" (section "Parts of unknown origin"). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_CFM56 . Also Wikipedia, "Bogus aircraft parts". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogus_aircraft_parts
- Bloomberg broke the story in 2023. European regulators found that AOG had supplied parts of unknown origin with "false documents for repairs" on CFM56 engines. The owner gave his nationality as British on some forms and Venezuelan on others, which no counterparty caught.
- UNVERIFIED (memory, could not re-check): in Sept 2023, CFM/Safran and US carriers (Southwest, United, American, Delta) reported that roughly 100 or more engines had AOG parts installed. CFM, GE and Safran sued AOG in the English High Court. Verify before citing.

### 3. Senate Armed Services Committee 2012 investigation (hard data, regulator/legislature)
Source: SASC press release, 21 May 2012. https://www.armed-services.senate.gov/press-releases/senate-armed-services-committee-releases-report-on-counterfeit-electronic-parts (full report PDF: https://www.armed-services.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Counterfeit-Electronic-Parts.pdf , scanned)
- About **1,800 cases** of suspect counterfeit electronic parts, totalling **more than 1 million parts**, from **more than 650 companies**.
- In the 100+ cases traced to source, **more than 70% of the parts came from China**. The UK and Canada were the next largest, acting as resale points. China denied the staff visas.
- One Shenzhen firm (Hong Dark Electronic Trade) supplied about 84,000 suspect parts.
- Systems affected: Air Force cargo and surveillance aircraft (the report covers C-130J and C-27J display and P-8A cases), Special Operations helicopters (SH-60B), Global Hawk, THAAD, and Army thermal weapon sights. MDA said THAAD would likely have failed if the suspect mission-computer parts had failed.
- In each of the three cases examined in depth, **DoD did not know about the counterfeits** until the committee found them. Only **271 GIDEP reports** were filed across 2009-2010, and most of the 1,800 cases were never reported.
- **Supply-chain depth:** in the SH-60B case the parts **changed hands five times** before reaching the Raytheon subcontractor.
- Testing was inconsistent. In one L-3 case, a supplier sent 18 sample parts for testing and then sold more than 10,000 untested chips.
- SIA estimate: counterfeits cost US chipmakers more than **$7.5bn a year**, about 11,000 jobs (industry advocacy number).
- Policy response: section 818 of the FY2012 NDAA (Levin-McCain), signed 31 Dec 2011.

### 4. GAO 2012 sting: every part bought from online brokers was fake (hard data)
Source: GAO-12-375, Feb 2012. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-12-375
- GAO set up a fictitious company on two online platforms and asked for 16 military-grade parts: obsolete or rare parts, parts with post-production date codes, and **part numbers that do not exist**.
- 396 vendors responded (334 in China). Quotes came within a day and parts arrived in about 2 weeks.
- **0 of 16 parts were legitimate.** All 12 testable parts were "suspect counterfeit": re-marked, with wrong metal composition and altered date codes. Four vendors supplied parts for **bogus part numbers**.
- The sample cannot be generalised (GAO's own caveat), but the result shows that **fast sourcing of obsolete parts is exactly where counterfeits concentrate**. "Sourcing is fast and cheap" and "sourcing is trustworthy" pull against each other.

### 5. DFARS 252.246-7008: law now ranks sources by authenticity, not speed or price (regulation)
Source: acquisition.gov, DFARS 252.246-7008 "Sources of Electronic Parts" (clause JAN 2023, change 05/07/2026). https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars/252.246-7008-sources-electronic-parts.
- Statutory basis: Pub. L. 112-81 s.818(c)(3), amended in 2014 and 2015.
- Tier 1: the original manufacturer or its authorized suppliers, for parts in production or in stock.
- Tier 2: "contractor-approved suppliers", only when Tier 1 cannot supply. The contractor must apply counterfeit-prevention standards (inspection, testing, authentication) and **takes responsibility for authenticity**.
- Tier 3: any other source. Requires notice to the Contracting Officer, inspection, testing and authentication, and documentation.
- Traceability: the contractor must keep risk-based tracking **from the original manufacturer through government acceptance**. Where traceability cannot be established, it must test and authenticate.
- Reading: after 2012 the US government **wrote OEM and authorized-channel preference into law**. This deliberately strengthens the OEM "monopoly" on supply, in the name of authenticity.

### 6. Industry standards response: authentication as its own testing discipline (practice)
Source: Wikipedia, "Counterfeit electronic component". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfeit_electronic_component
- A 2010 Commerce Department BIS study found counterfeit incidents rose from **3,868 (2005) to 9,356 (2008)**. The two common types were outright fakes and used parts re-marked as higher grade.
- Standards: SAE AS5553 (2009, later AS5553A) and AS6081 (Nov 2012, distributors, adopted by DoD).
- Detection methods: X-ray, XRF, decapsulation, scanning acoustic microscopy, curve tracing, leak testing, solderability testing, and DNA marking.
- Reading: verifying authenticity is physical and lab-based, and it does not get cheaper because diagnostic knowledge gets cheaper.

### 7. Bogus aircraft parts: long history, low frequency, high consequence (history and data)
Source: Wikipedia, "Bogus aircraft parts". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogus_aircraft_parts
- An FAA study covering May 1973 to Apr 1996 linked unapproved parts to **174 accidents or incidents, with 17 deaths**. **None involved major commercial airlines.**
- By 1996, DOT IG (Mary Schiavo) campaigns had produced hundreds of convictions and about **$47m in fines**. The 1993 FBI sting "Operation Skycrook" and the Aircraft Safety Act of 2000 followed.
- Sources of bogus parts: counterfeits, theft, "strip and dip" re-plating, scrapyard and crash-scavenged parts (American 965 parts turned up for sale in Miami), time-expired life-limited parts, and production overruns.
- Boeing has reported counterfeit fasteners, electronic parts, and raw materials (titanium, steel, composite chemicals).

### 8. Partnair Flight 394 (1989): the canonical counterfeit-part fatality (history)
Source: Wikipedia, "Partnair Flight 394". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnair_Flight_394
- 55 people died. Three of the four vertical-fin attachment bolts were counterfeit and wrongly heat-treated, at about **60% of rated strength**. Vibration from an APU mount drove resonance until the tail separated. The tail had vibrated over 16 earlier flights without anyone diagnosing it.

### 9. NBC Bay Area / NTSB: gaps in documentation rules (journalism with FOIA data)
Source: NBC Bay Area, "Unapproved Airplane Parts Creating Safety Risk in Aviation". https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/local/unapproved-airplane-parts-creating-safety-risk-in-aviation/110281/
- About two dozen crashes since 2010 linked to unapproved parts (7 dead, 18 injured). FOIA data show **at least 135 FAA cases since 2011**.
- FAA quotes: "**FAA Form 8130-3 is not required by U.S. regulations for domestic use**" and "The absence of an FAA Form 8130-3 does not necessarily indicate a part is unapproved."
- Price gap that drives the gray market: a used DC-10 decoder panel sold online for $60, against an estimated new price of more than $1,000.
- Civil penalties of up to $32,140 per violation. One 2015 civil verdict was $13.36m (an undocumented seat-track part).
- The FAA's view is that unapproved parts are "a tiny fraction" of hundreds of millions of aviation parts.

### 10. PMA: the legal route around OEM parts monopoly already exists, and runs through certification (regulation)
Source: Wikipedia, "Parts Manufacturer Approval". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval
- FAA PMA (14 CFR 21.303, now Part 21 Subpart K) lets third parties make replacement parts, through identicality, licensure, an STC, or Test & Computation. Approval needs the ACO (design) and the MIDO (production).
- The aftermarket grew in the 1980s as airlines looked for cheaper parts. The FAA's 1990s "Enhanced Enforcement" pushed many parts into formal approval. Australia, China and the EU (EPA parts) are building similar regimes.
- Reading: "make the part when the OEM won't" is a solved *legal* problem only if you go through design and production approval. That gate, not the knowledge or the machining, is the bottleneck.

### 11. FAA SUP program (regulator; the faa.gov page was blocked)
- The FAA Suspected Unapproved Parts program (https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/safety/programs/sups , returned 403) takes reports on FAA Form 8120-11. It is described in AC 21-29 (rev D). It publishes Unapproved Parts Notifications and runs investigations.
- Definitions (from AC 21-29, described from background knowledge, not re-fetched): an *unapproved part* is one not produced or maintained under approved processes, or not conforming to approved data. A *suspected unapproved part* is one that someone believes might be unapproved.
- The surviving data points are in finding 9 (at least 135 cases since 2011) and finding 7 (174 events from 1973 to 1996).

## Synthesis / reasoning

1. **The scarce layer in parts sourcing is trust and provenance, not knowledge.** None of the cases (AOG, SASC, GAO, Partnair) failed because someone did not know how to diagnose or fit a part. They failed because nobody could cheaply verify that a part was what its paperwork said. Verification ran on paper certificates, phone or email checks with the OEM, and brokers with no reporting duty. GIDEP got 271 reports against roughly 1,800 cases.
2. **Fast, cheap sourcing and trustworthy sourcing pull against each other.** GAO got quotes within a day and parts within 2 weeks, and 100% were fake. The SH-60B parts passed through five hands. The market response, codified in DFARS, was to *narrow* sourcing to OEMs and authorized channels. That pushes against C4's claim that sourcing costs drop.
3. **AI cuts both ways.** AOG needed only a home PC to alter release certificates and invent a quality manager. Generative AI makes convincing forged ARCs, invented staff and email trails cheaper still. If AI collapses the cost of producing *plausible documentation*, document-based trust gets weaker, and physical authentication plus cryptographically signed provenance become more valuable. This argues against C3 as stated: AI removes some knowledge gaps but widens the authenticity gap.
4. **Bespoke parts (C5).** In regulated domains, a part made outside approved design and production data *is* an unapproved part by definition. The working alternative is PMA, DER repairs or owner-produced parts, which are gated by certification, not fabrication. An "AI parts factory" is only legal on aircraft if it holds production approval for the specific part. In defense it sits in DFARS Tier 3 and carries the full testing burden.
5. **Inspection (C6), strongly supported.** Detection depended on OEM back-checks and lab authentication. The industry answer has been more inspection standards: AS5553, AS6081, ASA-100 accreditation for distributors, and AS9120.
6. **Structural inefficiency (C7), mixed.** The paper chain is clearly outdated: the FAA does not even require 8130-3 tags for domestic parts, and AOG was found by luck. But much of the "inefficiency" (authorized-source rules, back-to-birth traceability, OEM verification) is a deliberate defence that grew out of fatalities and fraud. Rebuilding has to keep that function, ideally making it cheaper and stronger, not remove it.
7. **The opportunity is to make authenticity cheap.** Possible products include signed digital release certificates verifiable at the point of receipt, an OEM and authorized-distributor provenance graph or API, automated incoming inspection (AI on X-ray/XRF/SAM images, AI checks of documents against OEM records), and AI flagging of anomalies such as the nationality mismatch, a PO sequence that does not fit, or an unknown signatory.

## Stance summary
- C1: mixed. Sourcing has a hidden sub-step, authentication/provenance check, which the chain leaves out. It also shows failure loops: Partnair's tail vibrated for 16 flights without diagnosis.
- C2: mixed. A knowledge gap exists for *verification* (only the OEM could say whether a cert was real). That is a trust and records monopoly, not a know-how monopoly, and it is partly justified by safety.
- C3: contradicts. AI lowers the cost of forging documents. Authenticity needs physical tests and records that AI does not provide.
- C4: contradicts or mixed. Cheaper, faster sourcing through open brokers is where counterfeits concentrate. Regulators answered by restricting sources. Gains are possible only if verification is automated.
- C5: contradicts. A self-made part is an unapproved part unless it passes PMA or equivalent design and production approval.
- C6: supports strongly.
- C7: mixed. The paper-based trust chain is weak (and was beaten by one person), but the friction is largely a designed fraud defence.
