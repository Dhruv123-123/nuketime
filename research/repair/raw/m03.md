## How the system works (process map)
**[BACKGROUND], regulatory structure, high confidence:**
- An airline mechanic may repair structure without new engineering only when the damage is within the limits of the OEM Structural Repair Manual (SRM). The SRM gives allowable damage limits (ADL) and standard repairs. Under 14 CFR 43.13(a), work must follow the manufacturer's manual or ICA, or other methods acceptable to the Administrator.
- Damage outside SRM limits, or a repair not in the SRM, needs approved repair data:
  - **US:** a Boeing ODA approval on FAA Form 8100-9, a DER-approved repair on FAA Form 8110-3, or approval through a Part 21 Subpart M repair design approval holder.
  - **EASA:** under Part 21 Subpart M (21.A.432B and following), the repair must be approved by the TC holder or an approved Design Organisation (DOA). Airbus issues an RDAS (Repair Design Approval Sheet) through AIRTAC.
- Workflow at Boeing: the airline's engineering group raises a Service Request through MyBoeingFleet / Boeing Communication System. Boeing Field Service representatives (resident at large operators and in the field) help with triage. If the aircraft is AOG, Boeing's Commercial Aviation Services operations center handles it as priority, and Boeing Repair Engineering issues the disposition (often a temporary repair first, then a permanent one).
- Workflow at Airbus: AIRTAC (Airbus Technical AOG Centre, Toulouse, 24/7) takes "TechRequests" through the AirbusWorld portal. Resident Customer Support Managers sit at operators. Airbus can send an on-site structural team for large damage, and issues the RDAS for non-SRM repairs.
- Many large carriers hold their own repair-design authority, so they do not always need the OEM. Examples: Lufthansa Technik and AFI KLM E&M as EASA DOAs; Delta TechOps and others using in-house or consultant DERs. Third-party DER and DOA firms sell non-SRM repair approvals as an alternative to the OEM. **So the knowledge "monopoly" is contestable and is regulated by delegation, not by secrecy.**

## Findings

1. **[FETCHED] AOG cost (Boeing estimate, dated).** Boeing's Commercial Airplanes Operations Center material (archived 2008, cited on Wikipedia) says a 1-2 hour AOG "can cost $10,000 to $20,000," and the cost can reach "$150,000 per hour" depending on aircraft type and route. Source: Wikipedia "Aircraft on ground", citing a Boeing archive page. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_on_ground. Kind: data (OEM-sourced, old).

2. **[FETCHED] A repair that skipped the SRM killed 225 people.** China Airlines 611: after a 1980 tailstrike, the airline's crew did not follow Boeing's SRM (which called for replacing the skin, or cutting out the damage and adding a correctly sized doubler). They put an undersized doubler over the scratched skin. Fatigue cracks grew for 22 years until the aircraft broke up in flight in 2002, killing 225. Investigators: a proper doubler would probably have prevented it. Tar stains in maintenance photos that could have revealed the cracks were not followed up. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_Airlines_Flight_611. Kind: history / regulator (ASC investigation). **Relevance:** the "understanding" step carries safety-critical liability. The repair data and the inspection are what stand between a repair and a catastrophe.

3. **[FETCHED] Even the OEM's own field team got it wrong: 520 dead.** JAL 123: Boeing technicians repaired the aft pressure bulkhead after a 1978 tailstrike. They used two splice plates instead of one continuous plate with three rivet rows, leaving about 70% of the correct fatigue resistance. JAL's post-repair inspection missed it because overlapping plates hid the defect. It failed after 12,318 flights in 1985, killing 520. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Air_Lines_Flight_123. Kind: history. **Relevance:** the failure was in execution and inspection, not missing knowledge. The OEM had the knowledge and still failed. That cuts against C2/C3 and supports C6.

4. **[FETCHED] Inspection failure, not a knowledge gap.** Aloha 243 (1988): the NTSB found Aloha's maintenance program "failed to detect the presence of significant disbonding and fatigue damage." Night-time checks made inspection harder, and the FAA had not mandated the full lap-joint inspection in Boeing's service bulletin. The knowledge existed (Boeing had a service bulletin); the system failed to apply and detect. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aloha_Airlines_Flight_243. Kind: history / regulator. **Background:** this led to the US Aging Aircraft Safety Act and later the FAA damage-tolerance and widespread-fatigue-damage rules. These make repair assessment (for example, repair assessment programs for pressurized fuselage repairs) a permanent obligation, and the SRM grew to include damage-tolerance inspection requirements for repairs.

5. **[FETCHED] The 2023-26 bottleneck is physical capacity and parts, not diagnosis.** Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal issue: all ~3,000 PW1100G engines were recalled for inspection. P&W said inspections and shop visits take 250-300 days and expected an average of ~350 A320neo-family aircraft on the ground through 2026. Estimated cost to P&W and partners was $6-7bn, about 80% of it customer compensation. The diagnosis (contaminated powder-metal discs) was known; the time goes to shop capacity, parts (HPT discs) and turnaround. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_%26_Whitney_PW1000G. Kind: data / news.

6. **[FETCHED] Size of the MRO market.** Oliver Wyman 2024-2034: MRO about $104bn in 2024 (from $101bn in 2023), heading to $124bn by 2034. Named post-COVID pressures: inflation and "raw material and labor shortages"; OEMs are at capacity limits. https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2024/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2024-2034.html. Kind: practitioner / investor view.

7. **[BACKGROUND] Cost of supply-chain problems.** IATA with Aviation Week Network (Dec 2024) estimated supply-chain problems would cost airlines more than $11bn in 2025:
   - ~$4.2bn excess fuel (older aircraft kept flying)
   - ~$3.1bn extra maintenance
   - ~$2.6bn engine leasing
   - ~$1.4bn extra spare-parts inventory

   Over 1,000 aircraft were parked, many awaiting engines or parts. The URL I tried (iata.org/en/pressroom/2024-releases/2024-12-10-03/) was the wrong release; search iata.org for "supply chain issues ... $11 billion." Kind: data (industry body). **Relevance:** the money goes into sourcing, delivering and capacity, not into the knowledge steps.

8. **[BACKGROUND] OEM aftermarket economics.** OEMs and engine makers earn most of their lifetime profit in the aftermarket: spare parts, licensed repairs, power-by-the-hour. Examples: GE Aerospace and RTX/P&W report that commercial services are well over half of segment revenue, and parts margins are high. Engine OEMs restrict DER repairs and PMA parts through warranty and flight-hour agreements, and by controlling data. The airline and MRO trade group AFRA, IATA's aftermarket advocacy, and the "Right to repair aircraft" EU competition complaints (for example, IATA's 2023-24 statements on OEM aftermarket practices) argue that OEMs limit competition. Kind: campaigner / investor view. **Relevance:** supports C2 in the sense of commercial lock-in, more than a true knowledge gap.

9. **[BACKGROUND] PMA parts and DER repairs as the existing "independent" route.** FAA PMA parts (14 CFR 21 Subpart K) and DER repairs have existed for decades. Firms such as HEICO and Wencor (now in HEICO/Boeing orbits) reverse-engineer and certify replacement parts and repairs at roughly 30-50% below OEM list. Each still needs certification data, qualification testing and FAA approval, typically taking months per part number. Kind: practitioner. **Relevance:** C5 "AI parts factory" already exists in an analog, regulated form. The cost is in certification, testing and qualification, not in making the part.

10. **[BACKGROUND] Typical non-SRM repair turnaround.** Practitioners report:
    - AOG structural dispositions from Boeing or AIRTAC usually arrive within hours to a day or two, often as a temporary or time-limited repair.
    - Non-AOG requests are usually answered in days to weeks.
    - Final RDAS or 8100-9 for a permanent repair can take weeks to months (time-limited repairs carry an inspection or replacement deadline).

    Airbus has stated AOG response-time targets (published in its support services marketing; not re-verified). Kind: practitioner view (unverified). **Relevance:** for an AOG, OEM engineering response is often not the longest pole. Parts logistics, ferry permits and mechanic availability often are.

11. **[BACKGROUND] Predictive maintenance / AI already in use.** Airbus Skywise (data platform, 10,000+ connected aircraft per Airbus claims), Boeing AnalytX / Airplane Health Management, Lufthansa Technik AVIATAR and GE engine health monitoring all aim at the "notice" and "diagnose" steps. Claims of fewer unscheduled removals and fewer AOG events are vendor-sourced and modest, typically low tens of percent on specific components. None says the overall cost has fallen by an order of magnitude. Kind: oem_view. **Relevance:** contradicts the idea that AI makes the knowledge step disappear. Data access is itself contested; airlines own the data, while OEMs run the platforms.

12. **[BACKGROUND] Labor and inspection are the scarce layer.** Oliver Wyman's and ATEC's mechanic workforce studies forecast a shortfall of roughly 10%+ of the North American mechanic workforce in the mid-to-late 2020s. Inspection (RII, NDT Level II/III) and certifying-staff signatures (EASA Part-66 B1/B2, FAA A&P with inspection authorization) cannot currently be automated in regulation. Kind: practitioner / data.

## Stances (reasoning)
- **C1 (universal chain): mixed.** It matches the aviation flow well (detection, troubleshooting with fault isolation, engineering disposition, parts, repair, inspection/RII, certificate of release to service). But aviation adds legally separate steps the chain leaves out: an approved-data step, deferral under MEL/CDL (fly with the defect, repair later), temporary repairs with deadlines, and permanent follow-up inspections (damage-tolerance repair inspections continue for decades). It is iterative, not linear.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed.**
  - For: OEMs own the type design data, the SRM is licensed, and repairs outside the SRM default back to the OEM. OEMs use data and warranty to block DER/PMA routes.
  - Against: the legal system already spreads repair-design authority across DERs, ODAs, DOAs, and the engineering departments of large airlines and MROs. Large operators have deep in-house knowledge. The binding constraint is **approval authority and liability** (who signs and is accountable), not knowledge itself. Findings 3-4 show failures with the knowledge present.
- **C3 (AI erases the gap): contradicts.** AI can draft dispositions, search SRMs and fault-isolation manuals, and triage. But approval needs an accountable certificated signer who holds or can access type-design data (stress analysis, fatigue and damage-tolerance substantiation). AI cannot be the approver under current FAA/EASA rules. Findings 2-3 show the error costs are catastrophic, which keeps human sign-off.
- **C4 (10x cost drop in four steps): contradicts / mixed.** Most cost and time goes to physical capacity: engine shops, parts (findings 5, 7), labor (12), and AOG downtime (1). The knowledge steps are a small share of a repair's cost. AI could cut engineering-disposition time from days to hours, which matters a lot for AOG. But 10x across the four steps including parts delivery is not supported; delivery is logistics, already optimized with AOG couriers and pooling.
- **C5 (AI parts factory): contradicts.** PMA and DER already let third parties make replacement parts. The barrier is certification: material and process qualification, conformity, and FAA PMA approval taking months. Additive manufacturing has a slow certification path (for example, the GE LEAP fuel nozzle took years). Making the part is the easy bit.
- **C6 (inspection essential, must evolve): supports.** Findings 2-4 and 12 all point to inspection, sign-off and repair assessment as the safety layer. Inspection labor is scarce. Automated NDT, drone or borescope AI, and digital records are where change is happening.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.** There are real structural rents: OEM aftermarket lock-in and data control. But much of the "inefficiency" is deliberate safety redundancy written in blood (approved data, independent inspection, traceability). Aviation's maintenance safety record is excellent. "Rebuild" framing is risky. Better: "speed up the approval and evidence layer inside the rules."

## Where the money and time go
For airliners, cost and time go to:
1. AOG / out-of-service downtime: $10k-$150k per hour; ~350 GTF aircraft parked for months.
2. Physical shop capacity and parts lead times: engine shop visits of 250-300 days in the GTF case; $11bn+ in 2025 supply-chain costs per IATA.
3. Certified labor.

The engineering disposition step (OEM tech request, RDAS / 8100-9) is a real but bounded delay. It matters most for AOG and for non-SRM structural damage, and is often hours to days. It is not the dominant cost.

## Real bottleneck
Approved data plus accountable sign-off (the right to certify a repair as airworthy, with liability), along with physical capacity (shops, parts, certified mechanics and inspectors). Knowledge exists, but it is gated by type-design ownership and the delegation system.

## Company shapes suggested
- An AI co-pilot for airline and MRO engineering departments, plus DER/DOA firms: drafts non-SRM repair dispositions and substantiation packages from SRM, damage photos and scans, for a human DER/DOA to approve. It sells speed in AOG.
- Automated damage capture and inspection (drone or robot visual and NDT, with AI damage mapping against SRM ADLs) to cut the notice, diagnose and inspect time, and to produce the evidence record regulators want.
- A certification-evidence platform for PMA / additive-manufactured replacement parts: it cuts the months-long qualification and approval cycle. Certification, not fabrication, is the moat.
- Becoming an approved repair-design organisation (DOA/ODA-like) that is AI-native. This captures the regulated sign-off rent rather than fighting it.
