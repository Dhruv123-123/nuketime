# e10: Marine commercial shipping repair (shipyards, class societies DNV/ABS/Lloyd's Register, engine makers MAN/Wärtsilä service): how classification and OEMs structure repair

## Findings

### 1. Class covers almost all tonnage, and the market is a concentrated oligopoly [V]
- IACS has 12 member societies. Its rules and standards cover "more than 90%" of the world's cargo-carrying tonnage, across design, construction and through-life compliance.
- Source: IACS homepage, https://www.iacs.org.uk/
- Over 50 organisations do marine classification, but the big ones are DNV, BV, ABS, ClassNK and LR. A class certificate is in practice needed to register a ship, insure it and sometimes to enter port.
- Source: Wikipedia, Ship classification society, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_classification_society
- Implication: the "safety inspection / return to service" gate in shipping is run by a private oligopoly that flag states (as Recognized Organizations under the IMO RO Code) and insurers rely on. It is not purely a state function.

### 2. Class attests to compliance with its own rules, not to safety, and courts have mostly shielded it [V]
- Societies disclaim responsibility for safety or seaworthiness (Wikipedia, as above).
- **Prestige (2002):** ABS had certified the ship "in class" for its final voyage.
  - Spain sued ABS in the SDNY in 2003, claiming about $700M.
  - The case was dismissed on 2 January 2007. The court held ABS to be a "person" under the CLC, so it was exempt from direct liability. [BK] The Second Circuit affirmed in 2012, finding no recklessness.
  - Spanish investigators found that ABS's 1996 Safe Hull programme had judged the sister ships to be in terminal fatigue decline. Those sisters were scrapped between 1999 and 2002. The Prestige hull later failed in the same frame region (61-71).
  - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prestige_oil_spill
- **Erika (1999):** In January 2008 a Paris court held RINA (the class society) jointly liable with Total, the owner and the manager for €192M. The appeal was lost in March 2010. [BK] The Cour de cassation upheld this in 2012.
  - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Erika
- Reading:
  - The inspection layer is a liability-and-trust product, not only a technical one.
  - Its failures were failures of incentive and follow-through, not of knowledge. Class knew about the fatigue in the Prestige case.
  - This bears on C2 and C3: the knowledge existed, and acting on it was the failure.

### 3. Competition among class societies erodes standards, so class-hopping is restricted [V]
- The European Commission raised concerns that competitive pressure could lower standards.
- IACS adopted the Transfer of Class Agreement in 2009 to stop "class hopping", where owners move to a laxer society to avoid outstanding repairs.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_classification_society
- This supports C7 in one specific sense: the repair-inspection system has adverse incentives (the owner pays the inspector). But the industry's fix was more rigidity, not less.

### 4. Engine OEMs: knowledge stays with the designer, manufacturing goes to licensees, aftermarket is captured [V + BK]
- [V] MAN Energy Solutions was renamed Everllence on 4 June 2025.
  - Revenue was €4.3bn in 2024, with about 15,000 employees.
  - Two-stroke engines are designed in Copenhagen but "manufactured by international licensees in the immediate vicinity of dockyards".
  - "Approximately 50% of global trade is moved by MAN engines."
  - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAN_Energy_Solutions
- [V] Everllence PrimeServ lists:
  - spare parts, service letters, retrofit and upgrade, digital solutions (CEON, PrimeServ Assist), service agreements, technical service;
  - "Omnicare 3rd Party Services", which services other makers' equipment;
  - a coming Cyber Security Update Service for engine control systems.
  - Source: https://www.everllence.com/
- [BK] The two-stroke design market is a duopoly of MAN and WinGD. Licensees such as HD Hyundai, Hanwha, CSSC and Mitsui build the engines.
- The structure:
  - The design IP and service letters (the "knowledge") stay with the designer.
  - Physical making is already distributed.
  - Over the engine's 25-plus-year life, OEM-genuine parts and service engineers are the margin pool.

### 5. For Wärtsilä, services are the core business [V + BK]
- [V] Revenue was €6.914bn in 2025 (2023 net sales were €6,015M).
  - The installed base is over 180,000 MW.
  - There are over 4,500 field service professionals in more than 160 locations across more than 70 countries.
  - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C3%A4rtsil%C3%A4
- [V] Wärtsilä marine services page:
  - Lifecycle and service agreements give "predictable costs and performance". Results "can even be guaranteed with a special agreement".
  - Expert Insight does anomaly detection.
  - Condition-based maintenance planning "extend[s] intervals and avoid[s] unnecessary overhauls".
  - "More than 90% of service agreement customers choose to renew."
  - Source: https://www.wartsila.com/marine/services
- [BK] Services were roughly half or more of Wärtsilä net sales in 2023-2025. Agreements are a growing share of service sales.
- Reading:
  - The OEM is already selling "AI-ish" remote diagnosis bundled with parts and labour under multi-year contracts.
  - AI diagnosis does not remove the OEM. It deepens the OEM's lock-in, because the OEM owns the fleet-wide sensor data and design data.
  - This contradicts the simple form of C3.

### 6. The survey cycle sets the repair rhythm, so most repair is scheduled, not noticed [BK]
- Under SOLAS and class rules, ships get annual surveys, an intermediate survey (around year 2.5) and a renewal/special survey every 5 years.
  - There are two bottom inspections in each 5-year period, at most 36 months apart. An in-water survey can replace one for eligible ships.
- Machinery can be on Continuous Survey or a Planned Maintenance System scheme. Condition-monitoring notations (for example DNV's) can extend intervals.
- The ISM Code (section 10) requires a documented maintenance system. Planned maintenance software such as AMOS and ShipManager is standard.
- Implication for C1:
  - Much of marine repair does not start with "notice". It is calendar- and class-driven bulk work, bundled into drydock windows.
  - Unplanned damage repair follows the founder's chain more closely: damage, class notified, occasional survey, condition of class, repair attended by a surveyor, class cleared.
  - So C1 holds as a logical skeleton, but the economics are dominated by planned batching.

### 7. Class is part of the repair itself, not just the final inspection [BK]
- Repairs to class items need class-approved procedures or materials (and often type-approved or certified parts) and a surveyor on site.
- Temporary repairs get a "condition of class" with a deadline.
- IACS Recommendation 47 (Shipbuilding and Repair Quality Standard) and UR Z17 cover the repair work and the service suppliers class approves (thickness measurement firms, in-water survey divers, drone/remote inspection providers).
- The founder's sequential chain (repair, then inspection) is really interleaved: class approves the plan, witnesses the work and certifies parts.

### 8. Remote surveys and drones are already happening: inspection is evolving (C6) [BK]
- IACS Recommendation 42 (2016) gives guidelines for remote inspection techniques (drones, ROVs, rope access).
- The large societies scaled up remote surveys during COVID (2020-21). DNV, LR and ABS all offer remote surveys and remote approval of minor repairs using video and photo evidence from crew.
- [BK, unverified] DNV reported tens of thousands of remote surveys, and at one point a large share of its occasional surveys were remote.
- Implication:
  - Class has already moved part of "understanding" and "inspection" onto crew with smartphones, which supports C6.
  - The remaining scarce piece is the accountable signature and liability. Seeing the defect is no longer the hard part.

### 9. Additive manufacturing of spares: class has frameworks, but only for selected parts (C5) [BK]
- Class frameworks:
  - DNV published an additive-manufacturing class guideline (around 2017, DNVGL-CG-0197).
  - ABS published AM guidance (around 2018).
  - LR has AM approval schemes.
- Deliveries and pilots:
  - Wilhelmsen, with Ivaldi and Thyssenkrupp, began delivering 3D-printed spares to ships around 2019-2020, mostly simple non-critical parts.
  - Navies (the US Navy, for example) print parts on board and ashore.
- What blocks scale:
  - qualifying the material, process and part for each part number;
  - the OEM's IP and data (no drawings);
  - class approval for critical components such as crankshafts, pressure-retaining parts and turbocharger rotors.
- Contradicts C5 as stated ("effectively solved"). It supports a narrower claim: digital inventory for long-tail, low-criticality spares is real and growing.

### 10. Labour and hands are the bottleneck more than knowledge [BK]
- The BIMCO/ICS Seafarer Workforce Report 2021 estimated a shortage of about 26,000 STCW-certified officers in 2021. It projected that about 89,500 more officers would be needed by 2026.
- Ship repair yards (Singapore, China, the Middle East, Turkey) report steady shortages of skilled welders, fitters and engine mechanics.
- Drydock slots are seasonal and geographically constrained. Owners position ballast legs to reach a cheap yard (China, Middle East, Turkey).
- This contradicts C3 and C4: removing the knowledge gap does not create hands, docks or slots.

### 11. Off-hire time cost dwarfs diagnosis cost [BK]
- A routine drydock for a large merchant ship is typically 2-4 weeks. Yard bills run from a few hundred thousand dollars to several million.
- Off-hire (lost charter income) adds tens of thousands of dollars per day for tankers, bulkers and boxships, depending on the market.
- For unplanned breakdowns, the extra cost is mostly time:
  - deviation to port;
  - waiting for a flown-in OEM service engineer;
  - waiting for a long-lead part (cylinder liners, crankshaft, turbocharger cartridge).
- Diagnosis itself is usually a small share of cost.
- So cheaper diagnosis matters mainly when it turns unplanned failures into planned ones, or shortens the parts-and-people waiting loop.

### 12. Some repair inefficiency is imposed by policy, not knowledge [BK]
- US Jones Act / Tariff Act (19 USC 1466) applies a 50% ad valorem duty on foreign repairs of US-flag vessels.
- Sanctions, flag and cabotage rules, and IP and warranty terms ("use genuine parts or void the warranty") also shape where and by whom ships are repaired.
- This supports C7 in part: some inefficiency is deliberate (protection, liability, rents). But it is political and legal, which AI does not dissolve.

### 13. Cyber has become a class requirement, which opens a security-tech angle [BK]
- IACS UR E26 (cyber resilience of ships) and UR E27 (cyber resilience of onboard systems and equipment) became mandatory for new ships contracted from 1 July 2024.
- [V] Everllence advertises a Cyber Security Update Service for engine control systems (https://www.everllence.com/).
- Implication: software and firmware "repair" (patching OT systems on ships) is now inside the class and OEM regime. This is a new repair category that fits the founder's security-tech remit.

### 14. Ship managers and superintendents already hold independent technical knowledge [BK]
- Third-party ship managers (V.Group, Anglo-Eastern, Synergy, Wilhelmsen Ship Management, BSM) manage thousands of vessels. They employ technical superintendents who plan and specify repairs.
- Independent engine overhaul shops and will-fit parts suppliers compete with OEMs, especially for older engines.
- So there is no simple "knowledge monopoly". Knowledge is spread across crew, managers, independent workshops, class and OEMs.
- The real monopoly pockets are:
  - OEM design data and service letters;
  - class's legal authority to sign off.

## Reasoning against the claims
- **C1: mixed.** The chain is right as a skeleton. But in shipping, most repair is calendar-batched into class survey and drydock windows, and class is woven through the repair (plan approval, attendance, part certification), not only at the end. "Notice" is often replaced by "the schedule says so".
- **C2: mixed.**
  - Real knowledge monopolies exist: OEM design data, service letters and the engine control software of MAN/WinGD/Wärtsilä; class's rule interpretation and signature.
  - But owners, managers and independent yards have deep expertise.
  - The big disasters (Prestige, Erika) happened despite knowledge, through incentives.
  - The OEM lock-in is IP, data and warranty more than tacit knowledge.
- **C3: contradicts.**
  - OEMs and class are the ones deploying AI and remote monitoring (Wärtsilä Expert Insight; Everllence CEON and PrimeServ Assist; DNV/ABS/LR remote surveys). Fleet data is proprietary, so AI may widen the OEM's advantage.
  - Liability and approval cannot be replaced by knowledge.
- **C4: mixed, leaning contradicts.**
  - Diagnosis and "understanding" can get much cheaper.
  - But the dominant costs are off-hire time, yard labour and steel, drydock slots, physical logistics to a moving ship and part lead times. An order-of-magnitude cut across the four steps does not translate into an order-of-magnitude cut in total repair cost.
  - It could matter a lot for unplanned breakdowns, where waiting for an engineer or part dominates.
- **C5: contradicts as stated.** Class frameworks for AM exist, and long-tail non-critical spares are being printed. But critical parts need per-part qualification and class approval, and OEM drawings and IP are the barrier.
- **C6: supports.** Class remains the legal gate (flag RO role, insurance, port entry). It is already evolving: remote surveys, drones (IACS Rec 42), AM approvals, cyber URs E26/E27. Its liability is the real asset.
- **C7: mixed.**
  - There are adverse structures: owner-pays-inspector, class-hopping, Jones Act duty, OEM aftermarket rents.
  - But much "inefficiency" is deliberate redundancy and liability management in a high-consequence domain.
  - The system is also adapting incrementally (condition-based survey extensions, remote surveys, lifecycle agreements).
  - "Rebuild from scratch" is not what the evidence shows. "Rebuild specific layers" (parts qualification, remote attestation, independent data) is more defensible.

## Company shapes suggested
1. **Independent condition-monitoring and evidence layer accepted by class.**
   - Sensors plus AI producing survey-grade evidence (hull, machinery) that class accepts for remote surveys and survey-interval extension.
   - It sells against off-hire days and is OEM-agnostic. This is the "inspection must evolve" wedge.
2. **Qualified digital-inventory and on-demand spares for long-tail, out-of-production marine parts.**
   - Reverse engineering, AM or CNC, and a class-approval pipeline for each part number.
   - Start with non-critical parts for older engines whose OEM support has lapsed.
   - The bottleneck it targets is qualification, not printing.
3. **OT cyber "repair" for ships under IACS E26/E27.**
   - Patching, configuration attestation and incident recovery for engine, ballast and navigation controllers, working with or around OEM update services.
4. **Breakdown-response orchestration.**
   - Compresses the unplanned-repair loop (diagnose remotely, pre-position the part, book the nearest qualified independent technician, pre-agree the scope with class).
   - The time saved is worth far more than the diagnosis itself.
