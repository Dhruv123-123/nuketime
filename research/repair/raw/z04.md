## Findings

### 1. EU Ecodesign Regulation 2023/1670 (smartphones and tablets) turns modular, standard-tool repair into law (regulator, hard rule)
- Source: EUR-Lex, Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/1670, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1670/oj
- Applies from 20 June 2025. Spare parts (battery, display, cameras, charging port, buttons, mics, speakers, hinge) must stay available **7 years** after end of placement on market.
- Delivery: **within 5 working days** for years 1-5, 10 working days for the remaining 2.
- Repair information (disassembly maps, wiring diagrams, **diagnostic codes**, repair instructions) to professional repairers: registration decided within 5 working days, access within **1 working day**. Serialised parts: software tool access within 3 working days.
- Fasteners must be removable/reusable/resupplied; display and battery replaceable with no tool, supplied tool or basic tools; battery replaceable by a layperson (exemption for IP67 devices with 83% capacity after 500 cycles / 80% after 1000).
- Reading: the regulator's view of what blocks repair is not "nobody knows how" but parts availability, delivery time, information access, fasteners/adhesives and **parts serialisation**. These are design and commercial-policy constraints, which modularity + mandated access address. It treats "diagnosing/understanding" (information) and "sourcing/delivering" (parts in 5 days) as things solved by mandate and standardisation, not by AI.

### 2. Fairphone 3 LCA (Fraunhofer IZM, 2020): the "modularity overhead" is small; benefits depend on real lifetime extension (study)
- Source: Fraunhofer IZM for Fairphone, "Life Cycle Assessment of the Fairphone 3", https://www.fairphone.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Fairphone_3_LCA.pdf (text extracted locally)
- Total GWP 39.5 kg CO2e per phone.
- Modularity overhead (extra housing, flex cables, press-fit connectors) = **0.744 kg CO2e = 2.3% of production GWP**, but **17.2% of ADP elements** (abiotic resource depletion) because of gold-plated connector contacts.
- Per-year impact drops **29%** going from 3 to 5 years of use and **42%** at 7 years; "This is still the case if repair is needed... This is, however, dependent on the effective lifetime extension that is achieved in reality."
- The overhead fell vs Fairphone 2 because pogo-pin connectors were replaced with press-fit connectors "not a unique feature of the Fairphone, as they can also be found in more conventionally designed smartphones."
- Reading: modularity's cost is mostly a resource/precious-metal cost in connectors; its benefit is entirely conditional on owners actually keeping and repairing devices. No repair-cost-in-euros figure; this is an environmental, not economic, study.

### 3. Fairphone's commercial numbers: modular repairable phones remain a niche, thin-margin business (data)
- Source: Wikipedia "Fairphone" (citing company annual reports), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairphone
- Units/revenue/EBITDA: 2018 22,623 / EUR10.8m / -5.6m; 2020 94,985 / 35.9m / +3.7m; 2022 115,681 / 58.9m / +4.5m; 2023 100,107 / 54.7m / **-14.3m**; 2024 103,053 / 54.4m / +1.7m; **2025 145,259 / EUR73.3m / +0.17m**.
- Context: ~1.2 billion smartphones ship per year globally [background], so Fairphone is ~0.01% share after 12 years. EUR49m impact round in 2023 (Invest-NL, ABN AMRO).
- iFixit 10/10 on all phones except Fairphone 1 and first earbuds. Fairphone 5: 10 swappable parts, 5-year warranty, 8 years support; Fairphone 6: 7 years OS, 8 years security.
- Reading: modularity demonstrably works technically but has not created a mass-market cost advantage; the binding constraint is demand/distribution/scale, not repair knowledge.

### 4. Fairphone 5 spare-part prices: modular repair is cheap at the part level (data)
- Source: https://www.fairphone.com/spare-parts (fetched 2026-10-20)
- Battery EUR39.95, display EUR99.95, USB-C port EUR19.95, back cover EUR24.95, main camera EUR114.90, selfie camera EUR34.95 (ex shipping).
- Comparison [background, not re-verified]: Apple out-of-warranty iPhone Pro-class screen replacement is roughly USD 279-379 and battery ~USD 99-119 (Apple's estimator failed to load during fetch). So user-swappable modular parts are roughly **2-4x cheaper per repair** than flagship OEM service, and the labour is ~zero (user does it in minutes with one screwdriver). That's a real, but not order-of-magnitude, cost drop, and some of it reflects a mid-range vs flagship part difference.

### 5. Framework Laptop: one chassis has accepted ~7 generations of mainboards (data/practitioner)
- Source: Framework marketplace filter taxonomy, https://frame.work/marketplace/mainboards ; Wikipedia "Framework Computer" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framework_Computer
- Laptop 13 mainboard families: 11th Gen Intel, 12th Gen, 13th Gen, Core Ultra Series 1, AMD Ryzen 7040, Ryzen AI 300, plus a Chromebook board, all for the same 2021-origin chassis. 2025 Laptop 16 GPU module backward-compatible with the 2024 model. Marketplace open to third-party vendors.
- iFixit 10/10; internals reachable "in under a minute with a single screwdriver."
- Funding: $9m seed (2021), $18m Series A (2022, Spark), $17m Series A-1 (2024). No public units/revenue.
- Criticisms: early AMD shipments delayed by electrical/firmware problems; 2024 Ars Technica on lagging firmware/BIOS updates (Framework admitted it had "fallen short"); Laptop 13 Pro and Laptop 16 AI 300 shipping delays (2025-26); Aug 2026 third-party data breach affecting all customers.
- Reading: the upgrade/repair hard part for Framework is **firmware and software support**, not mechanical modularity or repair knowledge. Standardised interfaces (USB-C expansion cards) are what make the third-party ecosystem possible.

### 6. Project Ara / modular smartphone failures: modularity has real physical penalties (history)
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Ara ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_smartphone
- Ara: Google's lead estimated a **<25% size, power and weight penalty** vs integrated components; FCC certifies single configurations, not modular ones (certification problem); prototype froze at Google I/O 2014; shelved Sept 2016 citing complexity, need for constant upgrades, lack of carrier support.
- Modu (2008-2011) went bankrupt; LG G5 and Moto Z modular add-ons discontinued; Shiftphone turnover < EUR1m/yr.
- Industry incentive: carriers and OEMs profit from trade-ins and replacement cycles.
- Reading: CONTRADICTS a naive "modularity/bespoke is solved" view. Deep modularity costs size, power, weight, reliability (connectors), and creates certification combinatorics. Shallow modularity (swappable battery/screen/port with screws, Fairphone-style) survives; deep "Lego phone" modularity failed.

### 7. Line replaceable units: the canonical repair-by-swap architecture (standard/history)
- Source: Wikipedia "Line-replaceable unit", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-replaceable_unit , citing MIL-PRF-49506 (2005), AFMAN 23-110, ARINC 404/600, MIL-STD-1553.
- LRU = sealed module removed/replaced at field level to restore readiness; the failed unit is repaired at a depot ("other lines"). Standardised trays, connectors, cooling, power, quick-release hardware, size/weight limits for hatches. On ISS: orbit-replaceable units.
- Key structural point: LRUs **separate return-to-service from repair**. The aircraft is flying again after a swap; diagnosis-of-the-module and repair happen offline. This collapses the founder's chain (notice-diagnose-understand-source-deliver-repair-inspect) at the point of use into notice -> fault-isolate to LRU -> swap from local stock -> functional test. The cost moves into **spares inventory (rotables pool) and depot repair**.
- No quantified savings in the article; DTIC F-16 LRU packaging trade study (cited) was not retrievable (403).

### 8. No-fault-found (NFF): the LRU model's main failure mode is diagnosis, costing DoD >$2bn/yr (data/study)
- Source: Wikipedia "No fault found", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_fault_found
- Units removed after BIT alarm or operator report, found serviceable at depot, returned; repeat cycles. NFF estimated to cost the US DoD **more than US$2 billion per year**.
- [background, not re-verified] Cranfield/aerospace literature commonly cites NFF/unjustified removal rates of roughly 30-50%+ for some avionics LRUs.
- Reading: SUPPORTS the idea that diagnosis is a real cost centre even in highly standardised modular systems, and that better diagnosis (where AI on BIT/telemetry data could help) has direct value. But note the waste comes from *over-swapping* because swapping is cheap and fault isolation is hard; modularity shifts cost to logistics rather than eliminating it.

### 9. GAO-13-651: open/modular systems promise repair and upgrade savings but DoD barely implements them (regulator)
- Source: GAO, "Defense Acquisitions: DOD Efforts to Adopt Open Systems for Its Unmanned Aircraft Systems Have Progressed Slowly", https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-13-651
- Open systems offer "significant repair, upgrade, and competition benefits" that "could save millions" as weapons age, but GAO gives no quantified figure; DoD had **no metrics** for open systems. Navy used open approach on 3 of 4 UAS; Army 0 initially; Air Force only 1 of 3 planned on fielded aircraft. Small Tactical UAS: 32 payloads from 24 manufacturers; integration in days/months instead of years. DoD FY2014 modification budget >$11bn (>10% of procurement).
- Workforce competency assessment not done as of Aug 2020; recommendation closed unimplemented. FY2015 and FY2017 NDAAs mandated MOSA.
- Reading: SUPPORTS C7 in a specific way: incentives (prime contractor lock-in of interfaces and data rights), not technical ignorance, keep systems closed. Even where the government is the customer and mandates modularity, adoption is slow. Expertise needed to write open-system specs was scarce (a knowledge gap of a different kind: systems architecture, not repair procedure).

### 10. Open Compute Project: hyperscalers redesigned hardware for serviceability, but hard public repair-cost numbers are scarce (practitioner)
- Sources: https://www.opencompute.org/about ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Compute_Project
- OCP claims Facebook Prineville was ~38% more energy efficient to build and ~24% cheaper to run than prior facilities (energy/opex, not repair-specific). 400+ members (2025). Open Vault: 30 drives in 2OU "designed for easy disk drive replacement."
- [background, not re-verified] Facebook's 2011 OCP launch materials described tool-less, "vanity-free" servers where components are swapped without tools and front-of-rack (cold aisle) service; hyperscalers run "fail in place" / batch-repair models where a dead node is drained by software and repaired later in bulk by low-skill technicians.
- Reading: in data centres, the decisive enabler is **software redundancy** (drain the node, fail in place), which turns repair from urgent bespoke work into scheduled, low-skill, batched swaps. That's the LRU logic again. I could not find a public before/after repair-cost number in this session.

### 11. Interchangeable parts: the historical origin of "repair by ordering a part" and its up-front cost (history)
- Source: Wikipedia "Interchangeable parts", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interchangeable_parts
- Before interchangeability, a failed firearm part meant a gunsmith's custom work or scrapping the gun; afterwards a replacement could be ordered and fitted with less skill. Gribeauval system 1765; Blanc c.1778; Whitworth thread standard 1841.
- For years US Armory interchangeable firearms **cost more to make** than hand-made ones; savings only evident around 1853, decades after the 1810s-20s start. Ford pursued cross-model parts commonality from 1913 but pulled back after losing share to Chevrolet's variety.
- Reading: standardisation is the original "knowledge removal" technology: it embeds knowledge in gauges and tolerances so the repairer needs less skill. It needed decades of capital investment and coordination, and it trades against product variety. Strong analog for C3: knowledge got commoditised before, through standards rather than AI, and the economics took decades to show up.

### 12. French repairability index (2021): standardised scoring nudges design but is self-reported (regulator)
- Source: Wikipedia "Repairability index", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repairability_index
- Since 2021 for smartphones, laptops, TVs, washing machines, lawnmowers. Five criteria: documentation, disassembly, spare-part availability, **spare-part price**, product-specific. Launch average ~5.4/10 (iPhone 12: 6.0, iPhone 11: 4.5). Self-reported with little oversight. Example effect: Samsung published a free Galaxy S21+ repair manual. No published evidence of changed repair rates.
- Reading: regulators decompose repairability into the same factors as the founder's chain (docs = understanding, disassembly = repair, parts availability/price = sourcing). Being self-reported is itself an inspection/verification gap (C6).

### 13. Integrated modular avionics (IMA): aviation moved *away* from one-box-per-function LRUs toward shared modules, adding certification complexity (practitioner/standard)
- Source: Wikipedia "Integrated modular avionics", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_modular_avionics
- IMA (A380, 787, Rafale) consolidates functions onto shared processing modules; claimed gains in reliability, maintainability, size, weight (no figures given); applications can migrate to spare modules. Rafale MDPU has up to 18 flight-line-replaceable modules. Downside: "much complexity is added"; certification under DO-178C/DO-254/DO-297/ARINC 653, and integrating pre-qualified supplier blocks is "the biggest challenge."
- Reading: modularity in safety-critical systems is gated by **certification**, not by knowledge. Every new module/configuration must be qualified, which is why bespoke parts can't just be "printed". SUPPORTS C6, CONTRADICTS C5.

## Synthesis

What modularity/standardisation actually does to the repair chain:
1. It **decouples return-to-service from repair**: swap at the point of use, fix the module later at a depot (LRU, hyperscale fail-in-place, Fairphone user swap). The asset-downtime cost, usually the largest cost for airliners, servers and industrial machines, falls sharply. The repair cost doesn't vanish: it moves to rotable inventory, logistics and depot labour.
2. It **embeds knowledge in the interface** (gauges, ARINC trays, USB-C cards, screws instead of glue), so the field repairer needs little skill. This is how the "knowledge gap" has historically been closed: through standards and design, not information.
3. It **moves the hard problem to fault isolation** (which module?) and to software/firmware (Framework) and serialisation (EU rule). NFF (>$2bn/yr at DoD) shows that even fully modular systems waste a lot of money on bad diagnosis.
4. It **costs something**: connector metals (17% of Fairphone ADP), <25% size/power/weight penalty (Ara), certification combinatorics (FCC, DO-297), decades of capital (interchangeable parts), product variety (Ford).
5. Adoption is blocked mainly by **incentives** (OEM/carrier replacement revenue, prime contractor data rights) rather than technical ignorance: GAO-13-651, Ara, Fairphone's 0.01% share.

On the founder's claims:
- C1: modularity reshapes the chain. With LRUs the point-of-use chain becomes notice -> isolate -> swap -> test, and the full chain runs offline at the depot. So the chain holds at the module level but isn't one linear sequence at the system level: it forks.
- C2: mixed. The regulator (EU) treats information access as one barrier alongside parts, tools, fasteners and pairing. Lock-in is via parts, serialisation and interfaces as much as knowledge.
- C3: contradicted-ish. The existing knowledge gap-closers are standards and mandated documentation; the remaining hard parts (firmware, certification, parts pairing, physical penalties) aren't knowledge problems.
- C4: mixed. Modular part-level repair is 2-4x cheaper at phone scale and slashes downtime in aviation and servers, but sourcing/delivery cost is set by inventory economics and law (5-day EU delivery), and NFF shows diagnosis still costs a lot. An order of magnitude across all four steps isn't evidenced.
- C5: contradicted. Certification (DO-254/297, FCC per-configuration) and connector/interface qualification mean a bespoke part isn't a drop-in without requalification.
- C6: supported. Certification governs modular aviation; self-reported repair scores show a verification gap.
- C7: mixed-to-supports. Incentive structures do keep things closed (GAO, Ara carriers), but aviation LRUs and hyperscale data centres are highly optimised for repair *where the owner bears downtime cost*. The industry is efficient where incentives align, so it's misaligned rather than uniformly "built to be inefficient".

## Real bottleneck (my read)
Interface standardisation plus control of the interface (who owns the spec, data rights, parts serialisation) and **fault isolation to the replaceable unit**. Plus certification of any module that wasn't the original. Knowledge of procedures is mostly not the scarce layer where modularity exists.

## Company shapes suggested
- Fault-isolation / NFF-reduction software for LRU-based fleets (avionics, rail, defence, wind, data centre): uses BIT logs + maintenance history to cut unjustified removals. Clear $ pool (>$2bn/yr DoD alone).
- "MOSA-as-a-service": tooling that generates and verifies open interface specs and conformance tests for defence/robotics programmes (GAO says the expertise and metrics are missing).
- Rotables/spares-pool optimisation for modular fleets (energy assets, robots, drones): predictive placement of LRUs to shrink inventory while keeping swap-time low.
- Design-for-repair modular platforms in robotics/energy (e.g., swappable actuator/battery/inverter modules with standard interfaces), copying the LRU and Framework playbook for new hardware categories where there's no incumbent lock-in yet.
- Conformance/certification-testing labs for third-party modules (the inspection layer for a modular aftermarket).
