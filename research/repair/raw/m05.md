# m05: Independent aviation MROs and the used serviceable material (USM) market: how teardown and USM lowered costs; what limits independents (OEM licenses, data, DER repairs)?

Research date: 2026-10-20.
Method note: the shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn was already used up when this agent started, so every web search attempt was refused. Everything below comes from direct WebFetch of primary URLs I already knew (SEC EDGAR 10-Ks, IATA pages, Wikipedia). Items marked [BACKGROUND, not verified this session] come from prior knowledge, are widely reported, and should be checked before anyone cites them.

## Findings (fetched, verified this session)

### F1. IATA: supply chain problems will cost airlines more than $11bn in 2025. $3.1bn of it is higher maintenance cost, and OEM aftermarket dominance is named as a cost driver
Source: IATA, Aviation Supply Chain Challenges, https://www.iata.org/en/programs/ops-infra/techops/aviation-supply-chain/
- Breakdown: $4.2bn in delayed fuel savings; $3.1bn in higher maintenance costs ("aging fleets are more expensive to keep in service"); $2.6bn in excess engine leasing, because engines spend longer in shops; $1.4bn in extra spares inventory, including AOG desk exchanges and parts "technical robberies" from other aircraft.
- Deliveries were 1,254 in 2024, about 30% below the pre-COVID peak. The backlog is a record 17,000 jets.
- IATA's statements on the aftermarket:
  - "OEM dominance in the MRO aftermarket can limit airline choices and increase costs."
  - OEMs earn a growing share of their profit from the aftermarket.
  - Many component repairs can only be done by OEMs or their licensees, and OEMs have little interest in developing new repairs.
  - Lessors often require OEM parts and repairs at lease return.
  - IATA wants Part 145 MROs to have access beyond the OEM-licensed networks.
- PMA: 74% of airlines in an IATA survey use PMA parts, mainly consumables and cabin parts. The main barriers are lease contract restrictions and OEM agreements.
- The renewed IATA-CFM agreement:
  - protects the warranty when an airline uses approved non-OEM parts or repairs;
  - gives independent MROs fair access to some repair manuals;
  - gives commercial recognition to PMA parts.
- A January 2026 IATA guidance letter covers commercial recognition of approved alternative solutions at lease end.
- IATA is working to widen access to USM, PMA and Part 21/DER repairs in order to shorten lead times and repair turnaround.
- Reference frameworks IATA cites: Boeing PSAA, Airbus SSC, IATA-Rolls-Royce best practices, and EASA/FAA rules on access to Instructions for Continued Airworthiness.
- Reading: the binding constraints are contractual and commercial (licenses, lease-return clauses, warranty threats, manual access). They are not a lack of technical knowledge. Where knowledge does matter, it is legally gated as IP or ICA access, not merely missing.

### F2. AerSale 10-K for FY2024 shows the USM/teardown business model and its limits
Source: AerSale Corp 10-K for FY2024, filed 2025-03-11, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1754170/000155837025002624/asle-20241231x10k.htm
- AerSale buys retired aircraft and engines ("Flight Equipment") from airlines and lessors as feedstock. It disassembles them "to the piece-part level for re-use as USM", which it describes as "a safe and widely-accepted low-cost alternative to purchasing new OEM replacement parts."
- Revenue in FY2024 was $345.1m (+3.2%), and gross profit was $103.9m. Gross margins by line:

  | Line | FY2023 | FY2024 |
  |---|---|---|
  | Aircraft | 28.1% | 34.8% |
  | Engines | 33.4% | 39.1% |
  | TechOps (MRO services) | 20.8% | 16.6% |

- Feedstock purchases fell from $131.9m in 2023 to $61.7m in 2024. Feedstock availability is a stated risk to revenue.
- Inventory: $224.8m current and $131.0m non-current, so the business is capital- and inventory-heavy.
- OEM IP risks, quoted from the risk factors:
  - OEM repair manuals contain language "asserting broad claims of proprietary rights to the contents of the manuals".
  - OEM contracts "contain restrictions on our use of the intellectual property and tooling".
  - The loss of an OEM contract "and the related license rights" could hurt results.
- AerSale uses PMA, STC and DER/DAR designees for its Engineered Solutions, such as AerAware EFVS.
- Reading: selling the parts out of teardowns earns 35-39% gross margin, while the labour-only MRO service earns 17%. Scarce feedstock and inventory capture the value; labour and know-how do not. The OEM legal claim on manuals is the explicit constraint on independents.

### F3. FTAI Aviation's 10-K for FY2025 shows aftermarket module and engine sales on old engines growing very fast
Source: FTAI Aviation 10-K for FY2025, filed 2026-02-27, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1590364/000162828026012940/ftai-20251231.htm
- Aerospace Products revenue:

  | Year | Revenue |
  |---|---|
  | 2023 | $455.0m |
  | 2024 | $1,079.8m |
  | 2025 | $1,600.5m |

- 2025 growth was driven by a $499.7m increase in CFM56-5B, CFM56-7B and V2500 engine and module sales. MRE (maintenance repair exchange) contract revenue rose $335.8m. Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA was $1,190.9m in 2025, up from $862.1m.
- FTAI holds 25% of the Advanced Engine Repair JV, which develops repairs and PMA. It sells "other serviceable used modules and parts" through an exclusive partnership.
- On 2025-12-30 it announced FTAI Power, which converts CFM56 engines into power turbines. This is a cross-domain reuse of aviation hardware for energy and data-centre power, relevant to an energy/machines founder.
- The filing names competitors that "include engine and aircraft parts manufacturers".
- [BACKGROUND, not verified this session] FTAI marketing and earnings calls pitch the "module factory" as a way to swap modules and avoid full OEM-network shop visits at a large discount (tens of percent). The filing text I read gave no percentage.
- Reading: the fastest-growing independent model is industrial logistics, not AI knowledge. It works by owning feedstock (engines), standardising module swaps, and adding PMA/DER repairs. Its scale depends on a huge installed base of mature engines (CFM56/V2500) whose OEM grip is weaker late in the product life.

### F4. HEICO's 10-K for FY2025 shows PMA/DER as a large, high-margin, regulated niche
Source: HEICO 10-K for FY2025, period ending 2025-10-31, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/46619/000004661925000082/hei-20251031.htm
- HEICO has about 20,000 FAA PMA parts and adds about 400-550 new PMAs a year, including through acquisitions.
- Flight Support Group (FSG) net sales were a record $3,117.3m (+18%), 70% of HEICO's $4,485m total. FSG operating margin was 24.1%, up from 22.5%.
- Its parts sell "at lower prices than those manufactured by OEMs". The filing gives no percentage.
- Repairs are "approved by an FAA-qualified designated engineering representative" (DER). HEICO cites "a successful track record of receiving PMAs and DER repair approvals".
- HEICO calls the PMA process "a significant barrier to entry in this market niche". FAA processing time depends on "the volume of PMAs being filed" and "the resources available to the FAA". An established track record brings "faster turnaround time".
- HEICO has "no material patents for the proprietary techniques" and relies on trade secrets. It lists as a risk "development by our competitors of patents or methodologies that preclude us from the design".
- Reading:
  - Reverse-engineering and redesigning a part, which is the knowledge step, is done routinely. The moat is regulatory approval throughput and track record with the FAA.
  - Having 20,000 parts after decades suggests the binding step is the certification of each part, not making it.
  - This is direct evidence against C5 ("bespoke part is solved") and for C6 (inspection and certification are the bottleneck).

### F5. Engine economics: OEMs sell engines at deep discounts and recoup on aftermarket parts. Teardowns depress LLP and part prices
Source: Wikipedia, Aircraft maintenance, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_maintenance (secondary; it cites Aviation Week and Flight Global)
- Engine makers sell engines at reported discounts of up to 90% to secure aftermarket revenue, a razor-and-blades model.
- On life-limited parts (LLPs): LLP prices rise to recover the original discount, and they make up a large share of shop visit cost (the page says about 80%, which looks high). Prices ease as teardowns increase the supply of used parts.
- Airlines' 2017 MRO spend of $70bn split: engines 31%, components 27%, line maintenance 24%, modifications 10%, airframe 8%.
- An engine's core value lies in its data plate and non-life-limited parts.
- Reading: USM is a price-discipline mechanism. Teardown supply caps what OEMs can charge for parts. When retirements fall, as in 2022-25 when airlines kept old aircraft flying, USM supply tightens and prices rise. That explains AerSale's falling feedstock purchases and FTAI's pricing power.

### F6. PMA regulatory history: alternative parts are a 75-year-old, regulated institution, not an AI novelty
Source: Wikipedia, Parts Manufacturer Approval, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval
- Approval of replacement parts dates to CAR 3.31 in 1947 and became FAR 21.303 in 1965.
- Airlines sought alternative parts sources in the 1980s. FAA enforcement in the 1990s "brought an explosion of PMA parts".
- Ways to qualify: identicality, licence (an assist letter from the OEM), prior STC, or test and computation.
- The 2009 rule (effective 2011) aligned PMA quality requirements with production certificates. Bilateral Aviation Safety Agreements (BASAs) make FAA PMA parts importable.
- Reading:
  - The "knowledge gap" was bridged decades ago by test-and-computation reverse engineering.
  - What persists is the per-part burden of showing compliance and the commercial barriers (warranty, lease, contract), not the ability to understand the part.

### F7. DERs: delegated private engineers approve repair data, but carry personal liability
Source: Wikipedia, Designated Engineering Representative, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Designated_Engineering_Representative (cites 14 CFR 183.29 and FAA Orders 8100.8 and 8110.37)
- Company DERs approve their employer's data. Consultant DERs approve data from anyone.
- A DER acts for the FAA but is not a federal employee and has no federal indemnification. "The FAA cannot shelter or protect DERs from the consequences of their findings."
- Reading:
  - DER repairs are the main legal route for independents to make repairs beyond the OEM manual.
  - The scarce input is a credentialed human who signs and takes liability. AI can draft the substantiation, but the signature (liability plus regulatory authority) is the constraint.
  - This is strong support for C6 and against C3.

### F8. IATA maintenance cost benchmarking covers about 25% of the world fleet. IATA sells a USM pricing tool (MRO SmartHub)
Sources: IATA MCTG, https://www.iata.org/en/programs/ops-infra/techops/mctg/; IATA MRO SmartHub, https://www.iata.org/en/services/data/mro-insights/mro-smarthub/
- More than 50 airlines contribute maintenance cost data, covering about a quarter of the world fleet and MRO spend.
- SmartHub provides "price references, parts availability, and supplier connections" for surplus parts.
- Reading:
  - Price and availability transparency for USM is already a product. Knowledge of where parts are, and at what price, is partly commoditised.
  - The gains left come from getting serviceable material and approval. Finding parts is no longer the main gain.

## Findings [BACKGROUND, not verified this session; check before citing]

- B1. The AOG Technics scandal (UK, 2023). A London broker supplied CFM56 parts with forged EASA Form 1 / FAA 8130-3 release documents.
  - Thousands of parts were affected, and dozens of engines at several airlines (including TAP, United, American and Southwest) were pulled for inspection.
  - The Serious Fraud Office prosecuted the director, José Zamora Yrala.
  - This shows the USM market's key weakness: documented traceability ("back-to-birth" records). The value of a used part depends almost entirely on its paperwork.
  - A lower-friction, AI-enabled parts market raises the importance of authentication and provenance, not just knowledge (supports C6; complicates C4).
  - The SFO case page returned 503 when fetched: https://www.sfo.gov.uk/cases/aog-technics-ltd/
- B2. Trade press and practitioners (Aviation Week, ICF, Oliver Wyman) commonly say USM sells at a substantial discount to OEM list price, often quoted at 30-50%+, and PMA at roughly 30-40% below OEM.
  - Material is typically the majority (often 60-70%+) of an engine shop visit's cost. Labour is a minority.
  - So savings depend mainly on the price and availability of material and approvals, not on diagnostic labour.
- B3. Lufthansa Technik bought about 20% of HEICO's Flight Support Group in 1997-98. This is the classic case of an airline MRO funding PMA to discipline OEM pricing.
  - Delta TechOps and other airline MROs have since used PMA/DER repairs. OEMs answered with warranty conditions, "power-by-the-hour" bundles (Rolls-Royce TotalCare, GE TrueChoice, Pratt EngineWise) and licensed networks that lock out alternative parts.
- B4. Next-generation engines (LEAP, GTF) are almost entirely under OEM or OEM-network service. Little USM exists for them (few retirements), and independent access to manuals and repairs is limited.
  - The GTF powder-metal recall (2023-) shows that knowledge of a fleet-wide defect sat with the OEM. The bottleneck was shop capacity and parts, not diagnosis.
  - Durability problems on new engines are pushing more airlines towards old engines (CFM56/V2500), which helps FTAI and AerSale.
- B5. Antitrust and legal angle: there have been complaints and investigations about OEM aftermarket practices, including the IATA-led push and the 2019-2021 debate over Boeing/Airbus moving into MRO.
  - The EU Commission's earlier enforcement on aerospace aftermarket licensing is cited in industry discussion. I could not verify specific cases this session.

## Synthesis: where the cost and time actually go
1. Material (new OEM parts or LLPs) dominates engine and component repair cost. Labour and diagnosis are minor. USM and PMA cut cost by attacking material price, and teardown supply is the price-discipline mechanism (F2, F5, B2).
2. Time goes to waiting for shop slots, parts and certified repairs: engines sit in shops longer, $2.6bn goes on extra engine leases, and $1.4bn on extra spares (F1). Diagnosis is rarely the long pole for airlines.
3. Independents are limited by:
   - (a) OEM IP claims on manuals and tooling, and licence contracts (F2);
   - (b) warranty and lease-return clauses that require OEM parts (F1);
   - (c) the per-part FAA approval burden and FAA reviewer capacity for PMA (F4);
   - (d) the need for credentialed, personally liable DERs to approve repairs (F7);
   - (e) feedstock: retirements and access to the engines to tear down (F2);
   - (f) document provenance and the risk of fraud (B1).
4. Knowledge in the "understanding how to fix it" sense is mostly not the scarce layer. Reverse engineering for PMA has been routine since the 1990s, at about 20,000 parts at HEICO alone. Where knowledge is scarce, the cause is legal gating (ICA access, licensed repairs), which AI does not remove.

## Stances on the hypotheses
- C1 (universal chain): mixed. Aviation adds steps the chain omits: feedstock acquisition and teardown, records and traceability, certification and approval of data (PMA/DER), and lease-return compliance. Sourcing is often "harvest from a retired asset", not order from a supplier.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed, leaning to supports with a twist. OEMs do capture value, but through legal and contractual control (IP claims, licences, warranty, lease clauses, ICA access) and through capital (inventory, feedstock), not because independents can't understand the hardware.
- C3 (AI removes the gap): contradicts. The gap that binds is legal and regulatory: approval, liability, licence and records. HEICO's moat is FAA approval track record, and DERs carry personal liability.
- C4 (10x cost drop in four steps): contradicts as stated. The big savings (USM, PMA) come from material price and availability, which are physical and capital-bound. Diagnosis and understanding are small cost shares, and sourcing is limited by supply.
- C5 (AI parts factory solves bespoke parts): contradicts. Making a part is not the binding step. A part is installable only with PMA/DER approval and traceable documentation. HEICO has spent decades building 20,000 approvals and adds about 500 a year, and FAA capacity sets the pace.
- C6 (inspection and certification essential and must evolve): supports. Approval throughput (PMA/DER), records and provenance (AOG Technics) and Part 145 releases are the scarce layer.
- C7 (structurally inefficient, must be rebuilt): mixed. The inefficiency is partly designed in (razor-blade engine pricing, lease clauses), and IATA itself calls OEM dominance a cost driver. But USM, PMA, DER and independent MROs are a mature, functioning counter-system with large, profitable incumbents (HEICO FSG $3.1bn at 24% margin; FTAI $1.6bn and growing fast). The trend is incremental opening (IATA-CFM deal, lease-end guidance), not a ground-up rebuild.

## Company shapes suggested
- Certification-throughput tooling: AI that drafts PMA/DER substantiation packages (test plans, analysis, conformity data) for DERs and the FAA ACO to approve faster. It sells to PMA houses and airline engineering departments, and attacks the real bottleneck.
- Provenance and records infrastructure for USM: automated back-to-birth records review, forgery detection on 8130-3 and Form 1 certificates, and a digital chain of custody. This is the AOG Technics lesson, and it sits close to security tech.
- Feedstock-and-teardown arbitrage with software-optimised part-out (which engine to buy, which parts to harvest, when to sell). This is capital-heavy. FTAI and AerSale show the economics.
- Lease-return and records compliance automation that lowers lessor resistance to PMA/DER at redelivery, in line with IATA's January 2026 lease-end guidance.
- Cross-domain reuse: aero-derivative engines repurposed for power (FTAI Power), and MRO for power turbines as data-centre demand grows. This fits an energy founder.

## Surprises
- FTAI's aerospace products revenue went from $455m in 2023 to $1.6bn in 2025, roughly 3.5x, on 25-year-old engine families. The independent opportunity is largest late in a platform's life, not at its frontier.
- At AerSale, labour-based MRO service has the lowest margin (16.6%). Owning parts earns 35-39%. Knowledge and labour are not where the money is.
- HEICO has no material patents. Its moat is regulatory track record and FAA reviewer capacity.
- IATA names lease-return clauses, not knowledge, as a key barrier to PMA use.
- FTAI is turning CFM56 jet engines into power turbines, which links aviation repair to energy.
