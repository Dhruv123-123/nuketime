## A. Hard data (pulled and computed this session)

### A1. Statistics Canada, Annual Capital and Repair Expenditures Survey, Table 34-10-0035-01

Non-residential tangible assets, C$ millions, current dollars. Canada is one of the few countries that measures business repair spending next to capex, which makes it the best public proxy for the size of the "maintenance economy".

(2025 preliminary / 2026 intentions are published for capex only: C$386.8B and C$401.2B. Repair is not yet published for those years.)

Sector cuts, 2024 (C$M):
- Manufacturing: repair M&E 6,343 vs capex M&E 21,994 (29%). In 2006: 9,214 vs 14,419 (64%). **Nominal manufacturing machinery repair spending FELL about 31% from 2006 to 2024** even as machinery capex rose 53%. In real terms the drop is larger (Canadian CPI rose roughly 45-50% over the period). Possible reasons: more reliable equipment, a shift to outsourced or bundled service contracts booked as capex or services, fewer plants. Hard data; the explanation is my inference.
- Mining, oil and gas: repair M&E 9,148 vs capex M&E 12,358 (74%). Heavy mobile equipment costs nearly as much to keep running as to buy.
- Transportation and warehousing: repair 12,792 total (M&E 5,673) vs capex 51,767.
- Utilities: repair 7,745 vs capex 42,429 (18%).

Rough scale: about C$95B of non-residential repair against roughly C$3.0T nominal GDP in 2024 is about 3% of GDP. The GDP denominator is from memory, not fetched. The figure excludes household repair, residential repair, and probably much of the in-house maintenance labour that is not separately reported.

Takeaway: business repair is about one-quarter the size of business capex, and stable over 20 years (23% to 28%). It is a big, steady flow, not a collapsing or exploding one. In equipment-heavy sectors (mining) repair approaches the cost of new equipment.

### A2. US repair prices are rising faster than general prices (a Baumol-type signal)
Source: BLS CPI via FRED, series CUSR0000SETD (Motor vehicle maintenance and repair) and CPIAUCSL. https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CUSR0000SETD
- Jan 2019 to Sep 2026: motor-vehicle maintenance and repair CPI **+61.1%** vs headline CPI **+33.2%**.
- Jan 2000 to Sep 2026: repair **+168%** vs headline **+99%**.
- Interpretation: repair is a labour-intensive service with weak measured productivity growth, so its relative price keeps rising (Baumol cost disease). This is consistent with the claim that repair is "not built for smart", but the mechanism is labour productivity and labour scarcity (technicians), plus parts-price and complexity effects (sensors, ADAS calibration). The data does not show knowledge monopoly specifically.
- Baumol mechanism background: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baumol%27s_cost_disease. Nordhaus (2008), using US data for 1948-2001, found that lower productivity growth closely tracks higher relative price growth.

### A3. Repair labour force (US)
Source: BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, Installation, Maintenance and Repair. https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/home.htm
- About **569,000 openings per year** projected for 2025-35, mostly replacement of workers who leave. Growth is projected faster than average.
- Median wage **$59,620** (May 2025) vs $50,980 for all occupations. The higher wage signals scarcity of skilled repair labour.
- From memory, not re-verified: the group is about 6 million workers, roughly 4% of US employment.

### A4. Cost of NOT maintaining: downtime
Source: Siemens / Senseye, "The True Cost of Downtime 2024". https://assets.new.siemens.com/siemens/assets/api/uuid:1b43afb5-2d07-47f7-9eb7-893fe7d0bc59/TCOD-2024_original.pdf (vendor study, so promotional bias).
- The Fortune Global 500 lose about **$1.4 trillion a year** to unplanned downtime, **11% of revenues**. That is down 6% since 2022.
- An hour of downtime costs a large automotive plant **$2.3M**, double the 2019 figure. Heavy industry per-hour cost is up 319% since 2019, against 19% US inflation. FMCG costs about $36k per hour.
- An average large plant loses **27 hours a month** to unplanned downtime, down from 39 in 2019. So hours are falling (condition monitoring and IoT help), but the cost per hour is rising.
- Siemens claims predictive maintenance could save $233B through a 40% reduction in maintenance costs.
- Takeaway: much of the economic value of repair sits in downtime and time-to-restore, not in the repair bill itself. That matters for C4. The cost of diagnosis is small next to the cost of the asset sitting idle.

### A5. Deloitte, "Using predictive technologies for asset maintenance"
Source: https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/manufacturing-industrial-products/industry-4-0/using-predictive-technologies-for-asset-maintenance.html (consultant figures, not independently verified).
- Poor maintenance strategies can cut plant productive capacity by **5-20%**.
- Unplanned downtime costs industrial manufacturers about **$50B a year** (WSJ/Deloitte).
- Predictive maintenance gains: uptime **+10-20%**, maintenance cost **-5-10%**, planning time **-20-50%**.
- Trenitalia spends about $1.3B a year on maintenance and expected an 8-10% reduction, about $100M a year.
- Takeaway: even the boosters of digital maintenance claim single-digit to low-double-digit percentage cost cuts, not an order of magnitude. This contradicts the 10x in C4, at least as of the pre-LLM data era. The bigger lever is uptime, not cost.

### A6. ASCE 2025 Infrastructure Report Card
Source: https://infrastructurereportcard.org/
- The US overall grade is **C**, the best in decades. Stormwater and transit get D, ports B. The 10-year investment gap is **$3.6T**. The IIJA funded more than 60,000 projects.
- This is the canonical "deferred maintenance" datapoint used by the Maintainers literature. Under-maintenance here is a funding and political-incentive problem (ribbon-cutting beats upkeep), not a knowledge problem.

## B. Arguments of the maintenance-studies literature

### B1. Vinsel & Russell, "Hail the maintainers" (Aeon, 2016) and *The Innovation Delusion* (2020)
Sources: https://aeon.co/essays/innovation-is-overvalued-maintenance-often-matters-more ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Innovation_Delusion ; https://themaintainers.org/
- **Thesis:** "Innovation-speak" diverts attention and money from maintenance, repair and care. What happens after technologies appear (maintenance, repair, infrastructure) shapes daily life more than most innovations do.
- **Labour:** innovators and inventors are "somewhere around one per cent" of the workforce. Most technical labour is maintenance, and it is undervalued, often along gender and race lines.
- They follow Edgerton: "technology is not innovation"; most technology in use is old.
- **Prescriptions:** "fix it first" for infrastructure, preventive over deferred maintenance, support for right to repair and repair cafes. They cite the US DoD's large maintenance budget share as a positive example, and Strong Towns (Marohn) on municipal maintenance liabilities.
- **Relevance to the founder:** Vinsel & Russell are sceptical of "rebuild the industry with new tech" framings. Their diagnosis is that the inefficiency is cultural, institutional and financial: maintenance is under-funded, low-status and invisible in accounting. A YC pitch built on "AI makes repair 10x cheaper" is exactly the innovation-speak they critique, unless it is framed as empowering maintainers (technicians, mechanics, utilities crews).
- Opinion, not data. Their numbers are mostly about discourse (for example, "more than 250 books with innovation in the title in 3 months of 2012").

### B2. Jo Guldi

From background knowledge, unverified: *Roads to Power: Britain Invents the Infrastructure State* (Harvard UP, 2012) argues that the 18th-19th century British state built road infrastructure centrally, which later devolved and fragmented. Infrastructure is political, and who pays for upkeep shapes access. *The Long Land War* (Yale, 2022) is on land reform globally. Her relevance here is that maintenance regimes are institutional arrangements of financing and governance, not technical puzzles. That supports C7 in the sense "built by institutions, not for efficiency", but not the knowledge-monopoly framing.

### B3. Other strands (unverified background, leads to check)
- **David Edgerton, *The Shock of the Old* (2006):** history of technology-in-use. Maintenance and repair are a huge, persistent share of technical activity. Poor-world "creole technologies" show that repair knowledge diffuses widely when OEM control is absent, for example informal car and phone repair in Ghana and India. This cuts against C2 as a universal claim: where OEMs cannot enforce lock-in, independent repair knowledge flourishes.
- **Steven J. Jackson, "Rethinking Repair" (2014), "broken world thinking":** repair is a site of innovation and tacit knowledge. Tacit, embodied skill is not the same as information, which is a caution for C3.
- **Graham & Thrift, "Out of Order" (2007):** maintenance and repair are a vast, largely invisible economy.
- **National accounts:** under SNA 2008 / BEA convention, ordinary maintenance and repair is intermediate consumption, not investment, so it is netted out of GDP rather than counted as capital formation. Major improvements that extend asset life are capitalised. This is why repair is "invisible" in GDP statistics, and why Canada's survey is unusual.
- **Depreciation:** US consumption of fixed capital is about 16-17% of GDP (BEA NIPA Table 1.7.5; from memory, FRED fetch blocked). It is the economy's annual bill for wearing out capital. Maintenance is the main lever to slow it. Conceptually the maintenance-depreciation trade-off is set by owners' economics. There is a literature on endogenous depreciation (for example McGrattan & Schmitz 1999, "Maintenance and Repair: Too Big to Ignore", Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review). From memory, that paper found that Canadian maintenance and repair spending was about 6% of GDP, and roughly half as large as investment in new equipment and structures. **This is the key citation for "repair as a share of GDP"; verify before using.** It argues that macro models ignoring maintenance misstate investment and growth dynamics.
- **Software:** maintenance is commonly cited as 60-90% of lifecycle cost. This analog supports the idea that "maintenance dominates".

## C. Synthesis against hypotheses

- **C1 (universal chain):** Silent to mildly mixed. The economic literature does not dispute a generic chain, but it shows that value concentrates in downtime and time-to-restore and in preventive regimes that bypass "notice" (scheduled and condition-based maintenance). The chain is therefore less universal: much maintenance is planned replacement with no diagnosis step.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly):** Mixed. The repair price premium (+61% vs +33% CPI since 2019) and the technician scarcity fit "specialists capture rents". But the maintenance-studies literature attributes under-maintenance mainly to funding, incentives, status and accounting invisibility, not to knowledge scarcity. The Edgerton and Global South evidence shows repair knowledge diffuses where lock-in is not enforced.
- **C3 (AI erases the gap):** Contradicts / silent. Tacit and embodied repair skill (Jackson) and the binding labour constraint (569k openings a year) are not removed by information access.
- **C4 (10x cost cut):** Contradicts. Even predictive-maintenance boosters claim maintenance cost cuts of 5-10% (Deloitte) to 40% (Siemens' own claim). The bulk of repair cost is labour, parts and downtime, and the diagnosis and understanding share is not shown to be large.
- **C5 (AI parts factory):** Silent.
- **C6 (inspection persists and must evolve):** Silent to mildly supports. ASCE and DoD maintenance regimes rest on inspection-driven programmes.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient, must be rebuilt):** Mixed. Maintenance scholars agree the system under-invests in upkeep for structural reasons (innovation bias, capital budgets favour new builds, maintenance hidden as an expense). But they argue for re-valuing maintainers and funding upkeep, not for a tech-led rebuild. Canadian data shows repair intensity has been stable for 20 years, and falling in manufacturing, which is not a system in crisis.

## D. Where money and time go
Repair is about 25% of business capex in Canada (about 3% of GDP for non-residential business repair alone; possibly about 6% of GDP if the McGrattan-Schmitz estimate is right). Within that, labour dominates and its price is rising fastest (Baumol). The largest economic cost is downtime (11% of Global 500 revenue per Siemens), which is driven by time-to-restore: waiting for parts, technicians and approvals. Diagnosis is a small part of that cost.

## E. Company-shape implications (my reasoning)
- Tools that raise technician productivity, attacking the Baumol wedge: AI copilots for field techs that reduce time per job and let junior techs do senior work. They address the labour scarcity, not a knowledge monopoly.
- Downtime-minimisation businesses in high cost-per-hour sectors (auto plants, heavy industry, mining, where repair is 74% of capex): parts-availability guarantees, predictive spares positioning, condition monitoring.
- Maintenance finance and accounting products that make upkeep visible: asset-condition registries for municipalities and utilities. This addresses the Vinsel/Russell/ASCE failure mode, which is about money and governance.
- Mining and heavy mobile equipment as a beachhead, because repair spend nearly equals capex there.
