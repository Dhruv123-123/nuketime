## Findings

### A. How big is diagnostic error?
1. **[VERIFIED] NASEM, *Improving Diagnosis in Health Care* (2015).** "It is likely that most people will experience at least one diagnostic error in their lifetime." The report defines diagnostic error as an inaccurate or delayed diagnosis, and it calls the diagnostic process "a complex, collaborative activity" of information gathering and clinical reasoning. https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/21794
   - Relevance to C1: medicine's own canonical model is a loop, not a straight line. It runs: engage, gather information, integrate and interpret, working diagnosis, treatment, outcomes. The working diagnosis is revised as the loop repeats.
2. **[RECALLED] Newman-Toker et al., BMJ Quality & Safety 2024;33:109 (online July 2023).**
   - About 795,000 Americans a year die or are permanently disabled by diagnostic error: about 371k deaths and 424k disabilities.
   - The mean error rate across 15 diseases is about 11%. It ranges from 1.5% for heart attack to 62% for spinal abscess. Stroke is missed about 17.5% of the time.
   - Fifteen diseases cause about half of the serious harms. Vascular events, infections and cancers (the "Big Three") cause about 75%.
   - https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/33/2/109 (returned 403 to fetch)
3. **[RECALLED] Singh, Meyer & Thomas, BMJ Qual Saf 2014.** About 5% of US adult outpatients (about 12 million a year) have a diagnostic error, and about half of those errors could cause harm. A WebFetch model gave the same 5% / 12M figure from general knowledge, but the PMC ID I tried was the wrong article.
4. **[RECALLED] Saber Tehrani et al., BMJ Qual Saf 2013.** Across 25 years of US malpractice payouts, diagnostic errors were the largest category of paid claims (about 29%) and had the highest total payouts. Diagnosis is where the liability sits.

### B. What share of cost goes to diagnosis?
5. **[RECALLED, weakly sourced]** An often-repeated claim says lab tests are about 2–3% of US health spending but inform about 70% of medical decisions. The 70% figure has been criticised as poorly sourced.
   - The useful point: the act of diagnosing (tests, imaging, cognitive work) is a small share of spend, but it decides where the large downstream spend goes.
   - US imaging is commonly estimated at about $100B a year.
   - I could not verify a clean "diagnosis share of total spend" number. This is an honest gap; NASEM also notes there is no good estimate of the cost of diagnostic error.
6. **Implication for C4.** If diagnosis is about 5–10% of the cost of an episode, then even a 10x cut in diagnosis cost saves only that 5–10% directly. The large saving comes from a *correct* diagnosis avoiding wrong treatment, which in machines means wrong parts swapped and repeat visits. The value of AI is error reduction, not cheaper diagnostic labour.

### C. What AI diagnostic evidence actually shows
7. **[VERIFIED] Goh et al., JAMA Network Open 2024 (randomised trial, 50 physicians, GPT-4).**
   - Physicians with GPT-4 scored a median 76% on diagnostic reasoning; physicians with conventional resources scored 74%. The difference was +2 pp (95% CI −4 to 8, P=.60), so not significant.
   - Time per case fell by an adjusted 82 s, also not significant.
   - **GPT-4 alone scored 92%, 16 pp above the control group (P=.03).**
   - Lesson: the knowledge is in the model, but giving it to a human expert does not move the human's results. Workflow and trust are the bottleneck, not knowledge.
   - https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2825395
8. **[VERIFIED] Microsoft AI, "Sequential Diagnosis with Language Models" (MAI-DxO), arXiv 2506.22405, June 2025.**
   - The benchmark used NEJM clinicopathological cases, where the model must choose step by step which tests to order.
   - MAI-DxO with o3 reached about 80% accuracy (85.5% at its maximum-accuracy setting), against about 20% for generalist physicians.
   - It cut diagnostic test costs by about 20% compared with physicians and about 70% compared with off-the-shelf o3.
   - Caveats: the cases are rare and hard, the physicians worked without colleagues or references, and the paper is a preprint from a vendor.
   - Lesson: AI can lower the *cost of the diagnostic search* (fewer tests), not only raise accuracy. This is the closest direct analog to C4's "diagnose + understand" steps, and it shows about a 20% cut, not 10x.
   - https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22405
9. **[VERIFIED] Agarwal, Moehring, Rajpurkar & Salz, NBER WP 31422 (2023), radiologists with AI.**
   - AI predictions did not improve radiologists' performance on average. Radiologists underweighted the AI and treated its signal as independent of their own.
   - Context that only humans had (patient history) did help.
   - The best design is to route each case to AI alone or to a human alone, not to AI-assisted humans.
   - https://www.nber.org/papers/w31422
10. **[VERIFIED] Wong et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2021, external validation of the Epic Sepsis Model.** The study covered 38,455 hospitalisations.
    - AUC was 0.63, against much higher figures reported by the vendor.
    - Sensitivity was 33% and PPV 12%. The model **missed 67% of sepsis cases**, while alerting on 18% of all hospitalisations.
    - This is a widely deployed, OEM-style proprietary model that underperformed badly when tested independently.
    - Lessons: AI diagnosis deployed at scale without independent validation creates alert fatigue and false confidence, and independent inspection and certification is essential (supports C6).
    - https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2781307
11. **[RECALLED] MASAI randomised trial, Sweden (Lång et al., Lancet Oncology 2023; Lancet Digital Health 2025).** About 100k women took part.
    - AI-supported screening detected about 29% more cancers, with no rise in false positives.
    - Radiologist screen-reading workload fell **44%**.
    - Later interval-cancer follow-up reported non-inferiority (check the 2026 publication).
    - This is the strongest RCT evidence that AI improves real-world diagnostic throughput. The gain is close to 2x on labour, not 10x, and it was achieved by triage (AI decides which cases need one reader versus two).
12. **[RECALLED] PRAIM study, Nature Medicine Jan 2025 (Germany, about 463k women, real-world).** AI-supported double reading raised cancer detection about 17.6% without raising recall rates.
13. **[RECALLED] Google Health diabetic retinopathy deployment in Thailand (Beede et al., CHI 2020).**
    - The model was highly accurate in the lab. In clinics it rejected many images (poor lighting, low quality) and depended on internet uploads, which frustrated nurses and patients.
    - Lesson: the "notice / sensor capture" layer and workflow integration, not knowledge, broke the deployment.
14. **[RECALLED] IBM Watson Health.** After roughly $4–5B in acquisitions it was sold off in 2022 (to Francisco Partners, about $1B). Watson for Oncology was criticised for unsafe and incorrect recommendations.
    - This is the canonical case of "AI fills the knowledge gap" failing in practice: the data was messy, there was no workflow fit, and local practice varied.
15. **[RECALLED] FDA list of AI-enabled devices.** About 1,000+ authorised by 2025 (about 1,250 by mid-2025), about three-quarters in radiology. Very few have dedicated reimbursement codes, and studies of claims show only a handful billed at scale.
    - Regulatory clearance is not adoption. Payment rules and liability decide uptake.
16. **[RECALLED] Radiology labour market.** In 2016 Hinton said to stop training radiologists. By 2025, US radiology residency slots and radiologist employment and pay were at record highs, with backlogs growing.
    - AI cut work per study, but demand for imaging grew (a Jevons effect). Humans stayed accountable for sign-off.
17. **[RECALLED] Google AMIE (Nature 2025) and Med-PaLM 2.** In simulated text consultations, AMIE matched or beat primary-care doctors on diagnostic accuracy, as rated by specialists.
    - This is benchmark and simulation evidence, not outcome evidence.

### D. Cross-check from machines (a related analog, not medicine)
18. **[RECALLED]** In aviation, a large share of removed avionics line-replaceable units (LRUs) come back "No Fault Found": figures of 30–50%+ are common in industry literature.
    - This is the machine counterpart of diagnostic error. Wrong diagnosis drives unnecessary parts sourcing and logistics, so diagnostic accuracy controls the cost of the "source parts / deliver parts" steps.

## Synthesis and lessons for machine diagnosis
- **Diagnostic error is large and persistent despite huge open medical knowledge** (textbooks, UpToDate, guidelines). Medicine has no single OEM holding the knowledge, yet error rates are about 5–15%. Error comes from:
  - noisy, missing or uncollected data, which is the "notice" and sensor layer;
  - cognitive shortcuts under time pressure;
  - fragmented records;
  - feedback loops that never tell the diagnostician they were wrong.
- This partly **contradicts C2's framing**. A knowledge gap is one cause, but the information gap (the data never captured) and feedback-loop gaps are as large or larger. In machines, OEM lock on data and diagnostic tools is real, but medicine shows that removing it does not erase diagnostic error.
- **AI has the knowledge, and frontier models now beat physicians on vignettes** (Goh: 92% vs 74–76%; MAI-DxO: 80% vs 20%). This supports C3 as stated: in a narrow sense, the cost of access to knowledge has collapsed.
- **But the AI does not transfer through humans** (Goh, Agarwal), and **deployed models break when tested independently** (Epic Sepsis, Watson, Google DR Thailand). C3's "essentially gone" is too strong for outcomes.
- **Measured efficiency gains in RCTs are about 20–45%, not 10x.** MASAI cut workload 44%; MAI-DxO cut test spend about 20%.
  - Best real effect sizes are about 1.2–2x on the diagnostic step.
  - 10x on diagnosis alone is not supported by medical evidence to date. Whether "combined" steps could reach 10x is not shown.
- **Where the money goes.** Diagnosis is a small share of direct spend but controls the large downstream spend. The real prize is fewer wrong treatments (in machines: parts swapped, return visits, No-Fault-Found removals), not cheaper diagnostic labour.
- **Certification and accountability are the binding constraint.**
  - FDA clearance does not bring reimbursement.
  - Liability is concentrated in diagnosis.
  - Radiologists still sign off.
  - This supports C6: inspection and validation of the AI itself becomes a new industry (independent validation like Wong et al., post-market monitoring).
- **On C7: incentives, not ignorance.** Fee-for-service, liability, credentialing and the absence of an error-feedback loop explain why medicine is inefficient. That suggests "built for inefficiency" is better framed as "incentives and accountability structures reward the current process". It is mixed support for C7.
- **On C1.** Medicine's diagnostic process is iterative, and diagnosis is often confirmed by response to treatment ("treat and observe"). The linear chain is a useful simplification, but real diagnosis loops back on itself.

## Implications for a machine-repair company
- Win on **routing plus validated accuracy**: AI-alone triage for routine cases, humans for the long tail, as in MASAI and Agarwal.
- Own **the data capture layer** ("notice"), since that is where the Google DR deployment failed.
- Build a **closed feedback loop**: the outcome of each repair labels the diagnosis. Medicine lacks this loop, and machines can have it cheaply.
- Sell to whoever pays for downtime and No-Fault-Found waste. Do not sell "knowledge".
