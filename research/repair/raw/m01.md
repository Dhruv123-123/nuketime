# m01: Commercial aviation MRO cost structure: breakdown of MRO spend by engine, component, line, airframe; labor vs material share; OEM share of MRO market (Oliver Wyman MRO survey/forecasts, IATA MCX)

## Findings

### 1. Market size [data]
- Oliver Wyman: the MRO market was $119B in 2025, about 12% above the 2019 record. It reaches $156B by 2035 (2.7% CAGR). The fleet grows from about 29,079 aircraft (2025) to 38,309 (2035). The 2024 edition gave $104B (2024), going to $124B (2034).
- IATA MCX (citing IATA Economics and Oliver Wyman): MRO spend was $103.9B in 2024, **11.5% of total airline operating costs** (10% in 2019) and 10.8% of revenue. History: $82B (2019), $50B (2020), $62B (2021), $79B (2022), $94B (2023), $104B (2024).
- Reading: maintenance is a big but bounded line item. A 10x cut across all of MRO would be worth about 10% of airline cost, which matters but is not "everything in the world". Fuel is 29% of operating costs (supply chain report).

### 2. Spend by segment [data]
- Oliver Wyman Exhibit 13 (2024 spend, in the IATA/OW Oct 2025 report): **Engine 51.8%, Component 18.2%, Airframe and modifications 17.3%, Line 12.7%.**
- Engine MRO grew from 48% of the market in 2019 to 52% in 2025 (OW).
- IATA MCX FY2024 gives slightly different rounding: engine 41% (2019) to 50% (2024), component 17% to 18%, airframe 26% to 17%. Line is the remainder, about 15%.
- Widebodies are 20.3% of the active fleet but 42% of MRO cost (MCX).
- Component MRO was about $20.9B (2024). Its largest ATA categories are wheels, tires and brakes (18%), APU (13%), avionics (13%), fuel systems (8%), other (7%) and landing gear (6%) (OW Exhibit 14).
- Reading: half of all money goes to engine shop visits, which are mostly scheduled and predictable overhauls. Most of that cost is not in the "notice, diagnose, understand" steps.

### 3. Labor vs material [data / consultant analysis]
- OW: "Materials content is highest in engine and component maintenance (60%+), while much lower for airframe and line maintenance (20% or less)."
- OW Exhibit 16, typical narrowbody engine shop visit, direct cost to the MRO:
  - Non-LLP visit: materials about 60%, piece-part repairs 20-30%, **labor 5-10%**, other 0-5%.
  - LLP visit: costs 2x or more. LLPs alone are about 50-60%, and total materials are 65-80%. Labor is 5-10% or less of a larger total.
- Labor in engine and component work is "concentrated in disassembly, inspection, test, and reassembly". Airframe and line work is labor-heavy, with materials 20% or less.
- MCX breaks costs into material, labor, engine LLPs and outside repairs, but the split itself is only in the members-only part of the report (not public).
- Reading: in the biggest segment, labor (including any knowledge-work labor in diagnosis) is about 5-10% of cost. The money is in physical parts (LLPs: discs, shafts; hot-section blades) and OEM-controlled repairs. Knowledge-heavy labor is a larger share only in airframe and line work, which together are about 30% of spend.

### 4. OEM share of MRO, by segment [data / consultant analysis]
- OW Exhibit 15, market participation by firm type, 2024:
  - **Engine:** OEM 63%, airline MRO 16%, independent 6%, JV 16%. OEMs plus OEM JVs are about 79%.
  - **Airframe:** OEM 5%, airline MRO 46%, independent 28%, JV 21%.
  - **Component:** OEM 27%, airline MRO 42%, independent 28%, JV 3%.
- OEM participation in components is strongest in high-value, complex categories (nacelles and thrust reversers, avionics, landing gear, APUs) and weak in simpler ones (hydraulics).
- Rough weighted average using segment weights (engine 52, component 18, airframe 17; line excluded or mostly airline): OEM plus JV is about 0.52×79 + 0.18×30 + 0.17×26 ≈ 41 + 5.4 + 4.4 ≈ **51% of non-line MRO spend**. This is my calculation.

### 5. How OEM control works: IP, licences, parts, not "unknown knowledge" [consultant analysis, IATA-commissioned]
- OW: "OEMs have the design details, technical publications, repair instructions, test equipment, and spare parts required to effectively participate in the aftermarket."
- OW: licensed MROs "are typically restricted by OEM commercial license terms from using other approved repairs (such as EASA Part 21 and/or FAA DERs), PMAs, and even USM. Independent MROs do not face these restrictions ... provided their customers (airlines and lessors) are receptive."
- OW: embedded licence and royalty costs "may not always be widely known among airline procurement teams."
- Lifecycle effect (OW Exhibit 18): OEM control is highest at entry into service and in growth (development 5-8 years, then 10-12 years). It falls as the platform matures and in its 30+ year sunset, when USM and PMA become good alternatives and "unlicensed providers develop lower-cost repair options."
- Engine OE discounts can be "up to 80% or more". Engine OEM gross margins are typically **-5% to 10% on manufacturing vs 20-35% on MRO**. MRO services accounted for 74% and 66% of revenue at named engine OEMs (OW report; the exact attribution is garbled in the PDF text).
- Regulatory counterweights: FAA Order 8110.54A and EASA PS-AIR-21.50-01 require design approval holders to make Instructions for Continued Airworthiness available. The report notes "ongoing debate within the industry on what level of information should be made available within ICAs."
- Reading: the knowledge gap is real, but it is contractual and legal (IP, licences, ICA scope, warranty tie-ins) and backed by physical parts supply. It is not an epistemic gap that AI can close. An AI that could deduce a repair still could not produce an approved repair (DER/Part 21), a certified part (PMA) or lessor acceptance.

### 6. Competition opens up when rules change [data / campaigner]
- IATA-CFM agreement (2019, renewed Jan 2026 to Feb 2033): CFM manuals and repair instructions stay usable with non-CFM parts and repairs, and warranty is judged on cause, not sourcing. On the CFM56, "close to 40 shops compete" and CFM does "only about a third of overhauls". On the LEAP, there are six Premier MROs and more than a dozen licensed shops.
- IATA/OW estimate that aftermarket and supply chain problems added **$5.7B** to airline engine leasing and maintenance costs in 2025: $3.1B extra maintenance and $2.6B extra engine leasing. The total supply chain cost to airlines was more than $11B in 2025, including about $4.2B of delayed fuel efficiency and $1.4B of excess inventory.
- Willie Walsh (IATA DG): "Airlines have long struggled with the aftermarket business practices of manufacturers." [campaigner view]

### 7. Where time goes: parts availability, not diagnosis [survey data]
- OW 2025 MRO Survey: more than 75% of respondents saw engine and APU turnaround times rise from 2024 to 2025. More than 60% saw them rise for interiors, avionics and landing gear.
- Engine TAT: the industry standard was 30-60 days. Visits now last "upward of 300 days in some instances", with full overhauls around 75 days and quick turns around 50 days. Landing gear overhauls went from 45 days to 90-120 days.
- **Asked why TATs increased, 80% named piece-part availability as the top issue, ahead of supply/demand imbalance, insufficient labor and OEM control of key repairs.**
- USM supply is limited because fewer old aircraft are being parted out. Use of PMA is limited by how many PMA options exist and by whether airlines and lessors will accept them at aircraft transitions.
- More than 1,100 aircraft were grounded (3.8% of the fleet, vs 1.3% in 2015-18), and 69% of them have PW1000G engines (MCX FY2024). The GTF problem (a powder-metal defect) was diagnosed and understood quickly. The constraint was shop slots and HPT disks.

### 8. Cost inflation [survey data]
- OW 2024 MRO Survey: material costs rose about 8.3% in 2023 (pre-pandemic: 3-4% a year). MRO labor costs rose 7.3% (pre-pandemic: 2-3%).
- OW 2025 survey: materials up more than 7% (operators 7.6%, MRO/OEM 7.9%). Wages had been expected to grow 5.8% but grew 6.6%.
- MCX: airline salaries rose 8% YoY in 2024 and were 26% above 2019.

### 9. Labor scarcity [data / forecast]
- OW 2023: a North American shortfall of 12,000-18,000 maintenance workers, peaking in 2027 with a gap of about 43,000-48,000 (24-27%). Independent MROs and regionals are hit hardest because they act as training pipelines.
- OW 2025: more than a third of North American mechanics are at or near retirement age. MROs report it takes 2-3 years to get new technicians fully productive.
- Reading: labor is a small share of engine cost but is the scarce input in hangar and line work and in teardown and inspection. AI tutoring, guided inspection and documentation help here more than with "knowledge monopoly".

### 10. AI adoption and value so far [survey data]
- OW 2024 survey: 85% of operators and 46% of MROs are investing in AI, but **80% said they have seen little value so far**. Value from frontline mobility, paperless operations and MRO IT modernization met or beat expectations for about three-quarters of respondents. Operators show high interest in predictive maintenance.
- The IATA/OW report recommends "central repositories for aircraft maintenance data to serve as a shared knowledge base" and digital tracking tools. This is a shared-data play. It is not framed as AI replacing OEM knowledge.

### 11. Profit pool [investor/consultant view, from sibling note o01]
- AlixPartners 2025 (via o01): engine OEMs take about 47% of the aerospace profit pool at a 19.5% margin. MRO providers earn about 7%, airframers about 1.5% EBIT. "In most subsystems the component designer keeps nearly all" the aftermarket margin. https://www.alixpartners.com/insights/102nrqd/engine-makers-gain-from-the-aerospace-earnings-divide/
- GE Aerospace FY2025 (via o01): CES services were about 75% of segment revenue, and spare parts revenue grew more than 25%. Third parties do more than 15% of LEAP shop visits.

### 12. Benchmark unit costs [data]
- MCX FY2024 (28 airlines, 2,703 aircraft, $13.19B spend): **$1,522 per flight hour, $3,758 per flight cycle, $5.05M per aircraft per year**. Average fleet age 10.6 years, dispatch reliability 98.86%. MCX itself covers more than 50 airlines in total (about a quarter of world fleet and MRO spend, per the IATA MCTG page). Note the 98.86% dispatch reliability: noticing and fixing day-to-day faults already works well.

## Synthesis against the hypotheses
- **C1 (universal chain): mixed.** Most MRO dollars (engine overhauls, heavy checks) are scheduled, interval or LLP driven. "Notice and diagnose" barely applies: overhauls are "typically highly predictable, interval-based". Unscheduled line and component work does follow the chain. The rubric needs a "scheduled/predictive" branch, and the inspection step sits inside the repair (teardown inspection makes up much of the labor).
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): supports, with a reframe.** OEMs hold about 63% of engine MRO (about 79% with JVs) and capture margin through IP: manuals, repair instructions, test equipment, licences and parts. But the monopoly is legal and contractual plus physical parts supply, not a gap in know-how. Where the rules opened (CFM56), about 40 shops compete and the OEM does only a third.
- **C3 (AI removes the gap): contradicts.** The gap is IP and certification. AI cannot grant DER/PMA approval, licence rights or lessor acceptance. Industry AI investment is high, but 80% report little value so far (2024).
- **C4 (10x on the four steps): contradicts for aviation.** In engine MRO (52% of spend), labor is 5-10%. Materials and repairs are 80-95%, and time goes mostly to piece-part availability (named by 80% as the top TAT cause). Diagnosis and understanding are a thin slice. Sourcing and delivery cost is dominated by the price and availability of OEM parts, which is a manufacturing-capacity and IP problem. Logistics optimisation does not solve it.
- **C5 (AI parts factory): contradicts, mostly.** PMA and USM are the existing alternative and are capacity and acceptance limited. LLPs (50-60% of an LLP shop visit) are among the most heavily certified parts in existence. Opportunity exists in the long tail of components, cabin and interiors (BFE, "limited repairs available from OEMs") and in sunset fleets.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports.** Labor in engine and component work is "concentrated in disassembly, inspection, test, and reassembly". Labor scarcity (24-27% gap, 2-3 years to train) plus paper-heavy certification means inspection and records work is a real bottleneck and an AI-relevant target.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.** The structure is deliberately designed: OE discounts of up to 80% are recouped through MRO margins of 20-35%, and licences restrict PMA, USM and DER use. That makes it inefficient for airlines but efficient for OEM profit. The system also yields 98.9% dispatch reliability and extreme safety. The fixes IATA proposes are competition rules (licences, ICA access), PMA/USM/DER expansion and capacity, not a ground-up rebuild.

## Where the money and time go
Money: about half of MRO goes to engines, and 80-95% of that is materials and OEM repairs. LLPs alone can be more than half of a shop visit. Time: piece-part availability and shop capacity (300-day visits). Labor and knowledge are a minor cost share in engines, but they are the scarce input in airframe and line work.

## Real bottleneck
OEM-controlled IP (repair data, licences, approved repairs) combined with the physical, certified production capacity for high-value piece parts (LLPs, hot section) and certified shop slots, plus a shrinking mechanic workforce.

## Company shapes suggested
- A tool for building and approving DER/PMA repair and part cases: AI-accelerated engineering substantiation, with airline and lessor acceptance data. It attacks the real moat legally.
- USM/teardown intelligence: predicting part-out yields, matching USM to shop-visit demand, and tracing records (back-to-birth documentation). This is records and paperwork automation.
- An AI copilot for inspection and records for technicians, aimed at the 2-3 year training lag and the paper-heavy certification documentation.
- A shop-visit workscope optimiser, cutting LLP and material spend by choosing the right workscope. This is the biggest dollar lever, and it is a knowledge problem, but the knowledge involved is optimisation, not repair.
- Long-tail component, cabin and BFE repair and parts manufacturing, where OEM support is thin and certification is lighter.
