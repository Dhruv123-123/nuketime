## Findings: hard data from primary sources

### F1. The OEM aftermarket is the larger half of a major pump OEM's business (data: Flowserve FY2025 10-K)
- "Aftermarket sales represented approximately 53% of total sales in 2025 and 51% of total sales in 2024." Total 2025 sales were about $4.7B, up $171.5M (+3.8%), and the increase was "driven by aftermarket customer sales".
- Network: 37 manufacturing plants and **126 Quick Response Centers (QRCs) in 48 countries**. "A large portion of FPD's service work is performed on a quick response basis and we offer 24-hour service in all of our major markets."
- On what drives lock-in: "In geographic regions where we are positioned to provide quick response, we believe customers have traditionally relied on us, rather than our competitors, for aftermarket products due to our highly engineered and customized products. However, the aftermarket for standard products is competitive, as the existence of common standards allows for easier replacement of the installed products. As proximity of service centers, timeliness of delivery and quality are important considerations..."
- Strategy: "we continue to selectively expand our global QRC capabilities to improve our ability to capture an increasing portion of this important aftermarket business"; digitising the installed base through the RedRaven IIoT monitoring product.
- Risk factor on knowledge: "We are subject to the risk that we will not be able to effectively replace the knowledge and expertise of an aging workforce as workers retire."
- Source: Flowserve Corp 10-K for FY2025, filed 2026-02-17, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/30625/000003062526000003/fls-20251231.htm
- Reading: the OEM's own filing says its moat in the aftermarket is (a) engineered/custom designs (proprietary geometry and know-how), plus (b) being physically close and fast. For standard API/ANSI pumps, it says common standards already make the aftermarket competitive. So the "knowledge monopoly" is real for engineered equipment but weak for standardised equipment, and proximity and logistics matter as much as knowledge.

### F2. Compressor/turbine OEM service runs on long-term service agreements of 10+ years (data: Baker Hughes FY2025 10-K)
- Baker Hughes total 2025 revenue: $27.7B. The IET segment (gas turbines, compressors, LNG trains) grew to $13.4B. Gas Technology Services (GTS) revenue was $3,028M in 2024 and $2,797M in 2023. Another table row shows 3,769 / 3,141 / 3,004, probably orders; I have not confirmed that.
- GTS "provides advanced aftermarket support and uptime availability in critical environments and through every stage of our customers' equipment and plant life cycle."
- "We have long-term service agreements with our customers, primarily within our IET segment. These agreements typically require us to maintain assets sold to the customer over a defined contract term. These agreements have an **average contract term of greater than 10 years**."
- Source: Baker Hughes Co 10-K for FY2025, filed 2026-02-05, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1701605/000170160526000007/bkr-20251231.htm
- Reading: for big compressors and turbines the OEM wraps diagnosis, parts and repair into multi-decade contracts priced on uptime. This is where C2's "knowledge monopoly" is strongest. It is held in place by contracts, warranties and insurer and lender requirements, not only by the knowledge gap.

### F3. The regulator makes repair quality, parts suitability and inspection legally mandatory (regulator: OSHA PSM 29 CFR 1910.119)
- (j)(4): "Inspections and tests shall be performed on process equipment", following "recognized and generally accepted good engineering practices" (RAGAGEP, which in practice means API standards such as API 610, 617, 682 and 686). Inspection frequency must follow manufacturers' recommendations and good practice, and be more frequent when operating experience calls for it. Each inspection must be documented.
- (j)(5): "The employer shall correct deficiencies in equipment that are outside acceptable limits ... before further use or in a safe and timely manner when necessary means are taken to assure safe operation."
- (j)(6)(iii): "The employer shall assure that maintenance materials, spare parts and equipment are suitable for the process application for which they will be used."
- (i): a pre-startup safety review is required for new or modified facilities where the modification changes process safety information.
- Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.119
- Reading: in the US process industry the chain ends in a legally required inspection step (supports C6). The parts-suitability rule (j)(6)(iii) also puts the burden of qualifying any reverse-engineered or AI-designed part on the plant owner. This is a direct brake on C5.

### F4. Failures of the inspection step, not of knowledge, drive the catastrophic cases (regulator: US CSB, Chevron Richmond 2012)
- CSB recommendations after the 2012 Chevron Richmond refinery fire (sulfidation corrosion in low-silicon carbon steel piping) include: 100% component inspection or replacement of at-risk piping; damage-mechanism reviews that make sure "all necessary mechanical integrity work" is identified and done on time; an "auditable process for denied or deferred inspection work"; and a technical review before minimum alert thickness values are lowered. 37 recommendations: 36 closed, 1 open.
- Source: https://www.csb.gov/chevron-refinery-fire/
- Reading: the damage mechanism was known in the industry. The failure was organisational: inspection work was deferred and denied. The bottleneck here was incentives and governance, not knowledge. This contradicts a pure C2/C3 framing and supports C6.

### F5. Standards bodies are relied on as the safety backstop (regulator: CSB Williams Geismar 2013)
- The CSB found that API 521 §4.4.12 allowed a hydraulic-expansion overpressure scenario to rely on administrative controls without a relief device. It recommended that API require a relief device. Two workers died.
- Source: https://www.csb.gov/williams-olefins-plant-explosion-and-fire-/
- Reading: API standards are the codified "knowledge" layer, and regulators push failures back into them. The knowledge is not secret. It is published and standardised, and it is behind a paywall (API standards are sold documents). The scarce resource is applying it correctly at the right moment.

### F6. Downtime cost scale and trend (study: Siemens/Senseye "True Cost of Downtime 2024")
- Survey of 181 maintenance, engineering and IT professionals at large industrial organisations (Automotive, FMCG, Heavy Industry, Oil & Gas), covering April 2019 to March 2023.
- Oil & Gas per-hour downtime cost follows the oil price. It peaked in 2022 (oil near $120/bbl) and by 2023 was back near the 2019 level. "Oil & Gas costs per plant in 2023 are half what they were in 2019. But in 2022, they were three times as high." The O&G per-hour dollar figures are only in charts, which did not extract.
- All sectors: the average large plant lost 27 hours a month to unplanned downtime (39 in 2019), about 326 hours a year. The Fortune Global 500 lose about $1.4T a year to unplanned downtime, about 11% of revenue. Heavy Industry cut its downtime hours to nearly a third of the 2019 level.
- Listed cost components include "Cost of emergency replacement parts" and "The cost of salaries for those rectifying the problem".
- The report claims predictive maintenance "reduces the need for replacement parts by up to 40%" and helps companies "cope better with the retirement of many experienced engineers". These are vendor claims.
- Source: https://assets.new.siemens.com/siemens/assets/api/uuid:1b43afb5-2d07-47f7-9eb7-893fe7d0bc59/TCOD-2024_original.pdf
- Reading: downtime hours were already falling sharply (about -30%) with IIoT and condition monitoring before generative AI. The "notice/diagnose" steps are already being digitised. Lost production dominates the cost, not the repair invoice: the value of a repair is set by hours of lost throughput.

### F7. Reverse engineering of obsolete pump parts is already an established OEM and independent service (practitioner/vendor: Sulzer)
- Sulzer Services offers reverse engineering of "worn or obsolete components" and of "complex, urgent, or discontinued parts". It uses 3D scanning, CAD and simulation, root-cause analysis, material upgrades and additive manufacturing, claims "reduced lead times", and validates each part by test. It gives no lead-time numbers.
- Sulzer: CHF 3.3B revenue (2023), three divisions (Flow, Services, Chemtech), about 180 manufacturing and service centres worldwide, about 13,130 employees.
- Sources: https://www.sulzer.com/en/shared/products/reverse-engineering ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulzer_(manufacturer)
- Reading: C5's "AI parts factory" exists today in a pre-AI form. Large non-OEM service houses (Sulzer, and independents such as Hydro Inc.) already reverse-engineer impellers, casings and wear rings. The binding constraints are material certification, testing and the owner's (j)(6) suitability duty, not geometry capture.

## Findings: practitioner background [BACKGROUND, not re-verified this run]
- B1 (Bloch, *Pump Users Handbook* / *Improving Machinery Reliability*): average refinery centrifugal pump MTBF is often quoted at about 2-4 years, while best-of-class plants reach 6-8+ years with the same API pumps. The gap is attributed to installation (API 686 alignment, baseplate grouting), lubrication practice, seal selection and operating off the best efficiency point, not to missing OEM knowledge. Bloch's long-quoted figure for the average refinery pump repair is roughly $10k+ in direct cost. Mechanical seals and bearings account for most pump repair events. Implication: inefficiency comes from discipline and practice, and the knowledge has been published for decades.
- B2 (turnaround literature, e.g. IPA and Solomon benchmarking as commonly cited): turnarounds take a large share (often quoted as 30-50%) of a plant's multi-year maintenance spend. Many turnarounds overrun cost and schedule. The main drivers are late scope freeze, "discovery"/emergent work found only when equipment is opened, contractor craft labour availability, and critical-path long-lead parts. Planning and scope definition 12-24 months ahead is the main lever.
- B3: the API machinery standards are API 610 (centrifugal pumps, 12th ed.), API 617 (centrifugal/axial compressors), API 618 (reciprocating compressors), API 682 (seals), API 686 (installation), API 670 (machinery protection) and API 691 (risk-based machinery management). They are design and purchase specs. Repair is governed largely by plant practice, OEM manuals and RAGAGEP, so repair quality varies a lot between shops.
- B4: the big lead-time items are engineered rotors, impellers, casings, dry gas seals and compressor bundles, which take months from the OEM. Plants keep "insurance spares" (capital spares) for exactly this reason. Supply-chain disruption in 2021-23 lengthened lead times.

## Stance reasoning
- **C1 (universal chain): mixed.** The steps exist, but in process plants the dominant mode is not reactive repair at all. It is (a) condition monitoring that never reaches "failure" and (b) planned turnarounds, where sourcing and scoping happen 12-24 months before "noticing" (inspection on opening). Long-term service agreements (F2) merge diagnose, source and repair into one contract. The chain is a usable rubric but not a fixed order, and repairs are often batched.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed.** It holds for engineered compressors and turbines and custom pumps (F1, F2: "highly engineered and customized products", LTSAs over 10 years). For standard API/ANSI pumps the OEM itself says common standards make the aftermarket competitive. Proximity, speed and quality are stated as equally important. The catastrophic failures (F4) came from deferred inspection, not ignorance. Knowledge loss does exist, but as tacit expertise retiring (Flowserve risk factor), not as a monopoly.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap): mixed, leaning contradicts.** Codified knowledge (API standards, published reliability practice) was never the binding gap. Tacit craft skill, liability, and the owner's legal duty to assure part suitability (F3) do not disappear with AI. AI can help retain retiring expertise and with diagnosis (F6 shows monitoring already cut downtime hours by about a third).
- **C4 (10x cost drop in four steps): contradicts.** The cost that matters is lost production per hour (F6), and in turnarounds it is craft labour, scope discovery and critical-path parts. Diagnosis and "understanding" are a small slice. Parts delivery is limited by lead times for metallurgy and machining, not information. A 10x drop in those four steps would not translate into a 10x system gain.
- **C5 (bespoke parts solved): contradicts.** Reverse engineering with scanning and AM is already commercial (F7). It is held back by qualification and testing and by OSHA (j)(6)(iii) suitability. It is not "effectively solved" by geometry generation alone.
- **C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports.** PSM mandates inspection and testing per RAGAGEP, documentation, and pre-startup review (F3). The CSB cases show inspection deferral as a root cause (F4, F5).
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.** OEMs explicitly design the aftermarket for capture (QRC expansion, LTSAs, aftermarket now 53% of Flowserve sales). But much of the apparent "inefficiency" is deliberate redundancy for safety and liability (insurance spares, standards, inspection). Downtime hours fell about 30% in four years, which points to incremental improvement rather than a system that cannot change.

## Where the money and time go
- Lost production dominates: per-hour downtime cost tracks the oil price, and per-plant losses run to tens to hundreds of millions of dollars a year (F6). Within turnarounds, contractor craft labour, emergent and discovery scope, and long-lead engineered parts (rotors, seals, bundles) set both cost and critical path [B2, B4]. Diagnosis is cheap relative to these and is already being automated by condition monitoring.

## Real bottleneck
- (1) Qualified hands and craft capacity during compressed turnaround windows. (2) Lead time on engineered, material-certified parts. (3) Qualification and certification of non-OEM parts and repairs under PSM and insurer rules. (4) Organisational discipline: deferred inspection and poor installation practice. Missing information is not the main constraint.

## Company-shape ideas the evidence points at
- An independent, AI-assisted reverse-engineering, qualification and certification house for obsolete or long-lead rotating-equipment parts (impellers, wear rings, seal components). It would sell a certified, PSM-(j)(6)-compliant data package, not just a part. This fits where Sulzer already plays (F7), but faster and OEM-agnostic.
- Turnaround scope-prediction software: predict discovery work before opening, using inspection and condition-monitoring history, to cut emergent scope and pre-order long-lead parts. This attacks the actual cost driver (B2).
- Captured tacit expertise for retiring rotating-equipment engineers (Flowserve's own risk factor), sold to owner-operators who want to reduce LTSA dependence for non-critical machines.
- Inspection and mechanical-integrity tooling that makes deferrals auditable (the CSB Chevron recommendation) and uses robotic or AI inspection inside PSM.
