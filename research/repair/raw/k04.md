# k04: Cases where full technical data rights or open designs existed (government-owned designs, open-source hardware, older aircraft with public manuals) — did repair become cheap? What remained expensive?

Date: 2026-10-20. Method note: the shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn was used up before this agent ran (every WebSearch call returned "budget used up"). All findings below come from direct WebFetch of known primary or secondary pages (GAO product pages, CCC Crash Course, Wikipedia). Items marked [memory, unverified] come from prior knowledge, were not re-fetched, and should be checked before anyone cites them. Coverage is thinner than intended. A follow-up run with search budget should pull: GAO-20-588 (Navy ship on-time rates), DoD IP Cadre material, MARPA PMA savings figures, Travis AFB / Rapid Sustainment Office additive-part cases, and Army right-to-repair 2025 contract language.

## Framing

The question tests C2/C3 (the knowledge gap is the bottleneck) with a natural experiment. If the "knowledge monopoly" were the main cost driver, then cases where the knowledge is fully available (government owns the data, the design is open, manuals are public, legally mandated information access) should show much cheaper repair. They mostly don't. Knowledge access helps at the margin, mostly by adding competition on price. But the dominant costs sit in: (a) physical capacity and labour (dock or depot slots, skilled hands); (b) certification and airworthiness authority, meaning someone legally accountable for the type design; (c) supply of physical parts at low volume (obsolescence, diminishing manufacturing sources); (d) funding and contracting process; (e) the inherent hours of disassembly, inspection and rework, plus unplanned work found once the item is opened.

## Findings

### 1. Navy ships: the government largely owns designs and runs public shipyards, yet there is a ~$1.8B deferred maintenance backlog driven by capacity and funding (data, regulator)
- GAO-22-105032 (May 2022), "Navy Ships: Applying Leading Practices and Transparent Reporting Could Help Reduce Risks Posed by Nearly $1.8 Billion Maintenance Backlog". https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105032
- Backlog of about $1.8B ($1.7B surface ships, about $0.1B carriers). $1.2B of it is tied to 9 ships proposed for early decommissioning, which removes 34 ship-years of service life.
- Stated causes: "operational demands or limited shipyard capacity and funding". Knowledge is not cited.
- The Navy's financial report showed only $181M, understating the backlog by about $1.6B.
- Interpretation: in the case most like "government owns the knowledge", the binding constraints are dry-dock and shipyard capacity, labour and money. Repair is deferred so long that the asset is retired early.

### 2. Navy ship repair delays come from the funding approval process and unquantified "growth work" (regulator)
- GAO-20-370 (May 2020). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-370
- Across 41 major repair availabilities since 2015, average cost growth was 5% (21 of 41 came in under estimate), but schedule outcomes were worse.
- Approval of extra prior-year funding (above $4M, crossing fiscal years) took 26 to 189 days. "Unquantified work" (repairs expected but impossible to scope until the ship is opened) is the main source of delay.
- Interpretation: even when you know what the ship is and what is wrong, how much is wrong only becomes clear after teardown. The delay sits in institutional process (budget law), not technical knowledge. AI diagnosis could narrow pre-scoping uncertainty, which partly supports C4 for the "understanding" step, but the funding cycle is a separate, non-technical bottleneck. That supports C7, but the cause is budget rules rather than a knowledge monopoly.

### 3. F-35: the opposite case. Without technical data, sustainment suffers, which supports C2 directly (regulator)
- GAO-23-105341 (Sept 2023). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- Life-cycle cost above $1.7T, of which about $1.3T is operating and support. Mission-capable rate about 55% (March 2023). More than 10,000 components waiting for repair. Depot stand-up behind schedule.
- Causes include "missing technical data and training", inadequate equipment, supply delays, and heavy reliance on the contractor without a settled government/contractor mix. DoD "has not identified or obtained the technical data" it needs.
- As of March 2026, 6 of 7 recommendations are open and partially addressed. The services must take over sustainment management by Oct 2027.
- Interpretation: this is the strongest evidence for C2. A missing data package does cripple repair. But note what GAO lists next to data: depot capacity, equipment, supply and training. Data is necessary but not sufficient.

### 4. Old fleets with mature, government-held knowledge are still expensive: aging, obsolescence and diminishing manufacturing sources (data, regulator)
- GAO-21-101SP (Nov 2020), weapon system sustainment. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-101sp
- Of 46 aircraft types (FY2011-2019), only 3 met mission-capable goals in most years and 24 never met them. Operating and support costs were above $49B in FY2018. KC-135 (a 1950s design with deep USAF depot knowledge at Tinker) cost about $4.24B in O&S, the most expensive in the sample.
- Cited causes: "aging aircraft, maintenance challenges, and supply issues, including service life extensions, diminishing manufacturing sources, and obsolescence."
- Interpretation: the KC-135 is the closest thing to an "older aircraft whose knowledge is fully in-house". It is the costliest fleet, because age creates structural fatigue and corrosion work and because the original suppliers no longer make the parts. Knowledge is not the scarce input. Physical parts and labour hours are.

### 5. A-10 rewinging: the OEM (Fairchild Republic) was gone, so the government paid to remanufacture wings. This is the "supplier gone" case, and it was not cheap (history, data)
- Wikipedia, A-10 Thunderbolt II (wing replacement section). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Republic_A-10_Thunderbolt_II
- Fatigue cracking at Wing Station 23. A 2003 review found the data behind the earlier wing-upgrade plan could not be trusted. USAF projected it would run out of wings by 2011.
- New wings (Boeing, 2007 contract): $741M initial, about $1.72B program life. 117 wings in the base contract, 173 on order by 2013. First flights Nov 2011.
- The earlier in-house plan was "greatly delayed" by base realignment cuts that removed about 80% of the original workforce.
- [memory, unverified] Boeing reportedly had to rebuild the design data (digitising and re-engineering drawings), and around 2019-2020 a follow-on contract went to Boeing/Korea Aerospace Industries for further wing sets after supply gaps.
- Interpretation: this tests C5 directly. When the original maker is gone, a substitute maker can be found, but the cost is in requalification, fatigue testing, tooling and low-rate production. That is about $6-10M per wing set, about 4-5 years to first flight, and about a decade of program time. The AI parts factory would face the same certification, test and tooling bill.

### 6. Automotive "right to repair" (MA 2012, national MOU 2014): independent shops got dealer-equivalent information, but repair costs kept rising (data, regulator, history)
- Wikipedia, Motor Vehicle Owners' Right to Repair Act, and Right to repair. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Vehicle_Owners%27_Right_to_Repair_Act ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_repair
- MA law passed with 86% voter support (2012). The 2014 MOU extended it to all 50 states (model year 2018 and later). The 2020 telematics expansion is in litigation, and NHTSA told OEMs in June 2023 to ignore it on preemption and cybersecurity grounds.
- CCC Crash Course (2025 edition, 2024 data). https://www.cccis.com/crash-course
  - Non-comprehensive total cost of repair is up $1,285 vs 2020. Labour rates rose 4.5% in 2024 (7.5% in 2023). Labour hours per claim were 27.3 in 2024 vs 23.0 in 2013.
  - Parts' share of increases is shrinking relative to labour and "miscellaneous" (which includes diagnostics). 86.3% of repairable DRP appraisals include a scan (about $157), 29.9% include a calibration (about $547). 56.3% of calibrations show up on supplements, which means they were discovered late.
  - Total-loss share hit a record 22.9% (non-comprehensive) in 2024.
- Interpretation: about a decade after the knowledge monopoly was largely dismantled by law in the largest repair market (light vehicles), repair cost rose, not fell. The cost drivers moved to labour hours, labour rates, sensors and ADAS calibration (equipment plus procedure), and part complexity. Information access helped keep the independent aftermarket alive. There is no evidence it made repair cheap. This contradicts C3/C4 as stated. It also suggests the "understanding" step is increasingly equipment-bound (calibration rigs, scan tools), not knowledge-bound.

### 7. Concorde: the design knowledge existed, but the type certificate holder walked away, and the fleet could not fly (history)
- Wikipedia, Type certificate (Validity section) and Concorde. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_certificate ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde
- If a TC holder stops support without transferring the certificate, the TC goes back to the authority and registry states can ground the fleet. The article cites Concorde: grounded when Airbus surrendered the TC. Retirement reasons (2003) were rising maintenance costs, low loads after the 2000 crash, and the post-9/11 slump.
- Interpretation: in regulated sectors the scarce asset is not knowledge but accountable design authority. Someone must legally own continued airworthiness (answering ADs, approving repairs). Supports C6 strongly, and refines C2: the "monopoly" is a liability and authority monopoly as much as a knowledge one.

### 8. DC-3: public, extremely mature knowledge base (90-year-old type, manuals everywhere), yet the fleet is dying out from maintenance and insurance cost (history)
- Wikipedia, Douglas DC-3. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-3
- About 2,000 flying in 2013, more than 300 in 2017, about 150 in 2023 (estimates differ in method, so treat as directional). The article says the number "is dwindling due to maintenance costs", aircraft age and insurance.
- Basler BT-67 turbine conversion (PT6A-67R, fuselage plug, structural strengthening under an STC) is how the type survives commercially. That is re-engineering plus re-certification, not cheap repair.
- Interpretation: perfect knowledge plus a cult following does not make an old aircraft cheap to keep flying. Corrosion and fatigue labour, scarce parts, and insurance dominate.

### 9. Cuba's "yank tanks": the knowledge is diffused into the population and labour is cheap, but parts are cut off. Cars survive through improvisation at much lower utilisation (history)
- Wikipedia, Yank tank. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yank_tank
- About 60,000 classic US cars out of about 173,000 cars in Cuba (dated figures). Owners use household items and Soviet engines (diesel retrofits) and cannibalise ("part out") cars.
- Average annual travel fell from nearly 3,000 km/yr (mid-1980s) to below 800 km/yr (2000-01).
- Interpretation: this is a natural experiment where know-how is abundant and parts supply is the binding constraint. The result is not efficient repair. It is low-reliability, low-utilisation survival. Supports the idea that parts sourcing and making (C5) matters a lot, while showing that improvised parts do not equal certified parts.

### 10. Iran Air under sanctions: knowledge partly available, parts and OEM support cut off, more than 100 aircraft grounded, safety fell (history, news)
- Wikipedia, Iran Air. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran_Air
- In 2014, more than 100 Iranian aircraft were grounded "for lack of new parts and technical expertise". Fleet average age was 24.1 years (Mar 2017). Aircraft were "parted out". Safety record was "plunging".
- Interpretation: when the OEM channel is cut, a capable state-level operator with engineers and manuals still cannot keep a fleet airworthy. Parts plus OEM engineering support (service bulletins, repair approvals) are the binding constraint. That is evidence against C5's "effectively solved" claim. Iran had strong incentives to reverse-engineer and limited success at fleet scale.

### 11. Open-source hardware (Open Source Ecology, Global Village Construction Set): open designs did not produce a functioning low-cost machine ecosystem (practitioner view, history)
- Wikipedia, Open Source Ecology. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Source_Ecology
- Goal: 50 open-source industrial machines. 12 of 50 prototyped by 2014 (4 documented). "33% completion" reported in 2018. Thin documentation after 2016. Seed Eco-Home materials went from about $30k (2016) to about $50k (2022) as lumber prices rose. Openness did not insulate it from input-cost inflation.
- Interpretation: openness lowers the cost of knowledge to zero, but fabrication skill, tooling, materials and QA remain. Open designs have not displaced commercial tractors or machines.

### 12. RepRap / open-source 3D printers: openness did collapse the price of the machines, and printers are cheap to repair. But non-printable parts (motors, electronics, sensors) still come from industrial supply chains (history)
- Wikipedia, RepRap project. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RepRap_project
- GPL designs. In 2008 Darwin replicated its printed parts, about 48% of total parts excluding fasteners. Steppers, sensors and microcontrollers cannot be printed. The commercial arm RepRapPro stopped trading in 2016 because of "market congestion for low-cost 3D printers".
- Interpretation: this is the clearest supportive case. Open design plus commodity parts produced cheap machines and cheap repair (Prusa i3 clones; repair is mostly swapping commodity parts). The conditions were low certification burden, commodity components, and low consequence of failure. That boundary condition matters for any "AI parts factory".

### 13. Repairable-by-design consumer hardware (Fairphone, Framework): full repair documentation and first-party spare parts make repair cheap and fast for owners, but the business stays small and margin-thin (data)
- Wikipedia, Fairphone. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairphone ; Wikipedia, Framework Computer. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framework_Computer
- Fairphone: iFixit 10/10 on most devices. Units sold: about 116k (2022), 100k (2023), 103k (2024), 145k (2025). EBITDA was -€14.3M in 2023 and +€0.17M in 2025, on €73M revenue. Needed €49M of impact investment in 2023.
- Framework: iFixit 10/10. Open-sourced EC firmware and motherboard CAD (2022). Marketplace for parts. Total disclosed funding about $44M. Repeated shipment and firmware delays.
- Interpretation: making knowledge and parts open does make individual repairs cheap (a screwdriver and a mail-order part). The cost moves to the manufacturer, which must hold spare-parts inventory for years at low volume. Spare-parts logistics, not knowledge, is the expensive layer. Market pull is modest (about 0.01% of the ~1.2B/yr smartphone market).

### 14. Additive manufacturing of out-of-production military parts: real, but qualification dominates [memory, unverified]
- Widely reported (2018-2019): Travis AFB 60th Maintenance Squadron 3D-printed a C-5 lavatory/galley component for about $300 against a quoted $10,000+ from the original supplier. USAF Rapid Sustainment Office and Tinker's Reclamation/Reverse Engineering groups report big unit-cost savings on legacy parts.
- Common practitioner finding: savings are concentrated in non-flight-critical parts. Flight-critical parts need first-article inspection, material allowables and engineering authority sign-off. Those steps take months to years and cost a lot per part number, so the economics only work for high-AOG-impact or recurring parts.

### 15. PMA parts (FAA Parts Manufacturer Approval): a lawful path to non-OEM parts has existed since 1947/1965, and adoption is real but bounded (regulator, history)
- Wikipedia, Parts Manufacturer Approval. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval
- Routes are identicality, licensure, STC, or test and computation. Each needs FAA design approval plus a production quality system (aligned with 21.137 since 2011).
- [memory, unverified] Industry figures commonly cited: PMA parts are typically 30-40% cheaper than OEM, but PMA is a low-single-digit to about 10% share of the commercial aerospace parts market. OEMs fight back through warranty terms, lease return conditions and engine-shop agreements (lessors often refuse PMA in return conditions).
- Interpretation: a 30-40% price cut from breaking a parts monopoly is meaningful but not an order of magnitude. Adoption is held back by commercial and contractual lock-in (lessors, insurers, residual values), not by technical knowledge.

## Synthesis: what stayed expensive when knowledge was free

1. Physical capacity and skilled labour hours. Shipyards and depots (findings 1, 4, 5), auto labour hours up 19% since 2013 (6).
2. Certification and accountable design authority. TC/STC/PMA, first-article, engineering sign-off (5, 7, 8, 14, 15).
3. Low-volume physical parts supply. Obsolescence and diminishing manufacturing sources (4), Cuba (9), Iran (10), spare-parts inventory economics (13).
4. Equipment-bound diagnosis and calibration. ADAS scans and calibrations (6).
5. Institutional process. Fiscal-year funding approval taking 26-189 days (2), contract structure (3).
6. Unknown scope until teardown. "Unquantified work" (2), 56% of calibrations found on supplements (6).

Where openness did make repair cheap: low-consequence, commodity-component, uncertified domains (RepRap/Prusa printers, Framework/Fairphone owner repairs). The boundary condition is low regulatory burden plus commodity sub-parts plus modular design. That boundary does not reach washing machines-to-airliners uniformly.

Counter-evidence for C2 (knowledge matters): F-35 (3) shows that missing data really cripples sustainment. So the right claim is "lack of data is a binding constraint when absent, but having it does not make repair cheap". Data is necessary, not sufficient.

## Stance notes
- C1: Mostly consistent. Navy cases add "funding approval" and "scope discovery after teardown" as loops, so the chain is iterative, not linear. Mixed.
- C2: Mixed. F-35 supports it. Auto right to repair, Navy, KC-135 and DC-3 show that removing the knowledge gap leaves most of the cost.
- C3: Contradicts. Even full human-readable knowledge (manuals, government data, open CAD) did not close the gap. AI does not address authority, capacity or parts.
- C4: Contradicts the order-of-magnitude claim. Best documented price effects from opening parts or knowledge are about 30-40% (PMA, unverified), and auto costs rose after information access.
- C5: Contradicts. A-10 wings (about $1.7B, about a decade), Iran, Cuba. Remanufacturing is feasible but qualification, tooling and low-volume costs dominate. Feasible for non-critical parts (Travis-type cases).
- C6: Supports strongly. Concorde TC surrender, PMA and STC regimes, the A-10 data trust problem.
- C7: Mixed. Institutional inefficiency is real (Navy funding process, F-35 contractor reliance), but much of the cost is physics: fatigue, corrosion, teardown hours.
