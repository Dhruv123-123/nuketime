# p03: Aircraft parts marketplaces and data (ILS, PartsBase, Locatory, Aviall/Boeing distribution): how sourcing works today, search friction, price transparency, broker margins

## Method note (read first)
The shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn (200 calls) was already spent when this agent started, so **no web searches ran**. I fetched known primary URLs directly with WebFetch. Many returned 403/404 (Locatory, DoD IG PDF and landing page, FAA AC, ICF, SFO, Reuters). The findings below are split into:

## Findings

### How sourcing works today: listing marketplaces, not transaction platforms
1. **[V] ILSmart (Inventory Locator Service)**, https://www.ilsmart.com/ . The homepage claims:
   - 1B+ parts and services listed
   - 180K+ daily part searches
   - 29K+ customers in 125+ countries
   - "$5B in RFQs annually" in one place and "$7B+" parts RFQ value in another
   - $10M+ sold at auction
   - pitched as "the largest worldwide Aerospace Marketplace and Intelligence platform", now with AI/ML buyer and seller tools
   - no public pricing; it runs a subscription with a 14-day trial
   - Reading: the core product is a *locator*. It shows who claims to hold a part number. Price and condition come out of an RFQ back-and-forth, not a listed price. RFQ value is tens of times the auction (actual transaction) value, which shows that most of the deal still closes off-platform.
2. **[V] PartsBase**, https://www.partsbase.com/ :
   - claims 90M+ parts on PBLocator, 6,000+ aerospace companies and 140+ countries
   - sells "Quick RFQ", a $300 one-time PBExpress pass for 60 days of access, a PartStore for direct purchase, advertising, government contracting data, analytics and an ERP API
   - Reading: the business model is selling access to an inventory directory plus ads and data. Price discovery is still mostly by RFQ.
3. **[M] Locatory.com** is a Lithuania-origin parts locator that started inside the Avia Solutions Group ecosystem, with a similar list-and-RFQ model. Its homepage returned 403, so I could not verify it.
4. **Synthesis (opinion)**: an aircraft part sale typically runs as follows. The buyer searches a part number on ILS, PartsBase or Locatory and gets back dozens of listings from brokers, MROs, teardown shops and distributors, often the same physical part listed by several brokers. The buyer then sends RFQs and waits for quotes. The quotes have to be checked on four things:
   - condition code (NE/NS/OH/SV/AR)
   - trace (back-to-birth or last-operator chain)
   - paperwork (FAA 8130-3 / EASA Form 1)
   - lead time
   The friction is mostly about **trust and paperwork verification**, not about finding the part number. AI makes the search step cheap, but the search step was already cheap: ILS has had electronic search since the 1980s.

### Distribution consolidation (Aviall/Boeing)
5. **[V] Boeing Global Services (BGS)**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Global_Services :
   - 2023 revenue about $19-20B, operating income about $3.6B (about 18% margin by my calculation)
   - 60% commercial, 40% military
   - the article notes customer complaints about "long lead times" and higher parts prices
6. **[M] Acquisitions**: Boeing bought Aviall in 2006 for about $1.7B and KLX (aerospace consumables distribution) in 2018 for about $4.25B. OEMs have moved into distribution and aftermarket to capture margin. This is evidence that the aftermarket is where OEM profit sits.

### Price transparency / OEM and sole-source pricing power
7. **[V] TransDigm**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransDigm_Group . Wikipedia summary of DoD audits:
   - 2019: a "9,400% markup on a metal pin"; TransDigm refunded $16M after a congressional hearing
   - 2021 DoD IG: "excess profit of $20.8 million on 105 spare parts on 150 contracts"
   - most of its parts are proprietary and sole-source
   - roughly half of revenue is aftermarket
   - [M] TransDigm reports EBITDA-as-defined margins of around 50%+
   - The DoD IG has repeatedly found it could not get cost data, because the parts were sole-source and/or treated as "commercial items".
   - Reading: the monopoly here is **legal and certification-based** (type-design data plus FAA approval on one source), not a knowledge gap. Knowing how to make a pin does not let you sell an airworthy pin.
8. **[V] HEICO**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HEICO :
   - 2024 revenue $3.86B, operating income $824.5M (about 21%)
   - its Flight Support Group makes FAA-approved replacement parts (PMA) and repairs
   - [M] PMA parts are typically sold at about 30-40% below OEM list prices. Lufthansa Technik holds a stake in HEICO Aerospace.
   - Reading: alternative parts at roughly a one-third discount already exist under a regulated route. The constraint is getting approval for each part, not the knowledge to design it.
9. **[V] PMA route**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval :
   - FAA PMA needs design approval from the ACO (by identicality, licensure, STC, or test and computation) **plus** production approval from the MIDO (a quality system)
   - PMA grew in the 1980s as airlines sought cheaper spares
   - MARPA is the PMA trade group
   - Reading: this is the existing legal path for "bespoke" replacement parts. It carries a big fixed cost per part number, so it pays off only for high-volume parts. That cuts against C5 (an "AI parts factory") for certificated parts.

### Data rights as the knowledge monopoly (defence analog)
10. **[V] GAO-23-105341 (F-35 sustainment)**, https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341 :
    - lifecycle cost above $1.7T, of which about $1.3T is operations and sustainment
    - more than 10,000 components waiting for repair
    - mission-capable rate about 55% in March 2023
    - DoD "has neither determined the desired mix of government and contractor roles" nor identified and obtained the **technical data** it needs
    - Status in March 2026: six recommendations still open; transfer of sustainment elements planned for end of 2028
    - Reading: strong support for C2 in a specific form. The gap is *contractual access to technical data and IP*, and AI cannot conjure it. The government owns the jets but does not have the data rights to repair them on its own terms.

### Supply-chain cost (macro)
11. **[V] IATA, Dec 2024 outlook**, https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2024-releases/2024-12-10-01/ :
    - non-fuel costs $643B in 2024, projected $692B in 2025
    - unresolved supply chain issues are driving up leasing and maintenance costs
    - maintenance costs rose sharply in 2024 because of groundings and an ageing fleet
    - [M] A separate IATA/Oliver Wyman 2025 study put the supply-chain cost to airlines at about $11B in 2025, about $4.2B of it excess maintenance cost. Verify.
    - Reading: the cost sits in **physical scarcity** (engine shop slots, castings and forgings, LLP availability), not in finding the part.

### Fraud and trust: why brokers and paperwork matter
12. **[M] AOG Technics (UK, 2023)**:
    - a small London broker supplied thousands of CFM56 (and some CF6/V2500) parts with forged EASA Form 1 / release certificates
    - the parts reached airline engines through normal broker channels; [M] reports put it at roughly 100+ engines across carriers including some US and European airlines
    - CFM/Safran and the airlines sued; the UK SFO prosecuted the founder (Jose Zamora Yrala) [M, outcome to verify]
    - Reading: the trust layer is the binding constraint, not search. Cheaper, AI-enabled sourcing that does not also lower the cost of *verifying provenance* makes fraud easier. That supports C6 strongly: inspection and traceability are the scarce layer.

### Contradicting evidence summary
- Search has been digital and cheap for decades: ILS reports 180K searches a day and PartsBase 90M+ listings. So the diagnose-to-source chain is *not* mainly slowed by "not knowing where the part is".
- The binding constraints are certification (PMA/STC/DER), data rights (GAO F-35), physical capacity (IATA) and provenance trust (AOG Technics). AI does not remove any of them.
- Price opacity is real: RFQ-based quoting, sole-source markups and broker layering. But it is preserved by regulation and IP, not just by a knowledge gap.

## Stances
- C1: mixed. The chain fits, but aviation adds explicit steps the founder skips or understates: *trace/paperwork verification* at sourcing, and *return-to-service sign-off by a certificated person* (14 CFR 43). Sourcing is iterative (RFQ loops), not one linear step.
- C2: mixed. A knowledge or data monopoly exists, but its form is IP plus certification (sole-source TransDigm, F-35 data rights), not owners' ignorance.
- C3: contradicts. AI cannot provide legal data rights, an FAA design approval, or a trustworthy trace.
- C4: contradicts / mixed. Search friction can fall a lot. But marketplaces already made search cheap, and the cost sits in scarcity, sole-source price and certification. A 10x drop is not supported.
- C5: contradicts for certificated parts. The PMA/DER route needs per-part ACO plus MIDO approval; HEICO shows it works but slowly, one part at a time.
- C6: supports strongly (AOG Technics, 8130-3 trust layer).
- C7: mixed. Inefficiency is partly *designed in* (sole-source IP, the OEM move into distribution), so it is "built for rent extraction", but much of it is safety regulation working as intended.

## Company-shape ideas
- Provenance and trace verification (document forensics on 8130-3/Form 1, back-to-birth graph, cross-broker duplicate detection) sold to airlines and MROs. This attacks the real bottleneck.
- An AI agent that runs RFQs across ILS/PartsBase/Locatory, normalises condition, trace and price, and builds a price index. Note the incumbents are already adding AI, so the moat is thin.
- A PMA/DER acceleration platform: AI-assisted reverse engineering plus a compliance-package generator to cut the per-part approval cost, which changes PMA economics for low-volume and obsolete parts.
- Defence: tools to manage technical data rights and sustainment (DoD's right-to-repair push).
