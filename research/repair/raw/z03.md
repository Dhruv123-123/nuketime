# z03: Dealer and authorised service networks economics (car dealers earn most profit from service and parts; ag and construction dealers): why do networks persist and what share of OEM profit flows through them?

Research date: 2026-10-20. Method note: the shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn was exhausted before this agent ran (0 searches succeeded), so all findings below come from direct fetches of primary documents: SEC EDGAR 10-K filings (FY2025), the NADA Data 2025 annual report PDF, the FTC "Nixing the Fix" report (2021), the FTC v. Deere complaint (Jan 2025), and the Lafontaine & Scott Morton JEP abstract. Figures marked [unverified] are from prior knowledge and could not be re-checked this session.

## Hard data: dealer side (where the profit sits)

### F1. AutoNation (largest US franchised auto dealer), FY2025 10-K
- Parts and service = 17.5% of revenue but 47.6% of gross profit (46.2% in 2024, 41.7% in 2023).
- New vehicles = 48.9% of revenue but only 13.4% of gross profit (20.7% in 2023).
- Finance and insurance = 5.3% of revenue, 29.6% of gross profit.
- Parts and service: revenue $4,835.4M, gross profit $2,355.1M, so about 48.7% gross margin. P&S gross profit +7% in 2025 while new-vehicle GP fell 14%.
- 10-K language: "Our higher-margin parts and service business has historically been less sensitive to macroeconomic conditions."
- Source: AutoNation 10-K FY2025, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/350698/000162828026007800/an-20251231.htm

### F2. Lithia Motors FY2025 10-K
- Aftersales revenue $4,086.8M of $37,634.9M total (about 10.9%); aftersales gross margin 57.7% (vs new vehicle 6.3%, used 5.5%).
- Implied aftersales gross profit is about $2.36B of about $5.72B total GP, roughly 41%.
- About 25% of aftersales revenue is manufacturer-paid warranty or OEM-sponsored maintenance, and Lithia flags the risk that OEMs cut warranty labor rates or parts markup. So the OEM partly funds the dealer's service P&L directly.
- Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1023128/000102312826000015/lad-20251231.htm

### F3. NADA Data 2025 (industry-wide, all US franchised light-vehicle dealers)
- Service and parts sales: $164.6B in 2025 (up from $116.5B in 2018), from 276.1M repair orders.
- Service and parts = about 13.2 to 13.3% of total dealership sales dollars (new about 55%, used about 32%).
- Per dealership: $9.69M service and parts sales, 16,252 ROs, 16 technicians; 278,424 technicians nationwide; parts inventory $9.7B.
- Average customer mechanical labor rate: $186/hour. Sales per customer RO $494; per warranty RO $551.
- Labor sales $75.81B: customer mechanical $31.48B, warranty $15.46B, internal $12.22B. Parts $93.42B: wholesale $24.54B (dealers selling OEM parts to independent shops), customer mechanical $24.21B, warranty $16.67B.
- Warranty (OEM-paid) labor plus parts is about $32B, roughly 19% of dealer fixed-ops sales.
- The public NADA Data report no longer prints the gross-profit split. The widely cited NADA figure that service and parts generate roughly 45 to 50% of dealership gross profit [unverified this session] is consistent with AutoNation's 47.6% and Lithia's about 41%.
- Source: NADA Data 2025, https://www.nada.org/nadadata (PDF https://www.nada.org/media/4695/download?inline)

### F4. Rush Enterprises (largest US commercial truck dealer; Peterbilt, International etc.), FY2025 10-K
- Aftermarket Products and Services: $2,523.0M = 33.9% of revenue but 63.7% of gross profit.
- Absorption ratio was 130.7% in 2025 (132.2% in 2024). Absorption is aftermarket gross profit divided by dealership overhead. Above 100% means the parts and service business covers all fixed costs, so every new-truck sale is pure incremental profit.
- Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1012019/000143774926005424/rusha20251231_10k.htm

### F5. Titan Machinery (largest CNH ag and construction dealer group), FY ending Jan 2026 10-K
- Revenue mix: equipment 73.1%, parts 17.6%, service 7.3%, rental 2.0%.
- Gross margins: equipment 7.3%, parts 30.9%, service 61.5%.
- Gross profit mix: equipment 33.8%, parts 34.6%, service 28.6%. So parts plus service (about 25% of revenue) produce about 63% of gross profit.
- Titan still made an operating loss (-0.3%) in a down ag cycle. Ag dealers are thin-margin businesses even with aftermarket income.
- CNH supplied about 74% of Titan's new ag equipment. Dealer agreements give CNH change-of-control vetoes, which shows how tightly OEMs control their dealers.
- Uses the "absorption" metric, defined as gross profit from parts, service and rental divided by operating expenses.
- Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1409171/000162828026022376/titn-20260131.htm

## Hard data: OEM side (what share of OEM profit flows through the network)

### F6. PACCAR (Kenworth, Peterbilt, DAF) FY2025 10-K. This is the cleanest OEM disclosure found
- Parts segment: $6,873.7M net sales (24% of revenue) and $1,668.0M pretax income.
- Truck segment: about 68% of revenue and only $870.8M pretax income (down from $2,852.6M in 2024).
- So in 2025, parts made about 66% of PACCAR's Truck plus Parts pretax profit on about a quarter of revenue. Parts pretax margin is about 24%, against about 4.5% for trucks.
- Parts profit was nearly flat across the cycle ($1,704.5M in 2024, $1,668.0M in 2025) while truck profit fell 70%. Aftermarket is the OEM's stabiliser.
- "Substantially all trucks are sold to independent dealers." Parts reach those dealers through 21 PACCAR parts distribution centres. Parts sales depend on "the total number of the Company's trucks in service and the average age and mileage."
- Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/75362/000119312526057025/pcar-20251231.htm

### F7. Caterpillar FY2025 10-K
- 41 US and 109 non-US independent dealers serving 190 countries. Cat owns the Japanese dealer directly (Nippon Caterpillar, about 80% of Japan).
- Dealer agreements grant a territory to "purchase and sell its products and to service the products." Each dealer must employ adequate support personnel and carry out Cat's product-improvement programs.
- Cat defines its "Services" revenue to include aftermarket parts and service-related revenue. It expects services revenues to grow in 2026.
- Services revenue was about $24B in 2024, against an investor-day target of $28B by 2026 [unverified this session]. That is about 35 to 40% of machinery, power and energy sales.
- Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/18230/000001823026000008/cat-20251231.htm

### F8. Deere FY2025 10-K and FTC v. Deere complaint
- About 2,050 independent dealer locations in the US and Canada (about 1,600 ag, 450 construction and forestry).
- Deere supplies parts, "many of which are proprietary," and sells precision tech as aftermarket retrofits through dealers.
- Deere does not separately disclose parts profit. The FTC complaint says PPA had $26.8B sales and $7.0B operating profit in FY2023, and that "Deere reaps massive profits from its parts business."
- The complaint says dealer "parts capture" is very high (exact share redacted) against Deere's much lower overall share of ag parts (also redacted). Owners and independent repairers use far fewer Deere parts. The dealer's parts margins are redacted but described as "highly lucrative and reliable."
- Dealer labor in 2024 was $130 to $200/hour, with field rates higher than shop rates, and most repair happens within 30 to 60 miles of the dealer.
- The mechanism is tool access, not knowledge. The fully functional Service ADVISOR tool is dealer-only, so Deere holds an alleged "100%" share of repairs that need it. Restricted repairs pull all other repairs to the dealer through one-stop-shop effects (complaint para 111). Owners "often are unable to obtain timely dealer service" (para 112). Deere benefits because dealers use more Deere parts (para 113).
- The dealer agreement requires dealers to "actively and aggressively promote the sale of Parts and Service" and to hold parts inventory. State laws bar Deere from requiring exclusive parts purchasing.
- Status per the 10-K (Dec 2025): an MDL class action is pending in the Northern District of Illinois. Deere moved to dismiss the FTC suit and is "in preliminary discussions with the FTC with respect to a potential resolution." The FTC vote to sue was 3-2, with Ferguson and Holyoak dissenting.
- Sources: Deere 10-K https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/315189/000110465925122321/de-20251102x10k.htm ; FTC press release https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-states-sue-deere-company-protect-farmers-unfair-corporate-tactics-high-repair-costs ; complaint https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/DeereCoREDACTEDComplaintCaseNo325-cv-50017.pdf

## Why networks persist: regulator, academic and legal evidence

### F9. FTC "Nixing the Fix" report to Congress (May 2021)
- The FTC lists the restriction tools: product designs that complicate repair, unavailable parts and information, policies steering customers to OEM repair networks, patents and trademarks, disparaging non-OEM parts, software locks, and EULAs.
- Finding: "there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions" (safety, IP, cybersecurity, liability).
- It says the auto industry "has taken important steps to expand consumer choice" (the 2014 R2R MOU), while other industries have not followed.
- It describes three aftermarket models: OEM plus affiliates as the only authorised channel; no OEM presence, so independents serve; and OEM competing with independents while steering work.
- Two commissioners noted the report excludes IP analysis, which "may provide legitimate justification for some repair restrictions."
- Magnuson-Moss anti-tying (1975) already bars conditioning warranty on dealer service. Even so, U.S. PIRG found 45 of 50 warranties appeared to violate it (2018).
- Kodak (1992) established that a firm can hold aftermarket monopoly power over its own parts and service.
- Source: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf

### F10. Lafontaine and Scott Morton, "State Franchise Laws, Dealer Terminations, and the Auto Crisis," Journal of Economic Perspectives 24(3), 2010
- Auto dealers pay a large share of state sales tax, so they have strong influence over legislatures. The resulting state franchise laws protect dealer survival and profitability: termination limits, relevant-market-area protests and direct-sales bans.
- The authors argue these laws raise distribution costs and retail prices and shift profit from OEMs to dealers.
- Written as GM and Chrysler moved to terminate about 2,200 dealers.
- Source: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.24.3.233

### F11. Franchise law structure (secondary, Wikipedia summary; verify before citing)
- Most US states ban OEM direct sales and limit dealer termination. Many require OEM buyback of inventory and tools.
- Tesla works around these laws with galleries and online orders.
- The summary says US dealer profit comes "mainly from servicing," with little from new cars.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile_dealership

## Synthesis and reasoning

1. **Profit share.** On the dealer side, aftermarket (parts plus service) is about 10 to 35% of revenue but about 40 to 64% of gross profit across autos (AutoNation 48%, Lithia about 41%), heavy trucks (Rush 64%) and ag/construction (Titan 63%). On the OEM side, the best clean number is PACCAR, where about two-thirds of 2025 truck-and-parts pretax profit came from parts sold through independent dealers. Caterpillar's services are about a third or more of sales [unverified]. Deere does not disclose, but the FTC says parts are "massive" profit and dealer parts capture is near-total. The shape is consistent everywhere: equipment is sold near cost, and the installed base is monetised through the network for 10 to 20+ years.
2. **Why networks persist.** The evidence points to several reinforcing reasons, and knowledge is only one of them:
   - Law: state franchise statutes and dealer political power (F10, F11).
   - Contracts: dealer agreements require promoting parts and service, holding inventory and accepting OEM vetoes (F5, F7, F8).
   - Tool and software gating: dealer-only diagnostics and parameterisation (F8, F9).
   - Warranty flows: OEMs pay dealers about $32B a year for warranty work in US autos (F3), and warranty work must run through authorised channels.
   - Capital and physical presence: parts inventory, about 16 technicians per store, field-service trucks, and a 30 to 60 mile service radius (F3, F8).
   - Counter-cyclical economics: aftermarket "absorption" covers fixed costs, so the network survives downturns that kill equipment margins (F4, F6).
3. **Implication for the hypotheses.** The inefficiency is real but deliberate and profitable. It is a business model built on the installed base, protected by law, contract and software locks, not by know-how alone. AI that removes the information gap does not by itself remove tool authorisation, warranty tying, parts distribution exclusivity, or franchise statutes. It also does not remove the physical needs: technicians, parts inventory and a field-service radius. Any startup that cuts diagnosis and sourcing costs threatens the profit pool (40 to 65% of dealer gross, up to about two-thirds of some OEMs' profit). Expect strong resistance through software locks and legal fights, as in Deere and the Massachusetts telematics law. Alternatively, sell the efficiency to the dealers and OEMs themselves: they have to keep absorption above 100% with a structural technician shortage (278k techs, $186/hour labor).

## Stance notes
- C1 (universal chain): not directly addressed. Dealer data does show that "warranty" and "internal" repair orders sit outside the owner-driven chain, so the trigger and the payer vary.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. The monopoly is real (Deere's dealer-only tool, the FTC findings), but it is enforced through software, tool access, contracts and law more than through knowledge itself. Parts capture is the money.
- C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicted in this domain. The bottleneck is authorisation, keys and parts distribution, which AI does not grant.
- C4 (an order-of-magnitude cost cut): mixed or contradicted. Labor ($186/hour) and parts ($93B) dominate dealer fixed-ops revenue. Diagnosis and "understanding" are bundled into labor hours. Parts margins of about 30% (Titan) and about 24% pretax (PACCAR) are rents that a rival could compress, but not by 10x on physical goods.
- C5 (AI parts factory): silent to weakly contradicting. Dealer parts capture and OEM proprietary parts mean the barrier is IP and trademark law plus warranty, not manufacturability.
- C6 (inspection persists): mixed. Warranty and authorised-repair requirements act as a de facto certification layer that OEMs use to keep work in the network.
- C7 (structurally inefficient): supports, with nuance. It is structurally built to extract installed-base rent, which is economically rational for OEMs and dealers, and it is protected by franchise law.
