### Certification structure
2. **[fetched] Wikipedia, Nondestructive testing**: the US and global aerospace use employer-based certification. In general industry the employer's "Written Practice" follows ASNT SNT-TC-1A (and ANSI/ASNT CP-189). In aerospace and defense it follows NAS 410, the basis of EN 4179. The EU and Canada use central certification (ISO 9712; ASNT ACCP/CP-106). There are three levels: L1 performs tests, L2 sets up, interprets and evaluates, L3 writes procedures and approves techniques. Key point: the **person** is certified to a method, not the tool. An AI system has no slot in this structure unless a Level 3 qualifies it as part of a procedure. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondestructive_testing
3. **[fetched] Wikipedia NDT / MIL-HDBK-1823A / ASTM E2862**: reliability is accepted through probability-of-detection (POD) studies, usually a90/95 (the flaw size found 90% of the time with 95% confidence). This is how any new method, AI included, would have to earn acceptance in aerospace. It needs statistical demonstration on flawed specimens, which is costly and slow per part and flaw type. Same URL as finding 2.
4. **[fetched] ASNT homepage and methods page**: ASNT says it is "modernizing employer-based NDT qualification under SNT-TC-1A". Its NDE 4.0 conference covers "AI, robotics, IIoT and digital transformation reshaping inspection". A 2026 recap mentions "an AI agent that checks acceptance criteria", and ASNT runs an "AI Assistant: Anita". These pages publish no workforce totals or shortage statistics. Evidence for C6: the certification body is itself adjusting to AI. URLs: https://www.asnt.org/ ; https://www.asnt.org/what-is-nondestructive-testing/methods
5. **[background, not re-verified] NAS 410 hour requirements**: NAS 410 sets formal classroom hours plus documented experience hours per method and level. Level 2 in UT or ET typically needs on the order of 1,000+ hours of experience, and reaching Level 3 takes years. This experience requirement, not the knowledge content, is what makes inspectors slow to train. AI can speed up the classroom part. It cannot shortcut the experience-hour rule unless the standard changes.

### Inspection as the binding constraint (aviation evidence)
6. **[fetched] Southwest 1380 (NTSB, via Wikipedia)**: the blade was inspected by fluorescent penetrant at its 2012 overhaul, and the crack was "most likely not detectable" by that method. After the event, emergency AD 2018-09-51 required ultrasonic inspection of CFM56-7B fan blades with 30,000+ cycles within 20 days. AD 2018-09-10 followed with repeat inspections every 3,000 cycles, and eddy current was added at overhaul. Lessons: (a) the method's physics limits what any inspector, human or AI, can see; (b) regulators react to failures by adding inspections, so the inspection burden only ratchets up. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Airlines_Flight_1380
7. **[fetched] Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal recall (via Wikipedia PW1000G)**: the recall covered 1,200 engines in July 2023 and expanded to all ~3,000 PW1100G engines in September 2023. About 350 aircraft were expected to be grounded on average through 2026. Cost is $6-7B, about 80% of it customer compensation. Inspections or shop visits take 250-300 days. This is a defect found by inspection, where the bottleneck is shop capacity, parts (HPT discs) and turnaround, not knowledge of what is wrong. Everyone knew the diagnosis. Strong evidence against C2/C3 as the main cause of cost in aerospace. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_%26_Whitney_PW1000G
8. **[background, not re-verified] GE Aerospace AI blade inspection tool (2024-2025)**: GE introduced an AI-assisted on-wing or shop tool for LEAP/CFM56 HPT blade borescope imaging. It standardises image capture and uses AI to flag distress, with claims of large time cuts per inspection. A human inspector still makes the serviceability call against the engine manual limits. I could not fetch the press release (404).
9. **[background, not re-verified] Rolls-Royce IntelligentEngine (2018)**: Rolls-Royce showed concepts for "SWARM" micro-robots and snake borescope robots for on-wing inspection. These were research, not certified practice, eight years later.

### AI vendors and drones
10. **[fetched] Waygate Technologies (Baker Hughes)**: lists "assisted defect recognition" for borescopes, "X|approver: ADR for X-ray and CT", FLASH! image processing, and the InspectionWorks platform. The marketing gives no accuracy metrics, and the framing is "assisted", which keeps a human in the loop. The incumbent largest NDT OEM has already put AI into its products, so AI inspection does not belong to startups alone. URL: https://www.bakerhughes.com/waygate-technologies
11. **[fetched] Skydio (via Wikipedia)**: revenue was about $100M in 2023. Over 50% of business was military as of Nov 2024. The company says its drones are used by "over half of US state DOTs", a company claim. It raised a $230M Series E in 2023 at about $2.2B valuation, plus a $170M extension in Nov 2024. Drone inspection is commercially real, but its revenue is small next to the inspection labour market and depends heavily on defence. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skydio
12. **[background, partially attempted] FAA Part 108 BVLOS NPRM** (Federal Register, Aug 7 2025, doc 2025-14992): it proposes routine beyond-visual-line-of-sight operations without waivers, and lists utility, pipeline and infrastructure inspection among target uses. My fetch of the final status was blocked by a CAPTCHA. Until a final rule is in force, scaled drone inspection runs on waivers. That makes a regulator, not AI capability, the gate.
13. **[background, not re-verified] Gecko Robotics**: makes wall-climbing UT robots for boilers, tanks, ships and the US Navy and Air Force. It became a unicorn in 2025 (Series D, about $1.25B valuation). The model is robot data capture plus software, with Level II/III inspectors still signing off. This is the best example of an "inspection rebuilt for AI" company in energy and defence.

### Workforce shortage
14. **[background, not re-verified]**: industry associations and service firms (ASNT, Mistras, Team Inc. commentary) have long said the NDT workforce is aging and that Level II/III technicians are scarce, especially in oil, gas and power turnaround seasons. I could not fetch a hard number this session, so treat the shortage as widely asserted but unquantified here.

## Stance reasoning
- C1: Inspection shows up in the chain twice: as the "notice" step (scheduled NDT finds the defect) and as the closing release step. In aviation the trigger is often a scheduled or mandated inspection (an AD), not someone noticing a fault. So the chain holds roughly, but it is a loop, and the first and last steps are often the same activity.
- C2: Mixed. NDT knowledge is gated by certification and by experience hours, a credential gate more than a hidden-knowledge one. OEMs hold the inspection limits in engine manuals, which is a real knowledge monopoly. But the GTF case shows the cost driven by capacity and parts, not knowledge.
- C3: Contradicted for inspection. AI can flag indications, but acceptance requires POD demonstration and a certified human signature. Physics limits also apply: no amount of AI could have made penetrant testing find the 1380 crack.
- C4: Mixed. AI-assisted borescope and drone capture cut time per inspection. But the dominant cost and time sit in shop turnaround and parts (250-300 days at GTF shops), so cheaper diagnosis does not give 10x end to end.
- C5: Silent or slightly contradicting. A part made bespoke would need its own inspection and qualification (CT, POD, material certs), which keeps the inspection burden.
- C6: Strongly supported. Certification is person-centric, and ASNT is modernising SNT-TC-1A and running NDE 4.0 and AI work. Regulators gate drones through Part 108, and AI acceptance runs through POD.
- C7: Mixed. The structure looks inefficient, but it was built conservatively on purpose: inspections are added after each accident (1380). "Built for safety and liability", not "built to be inefficient".
