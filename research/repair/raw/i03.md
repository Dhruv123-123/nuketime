## Findings

### F1. EASA AI Roadmap: versions and a human-centric framing [verified]
- Roadmap 1.0 came out on 7 Feb 2020 and Roadmap 2.0 on 10 May 2023. Both carry the subtitle "A human-centric approach to AI in aviation." EASA calls it "a living document, which will be amended once a year."
- Source: EASA, AI programme page, https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/research-innovation/ai
- [BK] The roadmap sets three levels. Level 1 is assistance to the human (1A augmentation, 1B cognitive assistance). Level 2 is human-AI teaming (2A cooperation, 2B collaboration). Level 3 is advanced automation (3A, 3B with no human oversight). Approximate targets: first Level 1 approvals around 2025, Level 2 around 2025-2030, Level 3 from about 2035 onward. The roadmap covers maintenance (Part-145/CAMO), along with ATM, flight ops and training, as an application domain.

### F2. EASA concept paper: guidance only, for Level 1 and 2 ML [verified + BK]
- Concept Paper Issue 2 (6 Mar 2024) gives guidance for Level 1 and 2 machine-learning applications. A new issue was released for comment on 3 Jun 2026.
- The MLEAP research project (on ML application approval) published its final report on 31 May 2024.
- Source: https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/research-innovation/ai
- [BK] The concept paper's core idea is "learning assurance", a W-shaped process that replaces the DO-178C V-model for data-driven parts. It is built around data quality, generalisation bounds and explainability. It applies only to **offline-trained (frozen) models**. Adaptive, in-service learning is out of scope.

### F3. EASA NPA 2025-07 (DS.AI): the first binding-track AI trustworthiness specs [verified]
- Published 10 Nov 2025. The comment period was extended to 10 Mar 2026. The rulemaking task is RMT.0742 (ToR 11 Sep 2024).
- It responds to EU AI Act (Reg. 2024/1689) Chapter III Section 2, the high-risk system requirements. Under Article 108, EASA implements those requirements for aviation instead of the generic AI Act route.
- The stated aim is to "maintain the current level of safety" while bringing innovation and efficiency benefits.
- Source: https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/document-library/notices-of-proposed-amendment/npa-2025-07
- Implication: as of Oct 2026, the EU has proposed binding AI rules for aviation but has **not adopted** them. Any AI that makes or supports a maintenance decision in the EU faces a high-risk-AI compliance layer on top of Part-145.

### F4. FAA Roadmap for AI Safety Assurance (v1, 2024) [BK, the page returned 403]
- Issued about July-Aug 2024. Its principles: work within the existing aviation safety ecosystem and regulations, do not create a separate AI regime, separate "learned AI" (frozen) from "learning AI" (adapts in service), focus first on learned AI, avoid personifying AI, and proceed incrementally using industry consensus standards (SAE G-34/EUROCAE WG-114, ARP6983).
- Source: https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/air_cert/step/roadmap_for_AI_safety_assurance (403 today)
- Reading: the FAA treats AI as a tool inside the existing certificated-person accountability chain. It does not treat AI as a new kind of certified actor.

### F5. US maintenance legal structure: a human signs the return to service [BK, regulation]
- Under 14 CFR 43.9 and 43.11, maintenance records need the signature and certificate number of the person approving return to service. Under 14 CFR 43.13, work must use methods "acceptable to the Administrator". Under 14 CFR 65.95, Inspection Authorization holders sign annual and major-repair inspections.
- Electronic records and signatures have been accepted for years (FAA AC 120-78A, 2016). Digital evidence is therefore not the barrier. **Who signs** is the barrier.
- Any AI diagnosis or inspection output enters the system as "data" or a "tool" that a certificated mechanic or repair station relies on. Liability does not move to the AI vendor.

### F6. Approved and accepted AI or automated inspection in aviation [BK, needs re-verification]
- **Donecle** (France): autonomous drones that inspect aircraft skin for lightning strikes and markings. Airbus is reported to have accepted Donecle drone inspection as an approved method in its maintenance documentation for lightning-strike inspection, around 2020-2021. Users have included Air France Industries, Sabena technics and others. Claimed: an inspection that took hours manually takes well under an hour (vendor claim).
- **Airbus "Aircam"** (launched 2018): drone general visual inspection of fuselage and wings. Airbus claimed the inspection time drops from hours to about 2-3 h total, image capture to report (vendor claim).
- **Mainblades** (NL): drone inspection used by KLM E&M and Lufthansa Technik; positioned for lightning strike / GVI support.
- **GE Aerospace** (2023-2025): AI-assisted borescope blade inspection tools used in shops on CFM LEAP/GE engines. Rolls-Royce has a similar IntelligentEngine/borescope analytics programme. These are aids to the inspector. They are not autonomous dispositions.
- Pattern: approval comes through the **OEM's maintenance data (SRM/AMM/ESM) accepting a new method**, not through a regulator certifying the AI. The OEM controls whether AI evidence counts. That matches the "knowledge monopoly" in C2, which reappears as a *data-approval monopoly*.

### F7. NRC: AI used for internal efficiency, licensee AI largely ahead of the rules [verified]
- The NRC FY26 AI Strategic Plan (29 Sep 2025, ML25269A196) was issued under EO 14179 by CIO/CAIO Scott Flanders. The use cases it names are internal: reviewing power uprate and license renewal applications, Nth-of-a-kind advanced reactor reviews, rulemaking support, environmental reviews, "inspection preparation", "inspection planning" and "inspection cross-qualification", meeting summaries and first drafts.
- Source: https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2526/ML25269A196.pdf ; https://www.nrc.gov/ai.html
- The NRC AI page mentions "AI-enabled plant monitoring systems" only in general terms, as part of the evolving landscape. It names **no approved licensee AI for safety-related inspection or maintenance decisions**. It records a public workshop on 15 Sep 2026 on AI across the nuclear sector.
- [BK] The earlier NRC AI Strategic Plan FY2023-2027 (NUREG-2261, May 2023) set notional AI autonomy levels from 0 (no AI) to 4 (fully autonomous). A 2024-2025 regulatory-gap assessment found the existing framework mostly workable but lacking AI-specific guidance. The NRC, CNSC (Canada) and ONR (UK) published trilateral AI principles in Sep 2024. Licensee use is concentrated in non-safety predictive maintenance and monitoring (for example EPRI-led ML for ultrasonic and eddy-current data screening). Safety-related UT must still pass ASME Section XI Appendix VIII performance demonstration, and an AI analyst would need the same qualification as procedure, equipment and personnel.

### F8. FDA: the most mature "approved AI" regime, and the counter-model [BK, the page returned 401]
- FDA's AI-enabled device list passed about 1,000 authorised devices in 2024 and about 1,250 by mid-2025. Roughly three-quarters are radiology. About 97% were cleared via 510(k) (substantial equivalence to a predicate), with the rest via De Novo or PMA.
- Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) guidance was finalised in Dec 2024. It lets a manufacturer pre-authorise how a model will be retrained or updated without a new submission. This is the regulator-sanctioned path for "learning" AI.
- Source: https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-devices (401 today)
- Relevance: FDA shows that AI inspection and diagnosis can be approved at scale (more than 1,000 products), but only as **device-level clearance with a human clinician in the loop**, and mostly via predicate equivalence. FDA is the closest analogue to "AI diagnosis accepted by a safety regulator."

### F9. Other digital-evidence precedents [BK]
- **Remote inspection**: during COVID, FAA and EASA allowed remote/virtual witnessing for some conformity and production inspections. FAA later formalised remote virtual inspection policy for certification activities. Digital video evidence is accepted for *witnessing*. It does not replace the signatory.
- **Pipelines (PHMSA)**: in-line inspection (ILI) data, increasingly analysed by ML vendor software, is accepted for integrity management under API 1163 / ASNT ILI-PQ qualification of tool and analyst. Again, the evidence is accepted once the *process* is qualified.
- **Rail (FRA)**: automated track geometry and ultrasonic rail inspection have been accepted for decades. FRA granted waivers (2018-2023) for automated track inspection to replace some visual inspections. The waivers were contested by unions and partly not renewed, which shows that acceptance can be political as well as technical.

## Synthesis: what the evidence says
1. **No major safety regulator lets AI sign off or return an asset to service.** FAA, EASA, NRC and FDA all keep a certificated human or organisation accountable. AI enters as a tool, aid or data.
2. **Acceptance runs through process qualification, not knowledge.** The binding constraint is qualification evidence (learning assurance, performance demonstration, predicate equivalence, OEM acceptance in maintenance data), plus the liability of whoever signs. Cheaper knowledge does not remove that constraint.
3. **Timelines are long.** EASA Level 3 autonomy is about 2035+. Binding EU aviation AI specs were still at the NPA stage in 2026. The NRC's 2025 plan is about its own internal productivity. FDA took about 10 years to reach about 1,000 devices.
4. **OEM maintenance data is the gatekeeper in aviation.** A drone or AI method becomes "approved" when the OEM writes it into the SRM/AMM. That supports C2's monopoly framing, but the monopoly is over *approved data and liability*, not raw know-how. AI does not dissolve it on its own (contradicts C3).
5. **Inspection is the most AI-ready stage.** Drones, borescope AI, ILI and radiology AI are the documented examples. That supports C6 strongly: inspection is where regulated AI is actually landing, and the certification of AI inspection tools is itself a growing industry.

## Company-shape notes
- A "qualification-evidence engine" that produces the learning-assurance / DS.AI / Appendix VIII / PCCP evidence packages for AI inspection tools (regulatory-grade MLOps).
- AI-assisted inspection that ships *inside OEM-accepted procedures*: partner with an OEM or MRO and get written into the SRM. Donecle and Mainblades show the path and how slow it is.
- A digital maintenance record and evidence chain (tamper-evident sensor, image and AI provenance attached to the signatory's sign-off). Electronic records are already legal (AC 120-78A), so the wedge is the trust and provenance layer.
- In nuclear: AI screening for NDE data (UT/ECT) qualified under PDI, sold to EPRI members. This carries high regulatory moat but slow sales.
