# u01: Technical documentation as a bottleneck: cost and quality of maintenance manuals, S1000D/ATA iSpec 2200, documentation errors as cause of maintenance errors (human factors studies)

## Findings

### F1 (VERIFIED, regulator/court-grade): NTSB AAR-04/01, Air Midwest 5481 (Beech 1900D, Charlotte, 8 Jan 2003; 21 killed)
- URL: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/AAR0401.pdf
- Probable cause: loss of pitch control from incorrect elevator rigging, compounded by an aft CG. The contributing factors explicitly include "(2) Air Midwest's maintenance procedures and documentation" and "(4) the Raytheon Aerospace quality assurance inspector's failure to detect the incorrect rigging."
- The mechanic, with the QA inspector's agreement, bypassed steps c, f, g, h, i, j, (part of) n, s and u of the OEM rigging procedure, and considered them "not required."
- Air Midwest's Director of Maintenance wrote to the NTSB that "a review of the Raytheon Aircraft Beech 1900D Maintenance Manual revealed a lack of explicit instructions, which if included, could have avoided the elevator mis-rigging." The letter listed deficiencies. For example, step d told mechanics to remove floorboards to reach turnbuckles that on the 1900D are actually in the tail. Several steps did not say how to make the adjustments.
- Even after the accident, the revised OEM procedure and revised work card still "do not contain a stand-alone procedure for checking elevator cable tension or for adjusting elevator cable tension without rigging the entire elevator control system."
- The OJT record falsely showed the mechanic had been trained on the D6 task.
- Reading: a fatal accident in which the OEM manual itself was ambiguous and partly wrong for the model, and the workforce responded by skipping steps. The knowledge existed and was in the OEM's hands, but its encoding as a usable procedure was poor. This is a documentation-quality failure, not knowledge being withheld. Inspection (RII/QA) also failed.

### F2 (VERIFIED, regulator): FTC "Nixing the Fix" report to Congress (May 2021)
- URL: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf
- The FTC lists the "Unavailability of Parts, Manuals, and Diagnostic Software/Tools" as a core repair restriction. "some manufacturers do not release service manuals that would be needed to fix otherwise repairable products ... in certain instances, manufacturers threaten to sue or sue entities who publish repair manuals or diagnostic codes." It cites Kyle Wiens (iFixit) on Apple DMCA takedowns of posted service manuals.
- The FTC found that manufacturers had not provided factual support for their claims that authorised repair is safer. It adds: "By not making parts and manuals available ... and not including information in these manuals about the dangers of particular repairs, manufacturers may be exacerbating the very safety concerns they have raised."
- The report says diagnostic software and firmware are "often necessary today to make repairs," and that some OEMs block alternative diagnostic tools.
- Reading: strong regulator support for C2 in consumer and commercial goods. Note that the gap here is withheld or legally protected documentation and software, not missing know-how. An AI model cannot legally or technically recreate diagnostic software keys, firmware pairing, or copyrighted manuals.

### F3 (VERIFIED, regulator/audit): GAO-23-105341, F-35 sustainment
- URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- DOD expects about $1.7T lifecycle cost for about 2,500 F-35s. Of that, about $1.3T is operating and sustainment, mostly maintaining and repairing the aircraft.
- DOD "has not identified or obtained the technical data" needed to support the government/contractor sustainment mix. Organisational-level maintenance is hurt by "a lack of technical data and training." GAO recommends identifying critical technical data needs, their costs and milestones.
- As of March 2026, DOD aimed to transfer the technical data element to the services by end-2028, with intellectual property requirements still unresolved.
- Reading: the clearest institutional case of a documentation and technical-data monopoly. The prime contractor holds the data, and the customer (the US government) cannot do its own maintenance without it. The bottleneck is legal and contractual (IP and data rights), and the money is huge. AI does not remove it, because it is a property-rights problem.

### F4 (VERIFIED, history): BA Flight 5390 (1990), windscreen blown out
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways_Flight_5390 (summary of the AAIB report)
- 84 of 90 bolts were 0.026 in undersized and 6 were too short. The prior windscreen had been fitted "like-for-like" with wrong bolts, "without reference to maintenance documentation," under departure time pressure.
- Reading: the right data existed in the illustrated parts catalogue. The failure was in using it (time pressure, workarounds, no independent check). Better or AI documentation helps only if it is actually consulted at the point of work. Independent inspection is the missing safeguard (supports C6).

### F5 (VERIFIED, history): JAL 123 (1985), the deadliest single-aircraft accident
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Air_Lines_Flight_123
- After a 1978 tailstrike, Boeing's own repair team did the bulkhead repair. They used two splice plates instead of the approved single continuous plate, leaving about 70% of the intended fatigue resistance. JAL's inspection missed it because the overlap hid the defect. The repair failed after 12,318 flights against an estimated life of about 11,000.
- Reading: CONTRADICTS a simple C2. The OEM itself had full knowledge and made the error anyway. The bottleneck was in execution and inspection (inspectability of the repair), not knowledge.

### F6 (VERIFIED, history): Alaska 261 (2000)
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Airlines_Flight_261 (NTSB AAR-02/01 summary)
- Interval extensions for jackscrew lubrication and end-play checks were approved by the FAA. The NTSB said the end-play extension "should have been, but was not, supported by adequate technical data." Home-made restraining fixtures did not meet the OEM spec and gave inaccurate measurements.
- Reading: the documentation and data problem here was about interval decisions and tooling, governed by the regulator-operator relationship. The failure was in the "notice/diagnose" step (wear-measuring inspection) and in the governance of maintenance programmes.

### F7 (VERIFIED, standards): S1000D and ATA iSpec 2200 landscape
- URLs: https://s1000d.org/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S1000D ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATA_100
- S1000D is an international XML spec for technical publications built on a Common Source Database (CSDB) of reusable data modules. It is jointly governed by ASD, AIA and A4A, and is free to download. Issues run from 1.6 (1995) and 2.0 (2003) to Issue 7 (July 2026). It is used in defence (land, sea, air), civil aviation, construction and ships.
- ATA 100 (1956) introduced the universal chapter scheme (e.g., 24 Electrical Power, 35 Oxygen, 52 Doors). iSpec 2200 (2000) consolidated it, with the stated aims of reducing cost and effort and improving data quality and timeliness.
- Reading: aviation already has a mature, structured, machine-readable documentation layer. The spec is free, but implementing it is not: CSDB tooling, authoring and conversion are a services industry. Two partly overlapping standards (iSpec 2200 for commercial airliners, S1000D for military and new programmes) plus many versions create interoperability friction. Structured data like this is exactly what AI retrieval can use, which favours C3 in aviation. But the content is still authored and owned by the OEM.

### F8 (RECALLED, not verified this session): human factors literature on procedures and documentation
- McDonald, Corrigan, Daly & Cromie (2000), "Safety management systems and safety culture in aircraft maintenance organisations," Safety Science 34:151-176 (DOI 10.1016/S0925-7535(00)00012-6; fetch got 403). Widely cited result: about a third (about 34%) of technicians said they did not follow the procedure exactly on their most recent task. Common reasons given were that the procedures were wrong, inefficient or there was a "better way."
- Hobbs & Williamson (2003), "Associations between errors and contributing factors in aircraft maintenance," Human Factors 45(2) (DOI 10.1518/hfes.45.2.186.27244; 403). This analysed about 600+ Australian occurrences. Documentation and procedures were among the factors, and memory lapses were linked to pressure and fatigue more than to documentation.
- Boeing MEDA (Rankin, Aero Q2 2007): "Information" (work cards, maintenance manuals, service bulletins) is one of the most frequently cited contributing-factor categories, alongside environment/facilities and technical knowledge. Boeing has also estimated maintenance as a factor in roughly 15% of accidents. Exact percentages were not re-verified here.
- Drury and colleagues (FAA human factors programme, around 1990s-2000s): many documentation-design studies found task cards poorly laid out and hard to follow. They produced the FAA "Documentation Design Aid," and found that redesigned work cards cut errors (often cited as about a 15-20% improvement in comprehension and error rates in controlled tests).
- Reading: in the human factors literature, documentation is a real but minority contributor. It sits alongside pressure, fatigue, communication, culture and norms of non-compliance. Technicians often deviate because the official document is worse than tribal knowledge. That is a quality problem, not a lack of access.

### F9 (inference/opinion): where AI fits
- Structured S1000D and iSpec data plus LLM retrieval can plausibly cut the time spent finding and understanding a procedure (the "understand" step). But the safety-critical risk in F1, F4 and F5 is a procedure that is subtly wrong, or a step that gets skipped. An LLM that paraphrases or "helpfully" shortens a procedure would reproduce the F1 failure mode at scale.
- In regulated domains (14 CFR 121.369, EASA Part-145), the work must be done to *approved data*. An AI summary is not approved data unless it is certified. So AI changes access and navigation, but the authority that defines what counts as correct stays with OEMs and regulators. This supports C6 and limits C3.

## Stances
- C1 (one chain): mixed. The accident cases fit the chain, but they show it is not linear. Documentation and inspection failures feed back, and "notice" (Alaska 261 wear checks) and inspection (JAL 123, Air Midwest QA) are where the fatal misses happened.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. The FTC (consumer goods) and GAO F-35 (defence) strongly support a *documentation and data access monopoly*. But aviation accident evidence shows errors even with full knowledge (JAL 123 by Boeing itself, BA 5390 where the data was available but not consulted). In airline maintenance the gap is in the *quality and usability* of the knowledge, not who holds it.
- C3 (AI closes gap): contradicts as stated. The gaps are legal (IP, data rights, DMCA, diagnostic software locks), about quality (wrong or ambiguous OEM manuals), and about authority (approved data). AI can help navigation but cannot create legal access, fix wrong source data it doesn't know is wrong, or confer approval.
- C4 (10x cost drop in four steps): silent to mixed. No direct cost numbers were found. The F-35 sustainment total ($1.3T) shows the stakes, but the cost driver there is data rights and the sustainment structure, not a lookup cost.
- C5 (AI parts factory): silent (outside this question). F4 hints that parts identification and parts catalogue discipline matter.
- C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports. In F1, F4 and F5 the independent inspection or second check was the barrier that failed. Approved-data rules make certification the gatekeeper for AI-generated procedures.
- C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed. Withheld documentation (FTC) and contractor-held data (GAO) look like deliberate rent capture. But aviation's documentation stack (ATA 100 since 1956, S1000D) is a deliberate, if slow, standardisation effort aimed at reducing cost. Much of the "inefficiency" is redundant safety process.

## Where the money and time go (this domain)
Documentation is a large fixed cost owned by OEMs (authoring, CSDB, revisions, distribution). Downstream it costs time: finding, interpreting and working around procedures. Its biggest cost is in the tail: rare catastrophic errors when procedures are wrong or skipped. In defence, the real money is lifecycle sustainment ($1.3T for F-35) shaped by who owns the technical data.

## Real bottleneck
Rights and authority over approved technical data, and its quality. Who legally owns and may distribute the manuals, diagnostics and data. Whether the OEM's procedure is actually correct and usable for the specific configuration. And the rule that work must follow *approved* data. Raw knowledge is not the bottleneck.

## Company shapes suggested
1. Tooling for verifying and correcting documentation quality: AI that cross-checks OEM procedures against configuration and parts data and flags F1-type errors. Its output goes into the OEM/operator change process (procedure deficiency reports) rather than replacing approved data.
2. A point-of-work procedure compliance layer: guided step-by-step execution with sign-off evidence (photos, torque values) that makes skipped steps visible, for MROs, defence depots and energy/robotics field service.
3. Technical-data rights and IP infrastructure for defence sustainment: helping governments specify, acquire and use technical data packages (F-35 style), converting legacy data to S1000D.
4. AI-native authoring for new hardware OEMs (robotics, energy storage, drones), producing S1000D-grade structured documentation from CAD and engineering change orders from day one, so documentation cost does not lag behind.
