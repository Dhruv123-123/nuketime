## Synthesis: how a repair/insurance business became a ~$10B company
1. **Distribution capture.** It embedded at point of sale and in carriers' monthly bills, with near-zero customer acquisition cost and attach via opt-out or bundle. Exclusive multi-year carrier contracts (Verizon, AT&T) are the core asset; client concentration is the core risk, as Assurant's 10-K says.
2. **Risk pooling plus actuarial data.** Premiums are priced on claim-frequency data across hundreds of millions of devices. Claim frequency is low (D&G ~12% a year; an OEM's warranty cost is ~1.3% of sales), so a subscription priced well above expected repair cost earns a float and an underwriting margin.
3. **Claims-cost control by owning fulfilment.** Asurion bought uBreakiFix (repair), next-day replacement logistics and refurbishment; Assurant has 20M devices a year through care centres, Hyla and 1,150 repair locations. The profit is in replacing a device for less than the customer values it, then reselling the salvage.
4. **Behavioural pricing.** Warranties cost 20-30% of product price, 75% of buyers don't compare, and there are sales commissions and opaque eligibility (the SquareTrade class action).
5. **Roll-up plus leverage.** M&A of administrators (Lock/line, WaCA, NEW, D&G) and PE dividend recaps.
6. **Thin layer margin.** The public comp shows ~8% EBITDA on ~$9.6B segment revenue. It is a scale, pass-through business; the large equity value comes from durability and leverage, not high margins.

## Analog for machines (energy, robotics, industrial)
- The analog to Asurion is not "AI that knows how to fix it". It is a risk-transfer plus fulfilment network wrapped around a distribution chokepoint. Examples: OEM service contracts and power-by-the-hour (Rolls-Royce TotalCare), equipment breakdown insurance (HSB/Munich Re), and home-system warranties (American Home Shield, D&G, Asurion's appliance move).
- What would work for machines: (a) find the chokepoint where machines are sold or financed (OEM, dealer, lessor, EPC, utility programme); (b) gather failure-frequency data to price uptime risk; (c) own or control the fulfilment layer (parts, swap units, field techs, refurbishment) to cut claims cost; (d) carry an insurance licence or fronting partner.
- AI's real role in this model is to cut claims cost: triage, remote diagnosis, fraud detection, routing, and deciding whether to repair or replace. This raises the margin of the risk-and-fulfilment holder. It does not remove the need for one. The value goes to whoever holds the contract and the channel, not to whoever holds the knowledge.
- Warning from the comps: carriers, OEMs and retailers keep most of the economics, and a lost contract is existential. A new entrant needs either its own channel or a sharply lower claims cost to win the RFP.

## Stance notes
- C1 mixed: the protection-plan workflow (claim, triage, repair or replace, ship, refurbish) fits the chain. But the dominant path is often replacement, not repair, and money comes before the chain starts (premium, distribution).
- C2 mixed: the FTC documents OEM restrictions on parts, tools and information, so knowledge and access asymmetry exists. But in the Asurion model the information asymmetry that pays is between seller and buyer, about contract value and eligibility, not about repair know-how. Asurion's moat is channel, data and logistics.
- C3 contradicts (weakly): Asurion and Assurant are leaning on AI for triage and support, yet their moats (contracts, licences, logistics, balance sheet) are not knowledge, so AI does not dissolve them.
- C4 mixed: AI can cut triage and diagnosis cost in claims. But cost growth is in parts, labour and tariffs (Warranty Week), and in device protection the large costs are the replacement device and the channel's commission, which AI doesn't touch.
- C5 silent: there is no evidence in this domain. Device protection solves an unavailable part by swapping the whole unit or refurbishing, not by fabricating the part.
- C6 mixed/silent: inspection appears as authorized-repair status and OEM certification (uBreakiFix's Samsung and Google status), which gates parts and warranty; there is no evidence of AI-driven change.
- C7 mixed: margins are thin (~8%) and the structure is about channel capture and behavioural pricing, which rewards distribution rather than efficiency. But the fulfilment layer (next-day replacement, 20M devices a year refurbished, same-day in 85% of the US) is quite efficient.
