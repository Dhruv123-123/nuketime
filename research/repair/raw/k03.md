# k03: Is the bottleneck actually capital, inventory and logistics rather than knowledge? Evidence where knowledge is free but repair is still slow.

Date: 2026-10-20. Method note: the shared web-search budget for this workflow run ran out before this agent's first query, so no WebSearch results were available. All findings below come from direct WebFetch of primary sources (GAO, FTC, CCC, Wood Mackenzie, Oliver Wyman, IATA) plus Wikipedia for two well-documented industrial cases. Coverage is thinner than intended: no wind, rail, appliance or Russia-sanctions sources could be pulled. A follow-up run with search budget should add those.

## Framing

The test: find cases where the people doing the repair already have the knowledge (the OEM itself, government depots that own the designs, mandated data access) and see whether repair is still slow. If it is, the binding constraint is somewhere else: parts, capacity, labour, capital, money approval.

## Findings

### 1. Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal recall: the OEM has all the knowledge, yet each shop visit takes 250-300 days
- Source: Wikipedia, Pratt & Whitney PW1000G (citing P&W/RTX disclosures). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_%26_Whitney_PW1000G
- In 2023 contaminated powder metal led to inspections of 1,200 PW1100G engines, then all ~3,000. P&W expected an average of ~350 A320neo aircraft on the ground through 2026. Each inspection or shop visit takes 250-300 days. Cost to P&W and partners: $6-7bn, about 80% of it customer compensation. Go First (India) went bankrupt partly over a lack of spare engines.
- Reading: the diagnosis is known. The failure mode is understood and the OEM is doing the work. The time goes into shop capacity, new high-pressure-turbine disks (forging and powder-metal supply) and spare engines. This is close to a pure case of "knowledge is free, repair is still slow". Hard data (company figures).

### 2. Airline MRO and supply chain: the constraint is production and parts, not know-how
- Source: Oliver Wyman, Global Fleet & MRO Market Forecast 2025-2035. https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2025/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2025-2035.html
- Aircraft production fell from >1,800 (2018) to <1,300 (2024). The backlog is >17,000 jets, about 14 years at current rates. GTF and LEAP inspections run into 2027 and ground hundreds of planes at various times. Average fleet age rose from 12.1 to 13.4 years. MRO market is $119bn in 2025, 12% above the 2019 record. Named causes: supply chain delays, labour shortages and raw material shortages.
- Source: IATA Dec 2024 industry outlook. https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2024-releases/2024-12-10-01/
- Supply chain problems force "capacity discipline". Maintenance costs rose sharply because of groundings and an ageing fleet. The Middle East is constrained by "limited engine availability".
- Reading: the scarce thing is metal, slots and people. Investor/industry view plus data.

### 3. F-35: about 55% mission capable, >10,000 components waiting for repair, depot capacity behind schedule
- Source: GAO-23-105341 (Sept 2023). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- Mission capable rate was ~55% in March 2023. Over 10,000 components were waiting to be repaired. Depot repair capability for complex repairs was behind schedule. Organisational maintenance lacks technical data and training. DOD has not secured the technical data it needs. Sustainment is ~$1.3tn of a ~$1.7tn life-cycle cost.
- Source: GAO-24-106703 (Apr 2024). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106703
- None of the variants meet availability goals. Sustainment estimate rose 44%, from $1.1tn to $1.58tn. Spare-parts management recommendation not implemented. 30 of 43 GAO recommendations (~70%) are unimplemented.
- Reading: MIXED and important. Here the knowledge monopoly (contractor-held technical data) is real and GAO calls it out. But the same reports put depot capacity, component repair throughput, spares and support equipment alongside it. Even the program owner (DOD) lacks data, so this supports C2. Yet the 10,000-component repair queue is a capacity and throughput problem that data alone would not clear.

### 4. Military aircraft fleet-wide: 4 of 49 types met mission-capable goals; causes are ageing, maintenance and parts
- Source: GAO-23-106217 (Nov 2022). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106217
- Only 4 of 49 aircraft types met their annual mission capable goal in most years FY2011-2021, and 26 never met it. FY2021: only 2 met it. Program officials cited ageing aircraft, maintenance challenges, and problems obtaining parts and supplies. O&S cost was ~$54bn in FY2020, and maintenance's share grew.
- Reading: this includes many legacy fleets (KC-135, F-16, C-130, H-60) where the government has had decades of tech data and runs organic depots. The explanations given are parts and ageing, not knowledge. Hard data plus practitioner explanation.

### 5. Attack submarines: 10,363 idle or delay days. Government-owned designs and public shipyards, constrained by capacity, workforce and facilities
- Source: GAO-19-229 (Nov 2018). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-229
- FY2008-2018: 10,363 days of idle time and maintenance delay. More than $1.5bn spent supporting subs that delivered no operational capability. USS Boise sat idle pierside for more than 2 years waiting for a shipyard slot. Causes: public shipyards working above capacity, workforce shortages, facilities, and poor public/private allocation.
- Reading: this is the cleanest "military with full data rights" case. Navy public shipyards have done this work for decades on Navy-owned designs. The bottleneck is dry docks, people and work allocation. Strong contradiction of C2/C3 as the main driver here. Hard data.

### 6. Navy shipyards: >$1.6bn restoration backlog, more than half of equipment past service life, dry-dock costs doubled
- Source: GAO-22-105993 (May 2022 testimony). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105993
- The 20-year, $21bn SIOP plan. Restoration and modernisation backlog grew by >$1.6bn in 5 years. More than half of capital equipment is past expected service life. The 2018 estimate of $4bn for 17 dry docks was overrun by >$4bn on the first three projects alone.
- Reading: the scarce layer is physical capital (docks, cranes, machine tools). It is slow and expensive to add. Hard data.

### 7. Navy intermediate maintenance: 46% of submarine periods late (2,525 days). Causes are parts, growth work and personnel in training. The Navy did not analyse its own data
- Source: GAO-22-104510. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-104510
- 191 of 414 submarine intermediate maintenance periods were late, adding 2,525 days. Spend is ~$2.1bn/yr on high-priority maintenance. Named challenges: parts shortages, growth work (unplanned work found once opened) and personnel in training. The Navy "generally doesn't collect or analyze sufficient data". Lessons learned were not shared across fleets.
- Reading: a mixed picture. Parts and people dominate, but there is also an organisational knowledge failure. The Navy owns the data and does not use it. That is not a monopoly. It is an information-management gap, which AI tools could plausibly help with.

### 8. Navy surface ship maintenance: the biggest delay driver was funding approval (26-189 days), not knowledge or parts
- Source: GAO-20-370 (May 2020). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-370
- The main cause of delay was the approval process when an availability crossed fiscal years and needed >$4m of prior-year money. It took 26 to 189 days. Unquantified "growth work" was the other driver.
- Reading: the binding constraint can be administrative money flow and contracting. That is "capital" in the bureaucratic sense, unrelated to know-how. Hard data.

### 9. Sailor-led repair: 75% of executive officers say it is hard for sailors to complete repairs. Training and manning are the issue, alongside spares
- Source: GAO-24-106525 (Sept 2024). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106525
- Survey of 232 ships, 91% response. 63% say underway repairs are hard given manning. 64% say it is hard with the formal training available. 75% say it is hard for sailors to complete repairs. Persistent spare parts and materials shortages and inaccurate allowance lists are also cited.
- Reading: SUPPORTS C2 partly. Even when the organisation owns all the data, front-line skill and knowledge transfer is a real constraint. AI-guided repair (job aids, diagnostics) would directly target this. Parts and manning remain co-equal constraints, though.

### 10. Defence parts lead times: circuit card assemblies went from 19 to 34 months in two years
- Source: GAO-24-106649 (Apr 2024). https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106649
- One missile program saw electronic-part (circuit card assembly) lead times rise from 19 to 34 months within two years. GAO calls long supplier and raw-material lead times "long-standing" problems across many programs.
- Reading: the supply base, not information, sets the clock. An AI parts factory does not make certified CCAs or castings appear in weeks. Hard data.

### 11. Grid transformers: lead times rose from ~50 weeks (2021) to ~120 weeks (2024). US makers meet only ~20% of demand
- Source: Wood Mackenzie. https://www.woodmac.com/news/opinion/supply-shortages-and-an-inflexible-market-give-rise-to-high-power-transformer-lead-times/
- Average lead time went from ~50 to ~120 weeks. Large substation and GSU units take 80-210 weeks. Prices rose 60-80% since 2020. GOES steel nearly doubled and copper rose ~50%. Domestic makers cover ~20% of US demand. Up to 25% of renewable projects could be delayed.
- Reading: in energy, replacing a failed large transformer is limited by factory capacity and electrical-steel supply. Diagnosis takes hours. Sourcing takes years. Strong support for "capital/inventory/logistics is the bottleneck". It also points to spares pooling and manufacturing capacity as the investable layer. Hard data / analyst.

### 12. Cars: where repair information is broadly available (2014 auto R2R MOU), cycle times are still long. Parts count, calibration and labour drive them
- Source: CCC Intelligent Solutions, Crash Course 2025. https://www.cccis.com/crash-course
- Collision repair days are still 4-5 days above 2020 levels. EV repairs take 37.5 repair-process days. Parts per claim are 13.5 on average (EV 22). Calibrations appear on 56.3% of supplements, at an average fee of ~$547. Labour rates rose 4.5% in 2024 (7.5% in 2023). Parts shortages can turn repairable cars into total losses. Technician shortage "could be contributing".
- Source: FTC, Nixing the Fix (May 2021). https://www.ftc.gov/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions
- Says "the car manufacturing industry has taken important steps to expand consumer choice, other industries ... have not followed suit". Also: "there is scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions". The FTC lists parts unavailability as a restriction on equal footing with manuals and diagnostic software. Example: "manufacturers' control of spare parts may result in consumers' needing to replace appliances ... simply because they cannot obtain a spare part". Volvo limits key parts to authorised networks.
- Reading: autos are the best natural experiment. Information access is largely solved, and repair is still slow because of parts, ADAS calibration and technicians. Note, though, that the FTC frames parts gatekeeping as part of the monopoly. The "knowledge monopoly" in consumer goods is often really a parts-and-software-lock monopoly. Knowledge alone does not unlock it.

### 13. A-10 rewing: the original maker was gone, the government had the data, and it still took 2006 to 2011+ for first new wings. Workforce loss and untrusted data were part of the problem
- Source: Wikipedia, Fairchild Republic A-10 (citing USAF/GAO). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Republic_A-10_Thunderbolt_II
- Cracks were found in 1995-96. A 2003 independent review said "the data on which the wing upgrade relied could no longer be trusted". BRAC led to ~80% of the original maintenance workforce being let go, which "greatly delayed" the plan. The new-wing option chosen in 2006 cost $1.72bn for up to 242 wings. First flights on new wings came in Nov 2011.
- Reading: this is the founder's C5 scenario (supplier gone, so make it bespoke). In practice it took a prime contractor, 5+ years and $1.7bn, because remaking a flight-critical structure requires design re-validation, fatigue testing (a test wing failed early) and qualified production. It also shows knowledge decay inside the owner (tacit workforce knowledge, stale data), which is a nuance for C2.

## Synthesis

- Across the strongest "knowledge is not the constraint" cases (GTF engines, submarines at public yards, transformers, legacy military fleets, cars after data-access deals), repair time is dominated by four things: (a) physical capacity (docks, shop slots, test cells), (b) long-lead certified parts and materials (HPT disks, CCAs, GOES steel), (c) skilled labour, and (d) money and contract approval. Diagnosis is usually a small share of elapsed time.
- Knowledge does show up as a real constraint in three forms:
  1. Contractor-held technical data on new platforms (F-35). This is a genuine monopoly, but legal and contractual, not cognitive.
  2. Front-line skills and training (Navy sailors, the F-35 organisational level).
  3. Organisations not analysing data they already own (Navy IMPs, A-10 data distrust).
  AI plausibly helps with forms 2 and 3. Form 1 is a rights issue: AI cannot read data it is not given.
- In consumer and commercial goods, "knowledge monopoly" is mostly bundled with control of parts and software locks (FTC). Cheap diagnosis does not help if the part is unavailable or serialised.
- So an order-of-magnitude cost drop across diagnose, understand, source and deliver looks implausible in heavy assets. Diagnose and understand are cheap shares. Sourcing and delivery are set by manufacturing capacity and certification, which AI shortens only modestly. In consumer goods, an order-of-magnitude drop in diagnosis cost is plausible, but total repair cost is dominated by labour plus parts.

## Stance notes
- C1: This evidence adds hidden steps, which the chain omits: funding and approval (GAO-20-370), slot scheduling and capacity queueing (GAO-19-229), and "growth work" discovered mid-repair (GAO-22-104510, GAO-20-370). The chain is a useful skeleton but not linear.
- C2: Mixed. Real for F-35 data rights and front-line skills. Weak for subs, transformers, GTF and cars, where the knowledge holder is the repairer and it is still slow.
- C3: Contradicted as stated. The knowledge gaps that remain are legal (data rights), tacit and hands-on (sailors), or depend on data that does not exist. AI does not remove them by itself.
- C4: Contradicted for heavy assets. Sourcing and delivery time is factory and certification bound (19-34 month CCAs, 80-210 week transformers, 250-300 day engine visits).
- C5: Contradicted. The A-10 rewing case shows bespoke replacement of a critical part takes years and billions because of qualification. The CCA and transformer cases show materials and fabs are limits too.
- C6: Supports. Qualification, testing and certification gate every bespoke or replacement part (A-10 fatigue test failure; GTF inspections mandated by regulators).
- C7: Mixed. Inefficiency is real (funding approval delays, fragmented learning, under-invested yards). But much of it is underinvestment in capacity and spares, not bad design of the industry. Some slack, such as spares and redundancy, is deliberate.

## Company-shape implications
- Capacity and inventory layer: pooled rotable and spares exchanges (engines, transformers, CCAs), "inventory-as-a-service" for long-lead critical parts, and distributed certified MRO capacity (mobile test cells, modular dry-dock or shop capacity).
- Throughput software: AI that schedules scarce capacity (docks, shop slots, technicians), predicts growth work before a part is opened so parts are pre-staged, and forecasts spares. This attacks the queue, not the diagnosis.
- Qualification-accelerated replacement parts: reverse engineering plus additive or CNC, paired with a qualification and certification data package. The moat is the certification evidence, not the fabrication.
- Front-line AI job aids for militaries and fleets that already own data (Navy-type), where the gap is skill transfer.
