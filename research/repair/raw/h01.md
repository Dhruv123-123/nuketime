## Findings

### 1. The parts monopoly broke early, through distribution, and long before any information fix
- NAPA (National Automotive Parts Association) was set up in 1925, older than its parent Genuine Parts Company (1928). In 2020 there were about 6,000 NAPA stores, and "most are independently owned and operated". Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genuine_Parts_Company
- **[BACKGROUND - verify]** NAPA was formed by parts makers and jobbers in Detroit to fix fragmented, unreliable parts distribution. The scarce thing in the 1920s was *availability*, not knowledge.
- The FTC (2021) says that in autos, "replacement parts have been generally available outside of manufacturers' repair networks". That is the baseline, and it contrasts with phones and appliances. Source: FTC, Nixing the Fix, p.18, https://www.ftc.gov/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions

### 2. Knowledge was commoditized by third-party publishers, and the publishers did not keep the profits
- Haynes was founded in 1960. In December 2020 it stopped printing new manuals ("all new repair content would be online only"). Infopro Digital bought it for GBP 114.5M in 2020. Haynes publishes Chilton under licence from Cengage, and it bought Clymer from Penton in 2013. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haynes_Publishing
- **[BACKGROUND - verify]** Chilton (from about the 1910s-1930s) and Hearst's MOTOR manuals and flat-rate labor guides (from the 1930s-40s) made shop-level repair knowledge widely available for most of the 20th century. MOTOR's labor-time data became the de facto pricing standard and is now licensed B2B as data.
- **Interpretation:** a GBP 114.5M exit for the world's best-known DIY repair-knowledge brand, against AutoZone at about $19B in revenue, shows that once knowledge is democratized it earns little. Value moved to whoever owned inventory and logistics. This is a strong caution for C3/C4. Removing the knowledge gap has happened before (print manuals, then ALLDATA/Mitchell1 databases), and the money went to parts logistics, not to the knowledge provider.

### 3. Repair databases were absorbed by parts and tool companies as tools to win customers
- AutoZone introduced an electronic parts catalog in 1987 and "Z-net", a parts catalog plus repair database, in 2007. It launched Loan-A-Tool in 1986, which lends specialty tools for free so that DIYers buy parts. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AutoZone
- **[BACKGROUND - verify]** AutoZone bought ALLDATA (repair-information subscriptions for shops) in 1996. Snap-on bought Mitchell Repair Information (Mitchell 1, ProDemand) in 1996. Repair info became a loss-leader or attach product for selling parts and tools to professional shops.
- Mitchell International (collision estimating, founded 1946 as a parts catalog for collision estimating) passed between private-equity owners: H&F in 2000, Aurora for about $500M in 2007, KKR for $1.1B in 2013, Stone Point in 2018. It handles more than 50M transactions a year for 300+ insurers and 30,000 collision shops. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_International
- **Interpretation:** the knowledge layer that makes money is the one built into the *transaction*: estimating that links insurer, shop and parts (Mitchell, CCC), or catalogs that drive parts sales. Selling knowledge on its own makes little.

### 4. Who captured the profits: high-density parts retailers with logistics moats
- AutoZone FY2025 (year to Aug 30, 2025): revenue $18.9B, operating income $3.61B (about 19% margin), net income $2.50B, *negative* equity of -$3.4B (the result of heavy buybacks). About 7,657 stores; the infobox says 8,031 locations by Aug 2026. It built "mega hub" stores from 2015 for same-day availability. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AutoZone
- O'Reilly 2024: revenue $16.7B, operating income $3.25B (about 19.5%), net income $2.39B, equity about -$1.4B. 6,538 locations by Q3 2025. It grew by roll-up: Hi/LO in 1998, Mid-State in 2001, Midwest in 2005, CSK (1,273 stores) in 2008. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O%27Reilly_Auto_Parts
- **[BACKGROUND - verify]** O'Reilly's sales are about half professional (DIFM), and AutoZone's commercial share is about 30% and growing. Gross margins are around 51-53%. The edge is store density and inventory depth (SKU availability within hours), not knowledge.

### 5. The losers: parts distributors that got logistics wrong
- Advance Auto Parts in 2025: revenue $8.6B, an operating *loss* of $43M, net income $44M. In Nov 2024 it closed 523 corporate stores, 204 independent locations and 4 DCs, with restructuring expected to cost $350M-$750M. It sold Worldpac to Carlyle for $1.5B (closed Nov 2024). It became the largest aftermarket parts provider in North America when it bought General Parts/Carquest in 2014, and still fell behind. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_Auto_Parts
- GPC 2025: revenue $24.3B, net income $65.9M as shown in the infobox (a very thin margin, likely depressed by one-off charges; verify). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genuine_Parts_Company
- **Interpretation:** the same knowledge environment produced about 19% operating margins at AZO and ORLY and about 0% at AAP. Execution in supply chain and inventory decides who wins in the aftermarket. Knowledge does not.

### 6. Alternative parts as a profit pool: LKQ
- LKQ was founded in 1998 from a single salvage yard. It made more than 200 acquisitions of recycled, refurbished and aftermarket parts suppliers. 2025 revenue was $13.65B and net income $608M (about 4.5% net margin, my calculation). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LKQ_Corporation
- **Interpretation:** an "alternative source" for parts (salvage, reman, aftermarket copies) became a large business. But it is a thin-margin roll-up, and it depends on OEMs not locking parts with software.

### 7. The information monopoly re-formed around electronics, and legislation broke it, not technology
- Under the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments, cars from 1994 on had to carry on-board emissions computers (**[BACKGROUND - verify]**: OBD-II became universal from MY1996, along with EPA service-information rules). Federal Right to Repair bills were introduced in August 2001 and never passed. NASTF was set up in 2001 as a voluntary alternative. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Vehicle_Owners%27_Right_to_Repair_Act
- A 2005 Tarrance Group survey found that 59% of shops had trouble getting repair information or tools, and 67% had been forced to send vehicles back to dealers. This is the clearest numeric evidence for C2 in the electronic era. (Same source.)
- The FTC received 6,786 auto parts and repair complaints between 1996 and May 2006, and only 2 were relevant. That is evidence that the problem was over-claimed, or at least not visible to consumers. (Same source.) This counts against C2.
- In Massachusetts, a 2012 ballot measure passed with 86%. In 2014 a voluntary national MOU extended the Massachusetts standard to all 50 states. (Same source.)
- Massachusetts 2020 Question 1 (telematics) passed with 74.97%. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation sued. The district court dismissed the suit in Feb 2025, and the First Circuit appeal was still pending as of Aug 2026. In June 2023 NHTSA told OEMs *not* to comply on cybersecurity grounds ("A malicious actor here or abroad could utilize such open access to remotely command vehicles"), then allowed short-range Bluetooth access instead. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Massachusetts_Question_1
- **Interpretation:** each time knowledge was opened up (manuals, OBD, the 2014 MOU), OEMs moved control to a new layer: telematics, cybersecurity gateways, part pairing. The current "knowledge monopoly" is really an *access and authorization* monopoly, held up by safety and cybersecurity regulators. AI cannot read data it is not allowed to access.

### 8. FTC on current auto restrictions (2021)
- Telematics are "currently only accessible by the vehicle manufacturers". LKQ says OEMs can "steer the consumer... at the time when they are most vulnerable, to a dealership". FTC p.~24.
- "VIN burning" ties a control module to a single VIN, and is "reportedly being used by General Motors as well as a number of European luxury brands". Cybersecurity gateways are implemented in ways that block third-party repair. FTC p.~26.
- Volvo and others limit key replacement parts to authorized networks. FTC p.18.
- Software restrictions on reusing or remanufacturing ECMs "limit repair options to new OEM replacement parts only". "Dealer prices for OEM parts are almost always the highest", while reman and salvage modules sell "at a fraction". No comment rebutted the claim that restrictions raise repair prices. FTC p.~40.
- On safety, the FTC says: "The automotive sector demonstrates that consumers and independent repair shops are able to repair cars every day even though cars are a diverse group of complex machines... With appropriate parts, repair information, and training..." It also quotes Vermont Sen. Pearson calling OEM safety arguments "largely bogus".
- Source: FTC, Nixing the Fix (May 2021) PDF, https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/nixing-fix-ftc-report-congress-repair-restrictions/nixing_the_fix_report_final_5521_630pm-508_002.pdf
- **Interpretation:** autos are the FTC's model case of an open repair market, and the open market still kept dealer-only niches. The FTC's own conditions are "parts, information and training". All three are needed, not knowledge alone.

### 9. Magnuson-Moss (1975) as the legal precondition
- The FTC report notes that the MMWA bars an automaker from voiding a warranty because maintenance was done somewhere other than the dealer. In 2018 the FTC sent warning letters to automakers and others, and took action against BMW over MINI warranties being tied to MINI dealers. Consumer awareness is still low (AOCA research). Source: FTC report, pp.~10-12.
- **Interpretation:** the independent aftermarket rests on *legal* unbundling of the parts and service tie, not just on information.

### 10. Market scale
- The US aftermarket was about $516B in 2025 (light, medium and heavy duty; Hedges & Co), more than 1.9% of GDP, with nearly 5M jobs. US online parts sales were $42.4B in 2024. The DIY to DIFM shift continues as chains grow professional sales. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive_aftermarket
- **[BACKGROUND - verify]** Auto Care Association estimates that independents perform about 70%+ of out-of-warranty service. Average vehicle age is about 12.6 years or more.

## Synthesis: who got the profit, and why
1. **Parts retail and logistics density** (AutoZone, O'Reilly) took the profit: about 19% operating margins, $2.4-2.5B in net income each, and enough cash for buybacks that drove equity negative. The moat is having the right part within 30 minutes to a few hours in thousands of locations, plus credit and delivery for shops.
2. **Knowledge publishers** (Haynes, Chilton, MOTOR print) made knowledge universal, and then lost value when it became a commodity (Haynes went for GBP 114.5M; print ended in 2020).
3. **Knowledge built into transactions** (ALLDATA inside AutoZone, Mitchell 1 inside Snap-on, Mitchell and CCC in insurance estimating) survives as an attach product or as a workflow chokepoint.
4. **OEMs** keep re-creating lock-in at the newest layer: electronics, then telematics, part pairing and security gateways. Ballots and law broke it each time (MA 2012 at 86%, the 2014 MOU, MA 2020 at 75%), helped by the FTC. Technology did not break it.
5. **Distributors with weak execution** (Advance) end up near zero margin even with scale.

## Implications for the hypotheses
- C2 is partly supported: there was a measurable gap (59% and 67% in the 2005 survey), but parts availability and access/authorization mattered at least as much as know-how. The FTC complaint count (2 relevant out of 6,786) suggests consumers felt little harm.
- C3 is contradicted in practice: the auto aftermarket shows that democratized knowledge does not end the monopoly when OEMs control *access* (software, telematics, VIN-locked parts) and regulators accept security arguments (NHTSA 2023).
- C4: history suggests that even when knowledge becomes nearly free, profit and cost move to parts logistics. AutoZone and O'Reilly rule because delivery and availability are the hard part. Delivery is still physical.
- C7 is mixed: the auto aftermarket is not "built to be inefficient". It is a mature, highly competitive, efficient logistics system. The inefficiency is in deliberate OEM lock-in at the edges.
