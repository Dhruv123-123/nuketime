## Findings

### TransDigm
1. **DoD IG 2019 (DODIG-2019-060, Feb 2019)** [partly verified, via Wikipedia]
   - The IG found TransDigm earned excess profit on almost all of a sample of sole-source spare parts. Background figures: 46 of 47 parts, about $16.1M excess profit on about $26.2M of sales, and profit margins ranging up to roughly 4,400%.
   - The best-known example is a half-inch drive pin with a markup of about 9,400% (verified via Wikipedia).
   - Mechanism: the parts were bought below the TINA cost-data threshold or as "commercial items", so contracting officers could not get cost data. Prices were judged against TransDigm's own earlier prices.
   - TransDigm refunded about $16M after the May 2019 House Oversight hearing (verified via Wikipedia).
   - URL: https://media.defense.gov/2019/Feb/27/2002094522/-1/-1/1/DODIG-2019-060.PDF (403 to fetch). Summary: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransDigm_Group
2. **DoD IG Dec 2021 (DODIG-2022-043)**
   - Found about $20.8M excess profit on 105 spare parts across 150 contracts (verified via Wikipedia).
   - [BACKGROUND] The IG said TransDigm refused to provide uncertified cost data for some parts, and that DoD lacks the statutory tools to force disclosure for commercial or low-dollar sole-source buys.
   - TransDigm did not refund this time. In 2022, founder Nick Howley was called back to testify before Congress.
   - Unverified claim: Wikipedia says $119M was charged for parts that "should have cost" $28M, but tags it "citation needed".
   - URL: https://media.defense.gov/2021/Dec/14/2002908989/-1/-1/1/DODIG-2022-043.PDF
3. **FY2025 financials** (verified via Wikipedia)
   - Revenue $8.83B, operating income $4.17B, net income $1.87B.
   - That is about a 47% operating margin. Equity is negative at about −$9.7B, because the model is debt-funded buy-and-reprice.
   - [BACKGROUND] TransDigm reports "EBITDA as defined" margins of about 52-54%.
4. **Business model, from TransDigm's own disclosures** [BACKGROUND, with partial verification]
   - About 90% of net sales come from proprietary products, and about 75-80% of sales from products where TransDigm believes it is the sole source.
   - Aftermarket is roughly 50-55% of revenue but a clearly larger share of EBITDA. Wikipedia confirms the roughly half aftermarket, half OEM split as of 2016.
   - Its three "value drivers" are: (1) obtaining profitable new business, (2) continually improving the cost structure, and (3) providing value-based pricing.
   - It buys small proprietary-component makers (more than 60 acquisitions, Esterline for $4B in 2018; verified), then raises aftermarket prices.
   - Its stated aim is private-equity-like returns on a holding of about 15-20% IRR.
5. **Practitioner view** (verified via Wikipedia)
   - GA Telesis CEO Abdol Moabery says TransDigm's pricing makes airline repairs more expensive, and that Boeing and Airbus get squeezed when TransDigm buys their suppliers.
   - A former AvtechTyee employee says airlines "can't refuse": without the part, the plane doesn't fly.
   - Point: the AOG (aircraft on ground) cost of a grounded jet creates extremely price-inelastic demand.

### HEICO
6. **PMA model** [BACKGROUND]
   - HEICO's Flight Support Group designs FAA-approved PMA replacement parts and repairs. Wikipedia confirms "FAA-approved replacement parts and repair solutions".
   - HEICO and industry sources usually put PMA parts at about 30-40% below OEM list price, sometimes up to about 50%. That is a discount, not an order-of-magnitude saving.
   - HEICO says it holds well over 10,000 FAA-approved PMA parts.
   - Lufthansa Technik has owned 20% of HEICO Aerospace Holdings since 1997, which gives HEICO a launch customer and engineering-data partner. Airline MRO (maintenance, repair and overhaul) partners co-fund PMA development.
7. **HEICO financials**
   - FY2024 revenue was about $3.86B, with an operating margin of about 21% (verified via Wikipedia).
   - [BACKGROUND] FSG segment operating margins are about 22-24%.
   - So the PMA "disruptor" earns roughly half TransDigm's margin. Undercutting the OEM by about a third still leaves a very profitable business, which shows how high OEM aftermarket prices are.
   - HEICO has made 90+ acquisitions (verified), so it is also a roll-up.
8. **PMA approval routes** (verified via Wikipedia, PMA page)
   - A PMA needs FAA design approval through one of four routes: identicality, licence from the original approval holder, an earlier STC, or test and computation.
   - It also needs production approval, meaning the FAA audits the maker's quality system.
   - Reverse engineering the geometry is only the first step. Demonstrating airworthiness and quality is the main cost.
   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval
9. **PMA penetration is small** [BACKGROUND]
   - Industry estimates (AeroDynamic Advisory and others) put PMA at only low single-digit percent of the commercial aftermarket parts spend.
   - Barriers are commercial and contractual, not technical:
     - lessors' return conditions and asset-value worries (a PMA'd engine can be worth less at lease return)
     - engine-OEM service agreements (fly-by-hour, or "power-by-the-hour", contracts) that ban PMA
     - insurer and regulator acceptance in some jurisdictions (EASA via bilateral agreements)
     - OEM warranty threats
   - So even where an alternative part exists and is certified, the installed base resists it.

### Defence analog (technical data rights)
10. **GAO F-35 sustainment (GAO-23-105341)** (verified this session)
    - DoD has not identified or obtained the technical data and intellectual property it needs to run F-35 sustainment, and "relies heavily on its contractor".
    - Organizational-level maintenance is limited by a "lack of technical data and training".
    - More than 10,000 components were awaiting repair. The mission-capable rate was about 55% (March 2023).
    - Of the program's $1.7T life-cycle cost, about $1.3T is operating and sustainment.
    - As of March 2026, the IP plans were still unfinished.
    - https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
    - Point: here the "knowledge monopoly" is legal (data rights). It is not a gap in cognitive understanding.

## Synthesis: where value sits in aftermarket parts
- Value does not sit in manufacturing; machining a pin is cheap. It sits in:
  1. the type-certificate and PMA approval that makes a part legally installable
  2. sole-source status locked in by certification and requalification cost
  3. the AOG price-inelasticity of a grounded asset
  4. procurement rules that block cost visibility (TINA thresholds, commercial-item exemptions)
  5. contract structures (engine service agreements, lease return conditions) that stop substitution
- TransDigm proves the rent is big: about 47% operating margin, and IG-documented markups of hundreds to thousands of percent.
- HEICO proves the rent can be competed away only partly: 30-40% discounts, low single-digit market share, and decades of FAA approvals and airline partnerships.
- The scarce layer is the right to install (certification, data rights and contractual acceptance), not knowledge or fabrication.

## Implications for the hypotheses
- **C2 mixed:** a monopoly exists, but it is on IP, certification and installation rights, not on know-how. Independent engineers (HEICO) can and do reverse-engineer parts.
- **C3 contradicted:** AI does not remove certification, data-rights law or lessor and engine-agreement constraints. HEICO already had the knowledge, and still wins only about a third off price and a small share.
- **C4 contradicted for regulated aerospace:** the observed best case for competing parts is about 30-50% off price, not 10x. Diagnosis and understanding are not where the TransDigm rent comes from.
- **C5 contradicted:** fabricating a part is the easy step. The airworthiness approval and quality-system audit are the slow, costly step. An "AI parts factory" still needs a PMA, or a DER (designated engineering representative) repair approval, per part number.
- **C6 supports:** certification is the chokepoint and the real value store. Any rebuild has to go through, or speed up, the FAA/EASA approval and inspection process.
- **C7 mixed:** the inefficiency is not accidental "slop". It is a rational result of certification and contract structure, used deliberately (TransDigm's value-based pricing). "Built to be inefficient" is partly true in that rents are designed in, but that is driven by incentives rather than missing smarts.
- **C1 silent.**

## Company shapes the evidence points to
- PMA/DER-as-a-service: AI-accelerated reverse engineering plus certification packages that cut the cost and time per PMA approval. HEICO's model is limited by engineering and certification throughput per part number.
- Should-cost and price-transparency tools for DoD and airline buyers. The IG found contracting officers lacked cost data; AI should-cost models from geometry and material could arm negotiators.
- Data-rights and tech-data reconstruction for defence sustainment (the F-35 IP gap, the 10,000+ component repair backlog). Organic-depot enablement for the services.
- Not a "parts factory" alone: without the approval layer, fabrication commoditises.
