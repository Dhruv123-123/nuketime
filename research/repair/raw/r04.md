# r04: Repair techniques innovation: cold spray, laser cladding, additive repair of turbine blades, composite repair; how these change repair-vs-replace and who controls approval.

## Method note (important caveat)
The shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn (200 calls across all agents) was already used up when this agent began, so this agent could run **zero** web searches. Findings below come from (a) direct WebFetch of pages whose URLs were known (marked FETCHED) and (b) background knowledge from before the model's training cutoff (marked PRIOR KNOWLEDGE, not re-checked this session). Treat PRIOR KNOWLEDGE items as leads to confirm, not citations. Several primary pages (faa.gov PMA page, FAA AC 43.13-1B PDF, eCFR) returned 403s or redirects.

## Findings

### F1. Cold spray already repairs military parts "in minutes"; Army and Navy use it (FETCHED, encyclopedic)
- Wikipedia "Cold spraying": cold spray is "used to repair machine parts in a matter of minutes". The US Army uses it to repair a Black Hawk helicopter component. The US Navy adopted cold spray across global operations on an experimental basis (USNI News, 12 Sep 2023). GE is adapting it for civil engines (Washington Post, 22 Nov 2013, repairing engines on a tight budget).
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_spraying
- PRIOR KNOWLEDGE: MIL-STD-3021 (DoD cold spray standard, about 2015) came out of Army Research Laboratory work. Early depot uses were restoring corroded magnesium gearbox and transmission housings (UH-60, SH-60), which had been scrapped because magnesium cannot be fusion-welded. The value is turning a "scrap and buy new" part into a repairable part. That approval came through **military** engineering authority (depot plus program office), not a civil OEM. This is the route where the owner is also the airworthiness authority.
- What it means: the technique exists and is fielded. Adoption was gated by the owner-operator (DoD) writing its own standard and approving the repair, not by new knowledge.

### F2. Expeditionary cold-spray AM: hours or days instead of weeks or months (FETCHED, vendor claim)
- SPEE3D says its CSAM makes "cast-equivalent parts in hours or days, compared with weeks or months". Deposition up to 100 g/min. Part envelopes go up to 40 kg (about 0.9 to 1 m diameter by 0.7 m). It works on "non-weldable, high temperature alloys". It is pitched for field battle-damage repair (Expeditionary Manufacturing Unit). No cost figures and no named customers on the page.
- URL: https://www.spee3d.com/
- Opinion/marketing, not data. It supports C5 on speed for **non-flight-critical or military-owned** parts. It says nothing about certification for civil flight-critical use.

### F3. Laser cladding/DED: works when the mould no longer exists, but still needs a skilled technician (FETCHED)
- Wikipedia "Laser cladding" lists repair of worn parts, sintered tools and power-plant turbine blades. It cites "Laser cladding repair of turbine blades in power plants: From research to commercialisation" (2009). Repair is useful "when the part's mould no longer exists or a new fabrication would take too long". That is the same scenario as C5.
- Limits: technicians still set laser power, focal point, speed and powder feed. Sensor-based closed-loop automation is "still under research". The article carries a "needs citations" tag.
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_cladding
- DED page: high-speed LMD (EHLA) runs at least 10x the deposition speed of conventional LMD, with coatings under 0.5 mm per pass. Meltio wire-laser DED has been adopted by the defence forces of the US, France, Spain and South Korea. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_energy_deposition
- Again the early adopters are militaries (owner equals approver).

### F4. Blisks: repair is allowed only "within thresholds set by the design authority" (FETCHED)
- Wikipedia "Blisk": damage on integrally bladed rotors "beyond minor dents and scratches requires the rotor to be replaced". The alternative is cutting off blades and welding new ones on. Repairs are hard to do on the flightline and usually need engine removal. Adaptive repair works like this: 3D-scan the part, regenerate toolpaths from the measured (not nominal) geometry, weld material back, then machine. This is possible **only if damage is within thresholds set by the design authority**. Blisks also need extra harmonic vibration testing and balancing.
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blisk
- This is the clearest statement of the control point. The technique (scan, weld, adaptive machine) is solved. The *permission* (repair limits) belongs to the design authority, usually the OEM.

### F5. PMA/DER: a legal non-OEM route to parts and repairs has existed for decades (FETCHED)
- PMA authority dates to CAR 3.31 (1947), became FAR 21.303 in 1965, and got PMA-specific data rules in 1972 (Amendment 21-38). The aftermarket "expanded greatly in the 1980s as airlines sought to reduce the costs of spares". Approval routes are identicality, licensure (OEM assist letter), STC, and test-and-computation. Parts fabricated during repair are a separate regime (AC 43-18). IATA publishes guidance on accepting PMA and DER repairs.
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_Manufacturer_Approval
- DERs (14 CFR 183.29): consultant DERs "may approve, or recommend FAA approval of, technical data produced by any person or organization". That is the legal basis for non-OEM ("DER") repairs. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Designated_Engineering_Representative
- HEICO (the largest PMA/DER-repair player) had 2024 revenue of $3.86B, operating income of $824.5M and more than 90 acquisitions. It began focusing on aftermarket in 1986 with "FAA-approved replacement parts and repair solutions". URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HEICO
- PRIOR KNOWLEDGE: PMA parts are commonly said to cost about 30-40% below OEM list. Lufthansa Technik has held a stake in HEICO Aerospace since 1997. PMA and DER repairs remain a small share (low single-digit to about 10%) of engine material spend. The cause is commercial lock-in rather than regulation: OEM flight-hour service agreements (e.g. GE TrueChoice, RR TotalCare), lessor return conditions that require OEM parts or repairs, and residual-value worries.
- **Contradicts C2/C3 in part.** The knowledge to design a non-OEM repair has been legally reachable since the 1960s-80s through DERs. The binding constraints are commercial (contracts, leases, warranties) and liability, not knowledge.

### F6. Inspection, not repair technique, can dominate time: GTF powder-metal inspections take 250-300 days (FETCHED)
- PW1100G: in July 2023 there was a recall to inspect 1,200 of about 3,000 engines for contaminated powder metal, expanded to all 3,000 in Sep 2023. P&W says each inspection takes **250-300 days**. About **350 aircraft on ground** on average through 2026. Cost to P&W and partners is **$6-7B, ~80% customer compensation**.
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_%26_Whitney_PW1000G
- Where the time goes: shop-visit queue, teardown, NDI and part availability (HPT disks). Knowing the fault was not the slow part. The fix is known; capacity and certified parts are the constraint. This supports C6 strongly and undercuts C4: AI diagnosis would not have cut the 250-300 days much.

### F7. LEAP hot-section durability: the OEM redesigns, then the OEM controls the fix (FETCHED plus PRIOR KNOWLEDGE)
- LEAP-1A HPT CMC shroud coating flaked (Oct 2017). It was found by borescope after an EGT shift and fixed by a coating design change. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_LEAP
- PRIOR KNOWLEDGE: In 2023-2025, LEAP-1A HPT blade durability in hot and sandy environments drove early shop visits. GE/CFM responded with an OEM "durability kit" (new blade design, FAA-certified about 2024 for -1A). GE has also announced large investments in MRO and repair development (about $1B across its supply chain and MRO, 2024-25) and in **new HPT blade repairs** to extend life. Repair *development* is an OEM asset and revenue line, not a knowledge gap.
- Notice that diagnosis here is borescope plus EGT trending: cheap and already digital. The cost lives in the shop visit and the new or repaired blades.

### F8. Siemens Energy AM burner repair: lead time cut by about 90% (PRIOR KNOWLEDGE)
- Siemens (now Siemens Energy) began 3D-printed repair of SGT-800 gas-turbine burner tips around 2013-2016 at Finspång, Sweden. It claimed repair time fell from about 44 weeks to about 4 weeks. The repair was done by the OEM on its own design.
- Lesson: an order-of-magnitude cut in lead time is achievable *for the OEM*, which owns the design data and approval. That supports C4/C5 on physics, but the gains go to the incumbent.

### F9. USAF/DoD reverse-engineering and additive parts for obsolete parts (PRIOR KNOWLEDGE)
- Widely reported case (2018): a C-5 or KC-135 lavatory cover quoted at about $10k was reverse-engineered and printed for about $300 by USAF (Travis AFB / Rapid Sustainment Office era). RSO and depots have approved many non-critical AM parts. The Navy's first flight-critical metal AM part (an MV-22 link and fitting, 2016) took extensive qualification.
- Diminishing Manufacturing Sources and Material Shortages (DMSMS) is a recognised chronic DoD problem: suppliers exit, and small-lot reorders take years or cost prohibitive amounts.
- Supports C5 for **non-critical** parts. For critical parts, qualification (materials allowables, process control, NDI) is the gating cost, and it is per part and per process, not per "factory".

### F10. Composite repair: bonding works, but you cannot verify bond strength by inspection (PRIOR KNOWLEDGE)
- FAA AC 20-107B (composite structure) and OEM SRMs (e.g. Boeing 787 SRM) limit bonded-repair size and location. Larger damage needs OEM engineering disposition or bolted repairs. Known physics problem: NDI (ultrasound) finds voids and disbonds but **cannot detect "kissing bonds" or measure bond strength**. Regulators therefore cap bonded repairs that rely on adhesive alone for limit load (fail-safe "chicken rivets" or limits such as those in FAA policy PS-AIR-100-14-130-001). Repair quality depends on process control (surface prep, cure) and certified technicians.
- Supports C6 strongly: inspection technology is the binding constraint on what can be repaired. Better NDI (e.g. laser bond inspection) would expand repair-vs-replace. AI on today's NDI data cannot get information the sensor does not measure.
- Wind blades: leading-edge erosion and composite blade repair cost is dominated by access (rope teams, cranes, weather windows), not knowledge. PRIOR KNOWLEDGE.

### F11. Right-to-repair analogs: the monopoly is enforced by tools, parts and contracts, not just knowledge (FETCHED)
- A Terrance Group study found ~59% of independent repairers still struggled to get diagnostic tools and parts from OEMs. Only Taylor could repair McDonald's ice-cream machines (DMCA exemption for commercial food equipment). In COVID, ventilator servicing was hindered and iFixit compiled the largest set of medical service manuals. John Deere-AFBF MoU (Jan 2023). Colorado farm equipment law (2023). EU Right to Repair Directive (May 2024, transposition by 31 Jul 2026). Oregon parts-pairing ban (Jan 2025). FTC "Nixing the Fix" (2021).
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_repair
- Supports C2 (repair monopolies are real) but sharpens it. The lock is **access to tools, parts, firmware and authorisation**. Information alone does not unlock it. AI can't unlock a parts-pairing check or a service-agreement clause.

### F12. MRO market size context (FETCHED)
- Commercial MRO was $88B in 2018 and military $79.6B. Airliner MRO was forecast at $115B by 2028. Airframe heavy maintenance was about $6B (2019). After any task, a licensed person (A&P/Part-66 B1/B2/C, or a delegated org) signs a release to service. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_maintenance
- PRIOR KNOWLEDGE: engines are about 40-50% of airline MRO spend, and within an engine shop visit **material (parts) is ~60-70% of cost**, labour much less. So repair-vs-replace decisions on hot-section parts (HPT blades, NGVs, disks) dominate cost. A repaired HPT blade often costs a fraction (roughly 20-50%) of a new one, which is why repair development is valuable and why OEMs guard repair limits.

## Synthesis / reasoning

1. **Repair techniques shift the repair-vs-replace line.** Examples: cold spray on Mg housings (previously scrap), laser/DED tip restoration on blades and burners, adaptive blisk repair, bonded composite patches. Each one turns a replace into a repair. The economic prize sits in material, since parts are ~2/3 of an engine shop visit, not in diagnosis.
2. **Who controls approval decides who captures the value.** Repair limits and SRM/CMM data come from the design authority (the OEM). Non-OEM routes exist (DER repairs, PMA, STC, military engineering authority) and have for 40-60 years. Uptake is limited by commercial lock-in (power-by-the-hour, lessor return conditions, warranty) and liability, more than by knowledge.
3. **Militaries adopt first** (cold spray, CSAM, DED, reverse-engineered AM parts) because owner, operator and airworthiness authority are the same entity.
4. **Inspection and qualification are the hard bottleneck.** Bond strength can't be measured, so bonded repairs are capped. GTF inspections take 250-300 days. Each new AM/DED repair needs process qualification, material allowables and NDI. This is per part number and per process, which caps the "AI parts factory" idea for critical parts.
5. **Where AI could matter:** generating and documenting DER repair substantiation faster (engineering data packages), adaptive toolpaths from scans, in-process monitoring (melt-pool sensing) that could become qualification evidence, and automated NDI interpretation. All of these speed up existing steps. None removes the approval step.

## Stances
- C1 (one chain): mixed. The chain holds, but at the component level, "repair development and approval" (getting a repair *allowed*) is a separate upstream loop that sets repair-vs-replace. It isn't in the chain.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed. OEM control is real, but it is enforced through approved data, contracts, leases and tools, and DER/PMA routes have long existed.
- C3 (AI removes gap): contradicts. The missing items are certified data, qualification evidence and authority, not information.
- C4 (10x on four steps): contradicts or mixed. Costs sit in material and shop capacity. Diagnosis is already cheap (borescope, EGT trend). A 10x lead-time cut is possible (Siemens ~44 to ~4 weeks) but only on narrow cases and through the OEM.
- C5 (bespoke part solved): mixed. True for non-critical and military-owned parts (SPEE3D, USAF $10k to $300). Not true for flight-critical parts, where qualification is per part.
- C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supports strongly (kissing bonds, GTF 250-300 day inspections, design-authority thresholds).
- C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed. Some inefficiency is commercial rent-seeking (lock-in). Much of it is the deliberate cost of safety assurance and liability.
