## Findings

### A. What share of failures are predictable (age/wear-related vs random)
1. **[RECALLED] Nowlan & Heap, "Reliability-Centered Maintenance" (United Airlines for US DoD, 1978; DTIC ADA066579, https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/citations/ADA066579, fetch returned 403).** Six conditional-probability-of-failure patterns across aircraft components: A bathtub ~4%, B wear-out ~2%, C gradually rising ~5%, D low then rising ~7%, E random ~14%, F infant mortality then random ~68%. So only ~11% (A+B+C) show a clear age/wear-out zone; ~89% show no wear-out point at which time-based replacement helps. Key nuance: random-in-time does NOT mean undetectable. RCM's answer for those modes is on-condition inspection where a detectable potential failure (P) exists, and run-to-failure where it does not.
2. **[RECALLED] Follow-on studies with the same pattern set.** Broberg 1973 (A3/B1/C4/D11/E15/F66). US Navy MSP 1982 (A3/B17/C3/D6/E42/F29). SSMD 1993 (A2/B10/C17/E56/F15). The share of age-related patterns differs by fleet and asset type. Submarines and ships show more wear-out (B) than aircraft. Still, in every study most failure modes are not age-predictable. Caveats: these data come from complex, already heavily maintained systems, with censored data. Some infant mortality (pattern F) is caused by maintenance itself, so pattern shares are not universal constants.
3. **[VERIFIED-FETCH] Wikipedia, Condition monitoring (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condition_monitoring).** States that "more than 80%" of complex mechanical equipment fails "accidentally" and unrelated to its life-cycle period. It also notes that permanent vibration monitoring cannot be economically justified on non-critical balance-of-plant machines, and that interpreting vibration signals takes specialist training. **[VERIFIED-FETCH] Wikipedia, Reliability-centered maintenance (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliability-centered_maintenance):** early-1960s UAL studies found the fixed-lifetime assumption wrong "in nearly every example" and that "the vast majority of failures are not necessarily linked to the age of the asset."
4. **[RECALLED] The P-F curve / P-F interval (Moubray, RCM II, 1991/1997; SAE JA1011/JA1012).** Condition-based detection is useful only when (a) a measurable potential-failure signal exists, (b) the P-F interval is longer than the inspection or data interval plus the time needed to respond (parts, crew), and (c) the interval is reasonably consistent. Many failure modes have short or inconsistent P-F intervals: electronics, fatigue cracks below detection limits, operator error, foreign-object damage, software. Moubray-era practitioners commonly claim 30-80% of preventive maintenance tasks add little or no value. Practitioner opinion, not hard data.
5. **[VERIFIED-FETCH] Wikipedia, Prognostics (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prognostics).** The "principal bottleneck" for data-driven prognostics is the scarcity of run-to-failure data, especially for new systems. When future usage differs from past usage, covering all loads and environments is "often close to impossible." Failure thresholds shift with operating conditions, and estimates carry irreducible uncertainty. This limit is structural. It is NOT a knowledge-access gap that an LLM closes.

### B. PdM ROI: claimed benefits and where those numbers come from
6. **[RECALLED] US DOE FEMP O&M Best Practices Guide, Release 3.0 (2010) (old URL https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2013/10/f3/omguide_complete.pdf now 404).** The most-recycled PdM figures: roughly 10x ROI, 25-30% lower maintenance costs, 70-75% fewer breakdowns, 35-45% less downtime, 20-25% more production. These trace back to 1990s industry surveys and vendor case data, not controlled studies. Consulting and vendor pages still repeat them. The same guide cites a survey finding that >55% of maintenance is reactive, ~31% preventive and ~12% predictive.
7. **[VERIFIED-FETCH] Deloitte, "Predictive maintenance and the smart factory" (https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/services/consulting/services/predictive-maintenance-and-the-smart-factory.html).** The only figures in its text describe the problem, not measured results: poor maintenance strategy costs 5-20% of productive capacity, and unplanned downtime costs ~$50B/yr (via an Emerson/IndustryWeek figure). The "quantitative benefits" appear only as an image, sourced to "internal Deloitte analysis". This is typical: headline PdM benefit numbers rarely trace back to published, audited results.
8. **[RECALLED] McKinsey.** In 2017 McKinsey said PdM "typically" cuts machine downtime 30-50% and extends machine life 20-40%. Its later reliability pieces (c. 2018-2021, e.g., "Digitally enabled reliability: beyond predictive maintenance"; "Establishing the right analytics-based maintenance strategy", fetch returned 503) are more sceptical. Many PdM pilots failed to scale. For many asset classes, false positives (unnecessary inspections, teardowns, parts pulled early) mean PdM costs more than well-designed preventive or condition-based maintenance. Only a minority of assets, those with high criticality and a detectable degradation signal, justify full PdM. McKinsey's recommended step change comes mainly from better maintenance processes, planning and RCM-style work selection, not the algorithm. Practitioner/consultant view.
9. **[RECALLED] Siemens/Senseye, "The True Cost of Downtime" (2022, 2024).** Fortune Global 500 firms lose ~11% of revenue (~$1.4T/yr in the 2024 edition) to unplanned downtime. An hour of downtime at an automotive plant costs roughly $2.3M (2024). These numbers explain why PdM is pitched so hard, but they are vendor-commissioned estimates.

### C. Adoption rates
10. **[RECALLED] Plant Engineering annual maintenance surveys (2019-2024) and similar trade surveys.** Run-to-failure/reactive maintenance is still used at a majority of plants (~60%+). Preventive maintenance is near-universal (~75-85%). "Predictive" use is reported at ~40-50%, but this mostly means handheld vibration, thermography and oil analysis rounds, not ML-based PdM. Self-reported trade-press surveys (opinion data). The verified fetch of the Plant Engineering 2019 study URL returned only a listing page.
11. **[RECALLED] IoT Analytics, Predictive Maintenance market reports (2021, 2023).** The PdM software and services market was estimated at single-digit $B (~$5-7B in 2022), growing ~17-30%/yr. That is small against the global MRO and industrial maintenance spend (hundreds of $B to >$1T). PdM remains a thin layer over a mostly preventive or reactive industry.

### D. Failed or disappointing platform deployments
12. **[VERIFIED-FETCH partly] GE Digital / Predix (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GE_Digital).** Predix ran as an internal platform from 2013 and launched externally in 2016, with ServiceMax bought in 2016-17. In 2018 GE announced a $1.2B standalone IIoT software company and planned to sell ServiceMax. It then reversed in October 2019 and kept the business. GE Digital was folded into GE Vernova, and the page lists it as defunct from the April 2024 spin. **[RECALLED]:** Immelt's 2015-16 goal was "top-10 software company" and ~$15B software revenue by 2020. GE Digital revenue stayed ~$1B-scale apart from existing software. Predix was cut back to an internal-asset focus after 2017-18 layoffs and leadership changes (Bill Ruh left in 2018). Commentators such as HBR 2018 ("Why GE Digital Didn't Make It Big", URL now 404) blamed it on trying to build a horizontal platform before proving vertical use cases, on customers' reluctance to hand data to an OEM competitor, and on the services-heavy nature of each deployment.
13. **[VERIFIED-FETCH] Uptake (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptake_(business)).** Funding: $45M at ~$1B valuation (2015), $40M at $2B (Feb 2017), then a $117M Series D at $2.3B post-money (Nov 2017), for >$250M total. Strategic customers: Caterpillar, Progress Rail locomotives, Berkshire Hathaway Energy wind turbines, US Army Bradley vehicles (a few dozen). It hired GE's ex-CDO Ganesh Bell as president (2018), who left in 2019, followed by repeated CEO and president changes (2020, 2021). **[RECALLED]:** multiple layoff rounds (2019-2020), a narrowing to fleet and rail niches, and no further marquee priced rounds. Litigation with C3 IoT (2017-18) involved allegations that one firm used a third party to obtain the other's software. Uptake is widely cited as a unicorn whose PdM-platform thesis did not deliver a scaled business.
14. **[VERIFIED-FETCH] C3.ai (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C3.ai).** FY2025 (to April 2025) revenue was $389.1M with a net loss of $288.7M and 1,181 employees. The stock IPO'd at $42 (Dec 2020), peaked at $177 and traded around $10.40 in February 2026. CEO turnover: Ehikian took over in September 2025, then Siebel returned in May 2026. **[RECALLED]:** reliability/PdM (e.g., US Air Force PANDA, Baker Hughes oil-and-gas reliability) was the flagship use case. Baker Hughes was historically about a third of revenue, so concentration was high. Ten-plus years in, the lead enterprise-AI PdM vendor still loses roughly 75 cents per revenue dollar.

## Synthesis / reasoning
- **Predictability is bounded by physics and data, not by access to knowledge.** Even in the best-instrumented domain (airlines), only about 10-20% of failure modes are age-related. Most of the rest can be handled by on-condition monitoring only where a detectable, long-enough P-F interval exists. The remainder is effectively random and is handled by redundancy, design and run-to-failure with fast restoration. AI improves the detection side (better signals and lower sensor and analytics cost) but cannot create a P-F interval where none exists.
- **The value is usually lost after the alert, not in the analytics.** The recurring story of failed deployments: false positives erode technicians' trust, integration with work-order and planning systems (CMMS/EAM) is weak, parts and crew are not available inside the P-F window, and every site needs custom data plumbing. The famous ROI numbers are old and vendor-sourced.
- **Horizontal "AI for all machines" platforms fail.** GE Predix, Uptake and C3 are all broadly a horizontal-platform thesis that met services-heavy, asset-specific reality. Survivors and successes tend to be vertical and bundled with the OEM's service contract (Rolls-Royce TotalCare engine health monitoring, Caterpillar dealer condition monitoring) or narrow (Augury in rotating equipment, SparkCognition/Avathon, Samsara for fleets). This last point is recalled, not verified here.

## Implications for founder claims
- C1: The P-F model adds steps the chain leaves out. "Notice" can happen before failure (a potential failure), and there is a strategy-choice step that decides whether to monitor, replace on schedule, redesign or run to failure. Mixed.
- C2: Mixed. OEMs do control fleet failure data (one reason GE's customers resisted Predix). But the binding limits are scarce run-to-failure data and short P-F intervals, not withheld manuals.
- C3: Contradicted for prediction. Data scarcity, uncertainty and the share of random failures are not knowledge gaps that AI removes.
- C4: Contradicted as a blanket claim. A decade of well-funded AI-PdM did not deliver order-of-magnitude gains. Credible ranges are tens of percent, on a minority of assets, with false-positive costs.
- C5: Silent.
- C6: Mildly supports. RCM and on-condition inspection are the core of the response to random failures, and inspection intervals themselves get re-tuned by data.
- C7: Mixed. Evidence shows industries are not dumb so much as constrained by physics, economics and integration. Aviation built RCM 50 years ago, so it is not "not built for smart". But adoption surveys do show lots of reactive maintenance.
