## Findings

### 1. F-35: sustainment is the dominant lifecycle cost, and the bottleneck is technical data/IP, depot capacity and parts, not diagnosis [VERIFIED THIS SESSION]
Source: GAO-23-105341, "F-35 Aircraft: DOD and the Military Services Need to Reassess the Future Sustainment Strategy", https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341 (page updated with March 2026 recommendation status)
- About $1.7T lifecycle cost for about 2,500 aircraft; about $1.3T of that is operating and sustainment.
- DOD "relies heavily on its contractor to lead and manage F-35 sustainment"; it "has not identified or obtained the technical data it needs". Organisational-level maintenance is hurt by "a lack of technical data and training".
- Over 10,000 components were waiting for repair; depot stand-up behind schedule.
- Mission-capable rate about 55% (March 2023).
- Services must take over sustainment management by Oct 2027; technical data transfer is targeted for end of 2028. All 7 GAO recommendations were still open as of March 2026.
- Reading: strong support for C2, but in a specific form. The knowledge monopoly is contractual (IP/data rights), not cognitive. The government lacks the *rights* to the data, not the ability to understand it. AI does not fix a data-rights problem (against C3). The 10,000-component repair backlog is a capacity/throughput problem at the repair and depot stage, which is outside the founder's four intermediate steps.

### 2. Aftermarket obsolescence supply is real, large and already "solved" commercially for many chips, through authorised licensing, not AI [VERIFIED THIS SESSION]
Sources: https://www.rocelec.com/about ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochester_Electronics ; https://www.rocelec.com/news
- Over 15 billion devices in stock across more than 200,000 part numbers; over 12 billion die in stock.
- "100% authorized by over 70 leading semiconductor manufacturers". Has manufactured more than 20,000 device types and claims capability for over 70,000.
- Services: design, wafer processing, assembly, test, reliability, IP archiving. Can replicate parts "when silicon or design documents no longer exist" (Wikipedia summary).
- Founded 1981 by ex-Motorola Curt Gerrish, on the insight that semiconductor lifecycles are much shorter than the lifecycles of the systems they go into. About 700+ employees. Private LLC.
- Recent news (2026): final-inventory buys of Infineon legacy MCUs; over 1.4 billion TI units in stock; expanded DLA certifications (May 2026); BAE Systems supplier award (March 2026); EOL continuation for NXP MPC5xx/MPC860.
- Reading: the "bespoke part when the supplier is gone" problem (C5) already has a 45-year-old business model. Its moat is OEM *authorisation* (IP licences, original die, test programs, traceability) plus DLA qualification. It is not fabrication capability alone. That cuts against "an AI parts factory can just make it": for safety-critical parts, provenance and authorisation are the product.

### 3. Grey-market sourcing of obsolete parts is dominated by counterfeits; provenance is the scarce asset [VERIFIED THIS SESSION]
Source: GAO-12-375, "DOD Supply Chain: Suspect Counterfeit Electronic Parts Can Be Found on Internet Purchasing Platforms" (Feb 2012), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-12-375
- GAO bought 16 military-grade parts (obsolete/rare part numbers, post-production date codes, bogus part numbers). None of the 16 were legitimate. All 12 tested parts were suspect counterfeit (re-marked, altered date codes, some failed metallic composition). Four vendors supplied parts for part numbers that do not exist.
- 396 vendors responded (334 in China). Responses usually came within a day, with delivery within about two weeks.
- Reading: "sourcing" an obsolete part is easy and fast. Sourcing a *genuine* one is the hard part. Fast, cheap sourcing without assurance makes things worse. This supports C6 (inspection and verification are essential) and contradicts the idea that AI-driven sourcing alone yields order-of-magnitude savings (C4).

### 4. 2011-12 Senate Armed Services counterfeit investigation led to statutory sourcing restrictions [FROM PRIOR KNOWLEDGE, NOT RE-VERIFIED]
Source: SASC, "Inquiry into Counterfeit Electronic Parts in the Department of Defense Supply Chain" (May 2012); FY2012 NDAA Section 818 (10 U.S.C. 2320 amendments); DFARS 252.246-7007/7008.
- Commonly cited: about 1,800 cases of suspect counterfeits, totalling over 1 million parts, in DoD systems including the P-8A, C-130J and SH-60B; most traced to China.
- Section 818 put the cost of counterfeit remediation on contractors and pushed procurement toward OCM/authorised sources (DFARS 252.246-7008 sourcing hierarchy).
- Reading: regulation deliberately narrows sourcing to authorised channels. This is a designed "inefficiency" that buys assurance. It nuances C7: some apparent inefficiency is intentional risk control.

### 5. Root cause: lifecycle mismatch between commercial electronics and defence platforms [FROM PRIOR KNOWLEDGE, NOT RE-VERIFIED, plus Rochester founding thesis VERIFIED]
- DoD/aerospace platforms run 30-60+ years (B-52 planned to about 2050s; KC-135 still flying). Commercial IC production windows are commonly 2-10 years. Defence's share of semiconductor demand fell from a large fraction in the 1960s-70s to roughly 1% or less today, so it has little leverage over fabs.
- The 1994 Perry memo on acquisition reform shifted DoD toward COTS and away from MIL-SPEC. That cut acquisition cost but sharply raised obsolescence exposure.
- Reading: obsolescence is structural and exogenous to the "repair chain". The part disappears because the commercial market moved on, not because of a knowledge gap. This is partial support for C7 (the system is not designed for long life), but the cause is the mismatch between market and platform, not repair-industry design.

### 6. DMSMS resolution costs: an escalating ladder, with redesign dominant [FROM PRIOR KNOWLEDGE, NOT RE-VERIFIED; source family: DoD SD-22 DMSMS Guidebook; DSPO/IDA "DMSMS cost factors" study]
- SD-22 groups resolutions into: existing stock, reclamation, alternate/substitute part, authorised aftermarket, emulation, lifetime/bridge buy, minor redesign, major redesign.
- The DoD cost-factor studies (IDA for DSPO, about 2015) published average per-case resolution costs that rise by about 1-3 orders of magnitude from the cheapest to the most expensive rung. Simple alternate-part or existing-stock resolutions cost in the low thousands of dollars. Emulation and aftermarket cost in the tens to low hundreds of thousands. Board-level or system redesign costs in the hundreds of thousands to millions, mostly from engineering, requalification and testing. EXACT FIGURES NOT RE-VERIFIED; see SD-22 (https://www.dsp.dla.mil/Programs/DMSMS/).
- SD-22's core message: proactive management (BOM monitoring, predicting obsolescence, planning bridge buys and technology refresh) has a large return relative to reactive resolution. Reactive cases are driven to costly redesigns because no time is left.
- Reading: most of the cost sits in redesign plus re-qualification/re-certification, not in "knowing" that a part is obsolete. Monitoring/prediction (a knowledge task) is already commoditised by tools like SiliconExpert, IHS/Accuris and Z2Data. This supports C4 only for the cheap rungs. The expensive rung is requalification, which is C6 territory.

### 7. Lifetime buys: cheap per unit, expensive in forecasting error and holding cost [FROM PRIOR KNOWLEDGE, NOT RE-VERIFIED; source family: Peter Sandborn, CALCE/Univ. of Maryland, e.g. Sandborn, "Cost Analysis of Electronic Systems" and lifetime-buy optimisation papers; Bartels, Ermel, Sandborn and Pecht, *Strategies to the Prediction, Mitigation and Management of Product Obsolescence* (Wiley, 2012)]
- Lifetime buys require forecasting demand for decades. Over-buy leaves capital in inventory, storage, and degradation risk (e.g. moisture or solderability, and the need for long-term storage such as nitrogen dry-boxes). Under-buy forces a later redesign anyway.
- Sandborn's work also argues that design refresh planning (MOCA, "Mitigation of Obsolescence Cost Analysis") can choose the timing of refreshes to minimise lifecycle cost.
- Reading: better forecasting (where AI could help) has real but bounded value. This is an optimisation of an inventory-financing problem, not removal of a knowledge monopoly.

### 8. Classic anecdotes showing scale [FROM PRIOR KNOWLEDGE, NOT RE-VERIFIED]
- NASA reportedly sourced legacy Intel 8086 chips on secondary markets (including eBay) for Space Shuttle ground test equipment (NYT, 2002).
- F-22: early avionics processors went obsolete before full-rate production. The program did lifetime buys and later costly avionics modernisation. This is often cited as a canonical DMSMS case.
- Reading: obsolescence hits even the most knowledge-rich organisations (NASA, Lockheed). That contradicts the claim that the bottleneck is lack of knowledge (C2/C3). They knew exactly what part they needed and could not buy a qualified one.

### 9. Additive manufacturing and reverse engineering for obsolete parts: real progress, gated by qualification [FROM PRIOR KNOWLEDGE, NOT RE-VERIFIED; source family: USAF Rapid Sustainment Office, Air Force Sustainment Center, Navy NAVAIR AM program]
- USAF RSO and the depots (Tinker/OC-ALC) reverse-engineer and additively manufacture out-of-production parts for legacy aircraft (B-1, B-52, KC-135, C-5). Examples include non-critical interior parts and some engine and structural parts after airworthiness approval. Reported savings are large per part in some cases, but volumes of flight-critical AM parts qualified remain small.
- The binding constraint commonly cited is airworthiness qualification and certification of each new process, material and part (MIL-HDBK-516, engineering authority sign-off), plus technical data rights to reproduce the part.
- Reading: C5 is contradicted for safety-critical parts. Making the geometry is the easy part, and the qualified material/process plus the airworthiness release is the hard part. Supports C6.

### 10. Data rights / IP as the knowledge monopoly mechanism [VERIFIED for F-35 via Finding 1; general pattern FROM PRIOR KNOWLEDGE]
- Repeated GAO and congressional findings (F-35, and Army "right to repair" provisions debated in FY2024-FY2025 NDAA cycles) show DoD often lacks the technical data packages to compete or organically perform repair and reproduce parts.
- Reading: the "knowledge monopoly" in defence is legally constructed (contract data rights, ITAR, proprietary designs), and can be addressed only by contracts, law or reverse engineering. Each of those carries legal and qualification cost. AI can speed reverse engineering (e.g. scanning to CAD, analysing legacy schematics), but it cannot confer the legal right to use the data or an airworthiness release.

## Synthesis

**Where money and time go in DMSMS.** The steps of noticing and diagnosing obsolescence are mostly cheap and already tool-supported (BOM monitoring databases, PCNs/EOL notices). The cost escalates at the resolution stage: lifetime-buy capital and forecasting risk, then emulation, then redesign plus requalification and recertification. Redesign and requalification dominate the cost of the expensive cases. Time is lost mainly when organisations are reactive, so the remaining window forces redesign. At the platform level (F-35), sustainment is about 76% of lifecycle cost, and the visible bottlenecks are depot repair capacity (over 10,000 components waiting), technical data rights and supply.

**Real bottleneck.** The scarce layer is assured provenance and qualification: an authorised, traceable and qualified source for a part, or a requalified redesign. Legal data rights decide who may make or repair the part. Knowledge of *what* is wrong or *which* part is needed is rarely the binding constraint in this domain.

**Stance on hypotheses.**
- C1: mixed. DMSMS often starts *before* failure (a PCN or EOL notice), so the chain is not always failure-driven. Obsolescence management is a supply-side, predictive loop, not just a repair sequence.
- C2: mixed. There is a knowledge monopoly, but it is mostly legal/contractual (data rights) and physical (OEM die, test programs), not cognitive.
- C3: contradicts. NASA, Lockheed and DoD have the knowledge and still face obsolescence. AI cannot grant data rights or produce qualified silicon.
- C4: mixed. AI could cut monitoring, forecasting and reverse-engineering costs. The dominant costs (redesign plus requalification, lifetime-buy capital) are not in those four steps or are only partly addressable. Cheap fast sourcing increases counterfeit risk (GAO: 16 of 16 bogus).
- C5: contradicts for safety-critical parts. Rochester shows a mature model that already exists, built on authorisation. AM parts are gated by qualification. It is plausible for non-critical parts.
- C6: supports strongly. Counterfeit risk and airworthiness qualification make inspection and verification central. AI-assisted inspection (X-ray, decapsulation analysis, AM process monitoring) is a real opportunity.
- C7: mixed. There is structural mismatch (30-60-year platforms against 2-10-year chips), plus contractor-controlled sustainment. However, authorised-source rules are deliberate risk control, not slop.

**Company shapes suggested by this evidence.**
1. AI-accelerated "redesign and requalification" engine for obsolete electronics. It would ingest legacy schematics and test data, propose form-fit-function replacements or FPGA/ASIC emulation, and auto-generate the qualification test plan and evidence. This attacks the most expensive rung.
2. Provenance and inspection-as-a-service for obsolete and secondary-market parts. Machine-vision and X-ray counterfeit detection, with a chain-of-custody ledger. This attacks the trust gap that blocks cheap sourcing.
3. Qualified-process additive and reverse-engineering foundry for legacy defence and industrial platforms. The moat would be pre-qualified materials and processes plus the airworthiness/engineering-authority paperwork pipeline, not the printers.
4. Predictive DMSMS and lifetime-buy optimiser that combines BOM data, fleet demand and failure data. Incumbents like SiliconExpert and Z2Data exist, so the wedge would have to be the decision layer (which buy quantity, and when to redesign).
5. A "Rochester-like" licensed continuation manufacturer for non-semiconductor obsolete parts (connectors, hydraulics, power supplies). The key is OEM licensing deals.

**Surprises.**
- GAO found that 16 of 16 obsolete or rare military parts bought online were not legitimate, and sellers would even supply part numbers that do not exist.
- Rochester Electronics holds about 12 billion die and is authorised by over 70 OEMs. The "parts factory for dead suppliers" already exists at scale and has existed since 1981.
- The F-35 government sustainment crisis is about data rights: all 7 GAO recommendations were still open in 2026, and over 10,000 components were waiting for repair.
