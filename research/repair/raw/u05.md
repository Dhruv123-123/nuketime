# u05: Tacit vs explicit knowledge in skilled manual work (Polanyi, Collins, Orr "Talking About Machines" on Xerox reps): what does ethnography say about how repair knowledge actually works?

Research-limit note: the shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn was used up before this agent ran, so no searches were made. Findings come from (a) pages fetched directly this session (marked VERIFIED, with URL), and (b) well-established canonical literature I know from before the session (marked CANONICAL, unverified this session; citations given so they can be checked). Confidence is high for the canonical items (widely cited classics), but specific numbers from them should be re-checked before they go in a pitch.

## Findings

1. VERIFIED: Polanyi, The Tacit Dimension (1966): "we can know more than we can tell"; all knowledge is rooted in tacit knowledge. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit_knowledge

2. VERIFIED: Collins's typology (Tacit and Explicit Knowledge, U. Chicago Press, 2010) splits tacit knowledge three ways. Relational tacit knowledge could be made explicit but isn't, because of secrecy, logistics or nobody thinking to say it. Somatic tacit knowledge is held in the body (feel, touch, motor skill). Collective tacit knowledge lives in social groups and current methods cannot make it explicit. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit_knowledge
   - Reasoning: this is the key lens for C3. AI and documentation can plausibly erase much of the RELATIONAL gap (manuals, service bulletins, fault trees, forum lore that exists somewhere but is hidden). They cannot erase SOMATIC knowledge (feel of a seized bearing, torque by hand, the sound of a failing pump). Machines also hit limits on COLLECTIVE knowledge: judging what counts as "fixed", or which customer complaint matters. So the "knowledge gap is gone" claim is roughly true for one of three types.

3. CANONICAL: Julian Orr, Talking About Machines: An Ethnography of a Modern Job (Cornell/ILR Press, 1996), based on fieldwork with Xerox photocopier field reps in the 1980s. Main points:
   - Xerox's official "directive documentation" (decision-tree repair manuals) assumed faults were predictable and listed. Real faults often weren't in the tree, especially ones from the machine's interaction with its site, its user and its history.
   - Reps diagnosed by building a coherent narrative of the machine's behaviour. They swapped "war stories" over breakfast and lunch, and those stories were the main way diagnostic knowledge was stored and passed on.
   - Repair is also social work with the customer: managing the customer's view of the machine, of the rep and of Xerox.
   - Famous example: a new machine with an intermittent fault that the error codes did not explain. Two reps spent most of a day on it and solved it only by building a shared story, piecing together fragments of prior experience.
   - Implication: the bottleneck was not access to information. The documentation existed. The bottleneck was interpreting ambiguous symptoms on a specific machine in a specific context.

4. CANONICAL: Brown & Duguid, "Organizational Learning and Communities-of-Practice", Organization Science 2(1), 1991 (https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2.1.40, 403 on fetch). They reread Orr to set "canonical practice" (manuals, training) against "non-canonical practice" (what reps actually do). Their claim: the gap between the two is where the learning and innovation happen, and organisations that suppress non-canonical practice by over-specifying procedure hurt performance. Three key processes: narration, collaboration and social construction.

5. VERIFIED (secondary): Xerox Eureka. Xerox built Eureka to share field reps' tips across its global network. "The Eureka database is estimated to have saved the corporation $100 million." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_of_practice, citing Brown & Duguid, HBR 2000, "Balancing Act: How to Capture Knowledge Without Killing It" (https://hbr.org/2000/05/balancing-act-how-to-capture-knowledge-without-killing-it).
   - CANONICAL details (from Brown & Duguid, The Social Life of Information, 2000): Eureka started in France around 1994-96 and reached about 25,000 or more tips. Reps' tips were peer-validated by other technicians, and authorship was credited instead of paid for. It reportedly cut parts and labour costs about 5-10%. The numbers vary across retellings, so treat them as approximate.
   - Reasoning: this is a 30-year-old precedent for C2/C3. Capturing field knowledge as searchable text works and pays, but only when practitioners author and validate it. It is also the precedent most like "LLM over repair knowledge". It cut costs by a single-digit percentage, not by 10x.

6. VERIFIED: Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work", NBER w31161 (QJE 2025), a field study of 5,179 customer-support agents. https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
   - Issues resolved per hour rose 14% on average. Novice and low-skilled agents gained 34%. The most skilled saw "minimal impact", and in the full text, small but statistically significant DECREASES in resolution rate and customer satisfaction.
   - Handle time fell from about 40 to 35 minutes, a 9% decline. Resolution rate rose 1.3 percentage points.
   - The paper says explicitly that the model captures top performers' tacit knowledge and spreads it. Agents exposed to the AI kept performing above their pre-AI baseline during AI outages, which suggests they learned from it instead of only depending on it.
   - Reasoning: the best hard evidence that LLMs can move some tacit-ish, conversational problem-solving knowledge from experts to novices. But the gain is about 14% (34% for novices), not 10x, and the work is text-based support, not hands-on repair. It supports a narrow form of C3 (the novice-expert gap shrinks a lot) and contradicts C4's order of magnitude.

7. CANONICAL: Dell'Acqua et al., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier", HBS WP 24-013 (2023), a BCG study with 758 consultants. On tasks inside the AI frontier: about 12% more tasks, about 25% faster, about 40% higher quality. On a task just outside the frontier: AI users were about 19 percentage points LESS likely to get the right answer. (The HBS PDF returned 403 on fetch; numbers are from memory.)
   - Reasoning: AI help is "jagged". In diagnosis, this is the risk that AI gives confident wrong answers on the off-tree, intermittent, context-specific faults Orr described, which are exactly the costly ones.

8. VERIFIED: Autor, "Polanyi's Paradox and the Shape of Employment Growth", NBER w20485 (2014). Machine substitution is overstated and complementarity understated. Tasks needing adaptability and common sense are hard to automate. Machine learning tries to get round Polanyi's paradox by learning tacit rules from examples. https://www.nber.org/papers/w20485

9. VERIFIED: Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation" (Automatica, 1983). Automating most of a job leaves humans with the hardest leftover tasks and takes away practice, so "operators will not practice skills as part of their ongoing work". Training needs go UP, to cover rare but crucial interventions. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironies_of_Automation
   - Reasoning: if AI does routine diagnosis, technicians lose the tacit skill they need for the hard cases AI gets wrong. This argues against "the knowledge gap is gone": the gap may move and come back as deskilling.

10. VERIFIED (partial): Matt Beane, The Skill Code (2024), and his robotic-surgery ethnography. Skill is built mostly by working alongside experts. Intelligent technologies can cut novices' chances to learn by sidelining them ("Today's Robotic Surgery Turns Surgical Trainees Into Spectators"). https://www.mattbeane.com/
    - CANONICAL: Beane, "Shadow Learning" (Administrative Science Quarterly, 2019). In robotic surgery, trainees mostly learned through rule-bending "shadow learning", because the official path gave them little hands-on time.

11. CANONICAL: Collins, "The TEA Set: Tacit Knowledge and Scientific Networks" (Science Studies, 1974). Laboratories could not build a working TEA laser from published papers and blueprints alone. They succeeded only after personal contact with people who had built one, and sometimes not even then. This is the classic proof that explicit specifications do not carry somatic and relational know-how.
    - Reasoning: directly relevant to C5. A drawing, a CAD file or an AI-generated spec does not equal a working part. Process knowledge (heat treatment, surface finish, tolerancing, qualification) is tacit, which is why making obsolete parts is hard and certification-bound.

12. CANONICAL: Crawford, Shop Class as Soulcraft (2009). From his own motorcycle repair: diagnosis means reasoning under uncertainty with the real machine, where "the thing" pushes back. The manual is necessary but not enough, and expertise is "stochastic" (probabilistic) judgment built through embodied experience.

13. CANONICAL: Douglas Harper, Working Knowledge: Skill and Community in a Small Shop (1987), an ethnography of a rural mechanic named Willie. His "kinesthetic" knowledge (the feel of metal, improvised fixes, making parts from scrap) let him repair what the official parts system couldn't supply. It is a historical analogue of the "make the bespoke part" idea, done by a skilled individual rather than a factory.

14. CANONICAL: Stephen Barley (ed.), Between Craft and Science: Technical Work in U.S. Settings (1997), plus Barley's work on technicians. Technicians sit between the material world and representations of it. Their value lies in translating symptoms into representations and back, and this work is systematically underrated by organisations and labour statistics.

## Synthesis

- The ethnographic consensus (Orr, Brown & Duguid, Harper, Crawford, Barley, Beane) is that repair knowledge is narrative, social, embodied and situated. Diagnosis is largely sense-making on a particular machine in a particular setting, not a lookup. Official documentation always lags practice, and much of the real knowledge lives in communities of practice.
- That cuts both ways for the founder.
  - FOR C2: knowledge really is unevenly spread, and organisations capture it badly. Even within Xerox, field knowledge was "non-canonical" and invisible to HQ. Eureka showed that pooling field-validated knowledge has real dollar value (about $100M).
  - FOR a weak C3: LLMs are the first technology that can absorb narrative, war-story knowledge (text, chat, logs) at scale. Brynjolfsson et al. show novices close a big part of the gap (+34%) and keep the learning during outages.
  - AGAINST strong C3: Collins's somatic and collective tacit knowledge, and the TEA laser case, show that explicit representations do not carry hands-on and community knowledge. AI's jagged frontier (Dell'Acqua) is weakest on the off-script, context-specific faults that drive repair cost. Bainbridge and Beane warn that AI can deskill the next generation and cut off the apprenticeship that produces tacit knowledge.
  - AGAINST C4's order of magnitude: the best-measured interventions (Eureka; GenAI in support) give gains of single digits to about 35%, not 10x. And the knowledge layer is only part of repair cost and time. Parts, access, logistics, labour and certification don't vanish when knowledge gets cheap.
  - The C1 chain is too linear. Orr shows diagnosis, understanding, repair and customer management happen iteratively and at once. Narrative diagnosis loops: try a fix, observe, revise the story. "Return to service" is socially negotiated with the customer.
- On C7: ethnography suggests the inefficiency is less "built to be inefficient" and more the canonical/non-canonical gap. Firms design procedures for the predictable case, and the real work is improvisation that the formal system doesn't see or reward. That calls for tools that serve and capture practice, which Brown & Duguid argue, not a teardown of the people who hold the knowledge.

## Company-shape implications
- An AI "community of practice" layer: an LLM-native successor to Eureka for field technicians in a vertical (energy assets, industrial robots, HVAC, wind turbines). It captures war stories, chat, voice notes and fix outcomes, has peers validate them, and serves them to novices in the field. It rests on Brynjolfsson-style evidence plus the Eureka precedent.
- Tools for technicians, not tools to replace them: an assistant that makes the novice-to-expert curve steeper, plus remote expert video, rather than "no technician needed".
- Outcome-labelled repair data, linking symptom to tried fix to result, as the moat. Tacit knowledge becomes learnable only when outcomes are recorded. Most CMMS and service records lack this.
- Process-qualified small-batch part replication, which needs process know-how and certification, not just geometry from AI.
