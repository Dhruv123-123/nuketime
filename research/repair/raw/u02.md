## Findings

### 1. FOGBANK: the cleanest case of lost tacit knowledge in maintenance (hard data, history)
- Source: Wikipedia "Fogbank" (summarising NNSA / GAO / Los Alamos accounts), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fogbank
- By 2000 the process for making Fogbank (a material in W76/W78/W88 warheads) had been lost. Few records from the 1980s survived; most staff with production expertise had retired or left.
- Recreating it for the W76 life-extension (a refurbishment, that is, a repair/overhaul programme) took about 5 years and about $92M ($23M + $69M). The first refurbished warhead reached the Navy nearly a decade after the programme began; deliveries were about 2 years late.
- Key mechanism: the failures traced to an *impurity* in the original purification process. A later step depended on that impurity, and nobody had ever documented, tested or controlled it. The newer, cleaner process removed it and broke the product. The team had to put the impurity back deliberately.
- Why it matters: the missing knowledge was **not in any document**. People did not know they knew it. A model trained on the surviving records could not have recovered it; physical experimentation did. This supports C2 (knowledge loss costs a lot) and directly contradicts C3 and C5 (the "knowledge" is often embodied in a process, not in text).

### 2. F-35 sustainment: the OEM knowledge/data monopoly in military repair (regulator)
- Source: GAO-23-105341, "F-35 Aircraft: DOD and the Military Services Need to Reassess the Future Sustainment Strategy", https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- Mission capable rate about 55% (March 2023), well below goals. More than 10,000 components waiting for repair. Depot setup behind schedule.
- "Organizational-level maintenance suffers from a lack of technical data and training." DOD "relies heavily on its contractor to lead and manage F-35 sustainment" and has not identified or obtained the technical data needed.
- Life-cycle cost over $1.7T, of which about $1.3T is operating and sustainment.
- As of March 2026, 6 of 7 recommendations are still "Open – Partially Addressed". Handover of sustainment elements to the services now targets end of 2028.
- Why it matters: strong support for C2 in the military domain, but the monopoly is **contractual/legal (data rights)** as well as cognitive. AI does not grant data rights. This supports C7 (the structure, not just ignorance) and makes C3 mixed: the gap is a matter of access and rights.

### 3. Navy shipyard delays: workforce capacity and unplanned work, not just know-how (regulator)
- Source: GAO-20-588, "Naval Shipyards: Ongoing Challenges Could Jeopardize Navy's Ability to Improve Shipyard Operations", https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-588
- FY2015–2019: 38 of 51 carrier/submarine maintenance periods (75%) finished late, totalling 7,424 days of delay. Late periods averaged 113 days for carriers and 225 days for submarines.
- Causes: **unplanned work** (discovered after planning) contributed more than 4,100 days, and helped cause a 36% underestimate of personnel needed. **Workforce performance and capacity** (not enough people, or not enough productive people) contributed more than 4,000 days.
- Overtime in production shops averaged 25–32%, peaking near 45%, which itself reduces productivity.
- Source: GAO-19-229 (attack submarines), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-229. FY2008–2018: 10,363 days of idle time and maintenance delay. Over $1.5B spent crewing and supporting submarines that could not deploy. USS Boise sat idle more than 2 years waiting for a shipyard slot.
- Source: GAO-22-104510 (intermediate maintenance), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-104510. 191 of 414 submarine intermediate maintenance periods (46%) late, 2,525 days. Spending about $2.1B per year. Lessons were "fragmented" and not shared across the fleet (for example, a carrier parts-shortage working group's results were not shared).
- [background] Other GAO shipyard work (around 2019–2020) reported that a large share of the public shipyard workforce had less than 5 years of experience after the post-2013 hiring surge, and that inexperience lowered productivity. Not re-verified this session.
- Why it matters: in the largest maintenance organisation studied, the dominant delay drivers are **discovery of hidden work (diagnosis at the moment the system is opened up), labour capacity, facility/dock capacity, and parts**. Inexperience is a real contributor. Knowledge in the "manual" sense is not the binding constraint. Partial support for C2, and contradicts C4's premise that diagnose/understand/source/deliver dominate. Here the physical repair labour and dock slots dominate.

### 4. DoD depot infrastructure (regulator)
- Source: GAO-19-242, https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-242
- 12 of 21 depots had "poor" facility condition. Equipment past its expected useful life at 15 of 21. Over $13B invested FY2007–2017, yet performance declined. The services lacked data linking facility condition to delays (delay codes added only in 2019–2022).
- Why it matters: the system was not instrumented to know where its own time went. This supports C7 ("not built for smart") but in the sense of missing measurement and capital, not a knowledge monopoly.

### 5. Aviation maintenance workforce: aging and shortage (data, practitioner)
- Source: ATEC 2026 Pipeline Report, https://www.atec-amt.org/pipeline-report
- 44% of certificated mechanics are over 60 (up 3 points from 2025). About 39,000+ expected to retire in the next 10 years.
- Oliver Wyman (cited by ATEC): about 25,000 aviation technical personnel short in North America this year, including 9,100 certificated mechanics. Deficit projected at about 20,000 mechanics (12% of demand) by 2030.
- Counter-signal: the FAA issued a record 11,103 new mechanic certificates in 2025 (+23.2% vs 2024). The school pathway was 7,496 (+27%).
- Source: Boeing Pilot and Technician Outlook 2026–2045, https://www.boeing.com/commercial/market/pilot-technician-outlook. 728,000 new maintenance technicians needed globally over 20 years.
- Why it matters: the experience pyramid is inverting. New certificates do not equal experienced inspectors or troubleshooters. This supports C2 in the sense of experience loss. It also points to a market: tools that let a 2-year mechanic perform like a 20-year one.

### 6. AI as a tacit-knowledge diffuser: the best causal evidence (study)
- Source: Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond, "Generative AI at Work", NBER w31161, https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
- 5,179 customer-support agents, staggered rollout. Productivity (issues resolved per hour) +14% on average, **+34% for novice/low-skill workers**, and minimal effect for experienced workers. Mechanism: the assistant spreads the practices of top workers, helping novices "move down the experience curve."
- Why it matters: a direct analogue for capturing the knowledge of retiring experts. It supports a weak C3: AI compresses the novice-to-expert gap. But the magnitude (tens of percent, not 10x) and the domain (text conversations, not physical systems) contradict the strong C3 and C4. The gains also come from distilling experts' past *conversations*. In maintenance, most expert knowledge was never written down (see FOGBANK), so the training data does not exist yet unless someone captures it.

### 7. Boeing / Alaska 1282 door plug: process knowledge and records failing in a modern OEM (regulator)
- Source: NTSB DCA24MA063, https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/DCA24MA063.aspx
- Probable cause: Boeing's "failure to provide adequate training, guidance, and oversight" so personnel could consistently follow its parts-removal process. Contributing factor: ineffective FAA surveillance.
- Recommendations include a structured on-the-job training and qualification programme with grading (A-25-30), and archiving of training results (A-25-31).
- Why it matters: even the OEM (the supposed knowledge monopolist) failed to transmit procedural knowledge to its own floor. The knowledge existed. The **execution and record system** failed. This supports C6 (inspection and verification remain the safety backstop and need modernising) and complicates C2: the problem is the reliability of transmission and execution, not ownership.

### 8. Alaska Airlines 261: maintenance failure driven by incentives, not ignorance (regulator / history)
- Source: Wikipedia summary of NTSB AAR-02-01, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Airlines_Flight_261
- The airline progressively extended jackscrew lubrication and end-play check intervals (1985–1996) with FAA approval, on thin data. It used non-compliant home-made fixtures. A lubrication task estimated at 4 hours was done in about 1 hour. Wear ran at 0.012 in per 1,000 flight hours vs 0.001 expected. A mechanic's 1997 recommendation to replace the part was overruled by a supervisor. NTSB board member Goglia: "This is a maintenance accident." Causes were linked to cost-cutting.
- Why it matters: **contradicts C2/C3 as the main story** in this case. The knowledge existed, and a mechanic even flagged the problem. Organisational incentives, interval decisions and oversight failed. AI knowledge tools do not fix this. Supports C6 (independent inspection).

### 9. Davis-Besse: hidden degradation, the cost of a missed "notice" step, and falsified inspection (regulator / court / history)
- Source: Wikipedia, "Davis–Besse Nuclear Power Station" (summarising NRC/DOJ), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis%E2%80%93Besse_Nuclear_Power_Station
- Boric acid ate through more than 6 inches of carbon steel in the reactor head. Only about 3/8 in of cladding remained. Shutdown from 2002 to March 2004, with about $600M in repairs and upgrades (they used a replacement head from the abandoned Midland plant). The NRC fine was over $5M (a record). DOJ settlement $28M. Employees were indicted and some convicted for hiding corrosion from regulators. Deferrals had been granted on engineers' letters claiming inspections were adequate.
- Why it matters: the expensive failure was in **notice and inspection**, plus **honesty in the inspection record**, not in diagnosis knowledge. Boric acid corrosion was a known industry issue. It supports C6 strongly (inspection integrity is the crux) and contradicts the idea that knowledge is the bottleneck. Note also the sourcing: the fix depended on finding a compatible head from a mothballed plant. That is a C5-relevant point: a bespoke nuclear pressure-vessel part could not be "AI-fabricated". Qualified forging lead times are years.

### 10. Tacit knowledge theory (study / history)
- Source: Wikipedia "Tacit knowledge", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit_knowledge (Polanyi 1958/1966: "we can know more than we can tell"; Collins on the sapphire Q-factor measurement, which Western labs could not replicate from papers for about 20 years until scientists measured together in person).
- [background] Collins (1974) showed the TEA laser could not be built from published papers, only by labs that had personal contact with a working group. MacKenzie & Spinardi (1995, *American Journal of Sociology*), "Tacit Knowledge, Weapons Design, and the Uninvention of Nuclear Weapons", argued that nuclear weapon design depends on tacit knowledge that can decay if not practised.
- Why it matters: a 50-year research literature says the part of skill that is not in text transfers through apprenticeship and co-presence. LLMs act on text and images. This is the main theoretical argument against C3. A partial rebuttal: AI plus sensors (video, telemetry, wearable capture of expert work) may capture some of what was previously untransmittable. That is the opportunity, not a solved problem.

### 11. Knowledge-capture programmes in nuclear/utilities (practitioner, [background], not re-verified)
- IAEA guidance (for example, TECDOC-1510 "Knowledge Management for Nuclear Industry Operating Organizations", 2006) and the TVA-originated knowledge-loss risk assessment use a score of attrition risk (years to retirement) × position criticality (how unique or undocumented the knowledge is). This triages which experts to interview, video and pair with successors.
- US utilities (TVA, Exelon, Duke) ran these programmes widely in the 2000s–2010s ahead of the "great crew change". Published results are mostly process metrics (number of interviews, number of documents captured). **Rigorous outcome data (fewer errors, shorter outages attributable to capture) are scarce.** That gap is itself a finding: knowledge capture has been run as compliance or HR, rarely measured against maintenance KPIs.
- Industry practitioners consistently report that captured documents are underused because they are unsearchable and lack context. That is the precise layer where LLM retrieval adds value now.

### 12. The general shape of maintenance cost and time (synthesis)
- Across the GAO naval and depot data, the binding constraints are: (a) **discovery work found only when the system is opened** (inspection and diagnosis in situ), (b) skilled **labour capacity** and overtime, (c) **facility/dock capacity**, (d) parts, and (e) missing technical data or rights (F-35). Pure "what is wrong and how do I fix it" knowledge is one factor among several, and it is usually entangled with physical access.

## Stance reasoning
- C1 (universal chain): mostly supports the stages, but the GAO shipyard data show the chain is iterative. Diagnosis happens again during repair ("unplanned work" found after opening up, 4,100+ days). The stages loop rather than run linearly. Mixed.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly/gap): supports, with qualification. Knowledge loss is real and costly (FOGBANK $92M and about 2 years late; F-35 tech-data gaps; inverting mechanic age pyramid). But in several flagship failures (Alaska 261, Davis-Besse, Alaska 1282) the knowledge existed and incentives, execution, records or honesty failed. And the F-35 "monopoly" is a matter of contracts and data rights as much as cognition. Mixed leaning supports.
- C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicts the strong form. The best causal study shows +34% for novices in a text-native job, not elimination. Tacit knowledge that was never written down (FOGBANK impurity) has no training data. AI is a real partial lever for experience transfer, not "essentially gone".
- C4 (10x cost drop in the four steps): contradicts as stated. In the large datasets, labour capacity, docks and discovery dominate, and they are not in the four steps. No evidence of order-of-magnitude reductions from knowledge tools.
- C5 (bespoke parts solved): contradicts. FOGBANK shows that replicating a legacy material/process can take years even with full government resources. Davis-Besse needed a salvaged qualified forging.
- C6 (inspection essential, must evolve): supports. Davis-Besse (falsified/inadequate inspection), Alaska 261 (check intervals, fixtures), Alaska 1282 (records, qualification) all point to inspection, verification and records as the safety-critical layer, and FAA/NRC oversight was itself implicated.
- C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed. There is evidence of uninstrumented, capacity-starved, contractually locked systems (depots without delay codes until 2019–2022; F-35 contractor dependence). But the inefficiency looks like underinvestment, misaligned incentives and data rights, more than design for inefficiency. Safety regimes intentionally trade speed for assurance.
