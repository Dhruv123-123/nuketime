# v02: EU right-to-repair directive (2024) and ecodesign spare-parts/repair-information rules: what inefficiencies did the impact assessments quantify (costs, lifetimes, repair rates)?

Researched 2026-10-20. I read the primary documents directly where I could (Council register copies of the Commission's staff working documents, converted with pdftotext), and used secondary sources only where marked.

## Findings

### F1. Problem size: market failure of at least EUR 5.1 bn/yr (R2R IA, hard-ish model estimate)

The R2R IA (SWD(2023) 59, p.12) puts the market failure from premature disposal of repairable goods at **at least EUR 5.1 bn per year, or EUR 62 bn over 15 years**. The IA itself calls this "a conservative estimate".

- **Basis.** The value of repairable consumer durables is EUR 792 bn/yr at consumer prices. The estimate counts only the "reluctant replacers" (9.2% of consumers), people who tried to repair, failed and replaced. Cars are excluded.
- **Upper range.** The IA says a "more realistic" figure is EUR 5-25 bn/yr, which gives an NPV of EUR 62-307 bn over 15 years (footnote 33).

### F2. Products are used for a fraction of their design life (R2R IA, Table 3-1)

R2R IA footnote 19 compares how long products are actually used ("consumption lifetime") with their design ("absolute") lifetime:

| Product | Actual use (years) | Design life (years) |
|---|---|---|
| Phones | 1.7 | 7 |
| TVs | 7 | 22 |
| Refrigerators | 10 | 20 |
| Laptops | 4 | 7 |
| Jackets | 1 | 4 |
| Shoes | 3 | 5 |
| Cars | 11.5 | 18 |
| Wooden furniture | 10 | 22 |

This gap is the inefficiency the policy is aimed at. Most of it is behavioural or economic (premature replacement), not technical inability to repair.

### F3. Consumer segmentation and repair behaviour (R2R IA)

- **Segments.** 13.4% are enthusiastic repairers, 37.8% reluctant repairers, 9.2% reluctant replacers and **39.5% enthusiastic replacers**. The last group do not want repair; they want new goods.
- **Within the legal guarantee, most consumers choose replacement.** On average 64% choose replacement and 32% repair. By product: shoes and clothes 78%, smartphones 68%, refrigerators 60%, wooden furniture 56%, cars 45%.
- **Most defects fall outside the guarantee.** Examples of the share of consumers with a defect within a year, and the share of those covered by the guarantee: smartphones 5.5% / 1.9%, cars 10.36% / 1.9%. So the bulk of the repair decisions happen out of warranty.
- **Low confidence in repair.** Fewer than 30% of consumers think it more likely they would repair than replace. The IA expects the preference for new goods to persist for 15 years.

### F4. Ranked drivers in the IA: price and hassle first, information second (R2R IA, section 2)

The IA names six drivers:

1. Legal-guarantee rules let consumers choose replacement.
2. Lack of transparency on the availability and conditions of repair (the information driver).
3. Inconvenience and hassle.
4. Repair is not economically attractive.
5. Products are not designed to be reparable. Out of scope here; left to ESPR, ECGT and the Design Directive.
6. Lifestyle and preference for new goods.

The numbers behind them:

- **Price is the top factor** (8.2/10). The acceptable repair price is about **20% of the purchase price** (range 17-27%). Between 25% (clothing) and 50% (dishwashers) did not repair because it would be too expensive (2018 Behavioural Study).
- **Diagnosis is a cost and uncertainty point in its own right.** "Finding out how much repair will cost can be difficult as it often requires the repairer to carry out a diagnostic... consumers need to first pay the diagnostic... Due to uncertainty about these upfront costs many consumers abandon the option of repair." Diagnostic costs for the most common defects run from **7% (shoes) to 22% (cars)** (Business Survey, p.38).
- **Information and search costs are real but secondary to price.** For example, 19% of smartphone owners did not repair because they could not find information on how to get it repaired, or no service was available.
- **Hassle and logistics.** Among smartphone owners who did not repair: 9% said it was too much effort to deliver the phone to a shop, 14% said repair takes too long, and 10% said there was no replacement phone during the repair. For fridges and TVs, 14% cited transport effort. The IA notes that "time is even longer where spare parts need to be ordered."
- **Trust in repair quality** scores 8.2/10, comparable to price.
- **Irreparable by design.** 25% of TV owners and 37% of refrigerator owners had to replace because the product was irreparable. ECOS (2021) says about 60% of discarded items were not durable or not designed to be repaired.
- **Repair Café barriers.** The top technical barriers from 1,582 observations were "no spare parts available", "irreparable" and "unable to open".

**Takeaway.** The IA treats information as one of several barriers. Price relative to a new product, hassle and time, trust, design and taste are equally or more important. The IA explicitly says "It is not in the realm of this initiative to influence factors that have an effect on prices."

### F5. How much the preferred package moves the needle (R2R IA)

Each option was estimated to raise repair take-up by single digits to low tens of percent:

| Option | Increase in repair take-up | Avoided purchases over 15 yrs | Consumer savings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repair quote (PO6B) | 13.4% | 167.9 m units | EUR 70.5 bn |
| Obligation to repair, all goods (PO6D) | 15.2% | 190.5 m units | EUR 79.9 bn |
| Platform options | about 6.7% | — | — |

The package as a whole is estimated to deliver over 15 years:

- **18.4 Mt CO2 saved.** ESPR is estimated at 471 Mt and ECGT at 0.33-0.47 Mt.
- **EUR 176.5 bn in consumer savings.**
- **EUR 4.8-4.9 bn in GVA gains for repairers.**
- **Business losses.** The IA frames these as a transfer from producer revenue to consumer welfare.

Note what the EUR 176.5 bn is made of. It comes mostly from consumers buying fewer new goods. It is **not** a reduction in the cost of performing a repair.

### F6. Baseline repair success rates (R2R IA)

- **Professional repairers.** At least 58% of products brought to repairers were fixed in 2021. The rate was 54.74% in 2014 and 57.72% in 2021, about +0.28 percentage points a year (Sharepair data across 40 goods).
- **Repair cafés.** The average success rate was 63% overall and 85% for non-electronic products (Repair Monitor 2019).
- **Manufacturer repair.** 91% of repair requests to manufacturers resulted in successful repair in 2018.

Success rates are moving slowly. **Interpretation:** about 40% of attempted repairs fail, and the cause is mostly physical, parts or design, not unknown know-how.

### F7. Smartphones: lifetimes, repair rates, repair costs (smartphone IA, SWD(2023) 101)

- **Active use lifetime.** About 3 years for smartphones and about 5 for tablets. The Kantar replacement cycle rose from 23.4 to 26.2 months between 2016 and 2018.
- **Few broken phones get repaired.** In Germany, 59% buy a new phone when the old one breaks and only 11% try to repair it. In Austria, only 34% of owners of a defective phone tried repair; of those, 43% were beyond repair, 31% reparable and 26% "did not know how to repair it". Net, **about 10% of defective phones are actually repaired**. Yet 77% say they would prefer repair (Eurobarometer).
- **Repair cost relative to a new device.** Across 52 smartphones and 15 tablets:
  - A display replacement costs **42% of the average new-smartphone price** and 37% for tablets.
  - A battery replacement costs about 14% (smartphone) and 21% (tablet).
  - Willingness to pay for repair is about 27% of the new price and falls about 6.7% a year. Only 26% of Germans would pay more than 30% of the new price to repair a one-year-old phone.
- **OEM restriction mechanisms named in the IA** (citing the FTC's *Nixing the Fix*, 2021: "scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions"):
  - design that does not prioritise repair;
  - spare parts and repair information restricted to OEM networks;
  - impeding non-OEM parts;
  - software locks and parts pairing;
  - IP rights.

  On software, the IA notes: "Specific software is then required to calibrate repaired or replaced components and is typically only available to authorised repairers."
- **Modelled policy effect:**
  - Smartphone lifetime goes from **3.1 years (baseline) to about 4.14-4.26 years**. Tablets go from 5 to about 6.1 years.
  - Device price rises about 1% (mid-range from EUR 500 to EUR 504).
  - Annual spend per typical consumer falls from EUR 170 to EUR 127-129.
  - EU consumer expenditure falls by about **EUR 18-19.5 bn a year by 2030**, against a baseline of about EUR 80 bn.
  - Business revenue falls about 24-26%.
  - A battery removable without tools cuts battery repair cost by EUR 5-30.
  - A repairability score adds only about **1 month** of lifetime.
- **Jobs in repair and refurbishment.** Phones: from 22,700 (no action) to about 25,450-25,600 in 2030. Tablets: from 7,350 to 7,600-7,700.
- **Spare parts are a supply-chain risk for OEMs.** "OEMs depend on the continued availability of spare parts or have to stock spare parts."
- **What the adopted Regulation (EU) 2023/1670 requires** (secondary sources: Commission press release IP/23/3315; EUReporter, June 2025):
  - critical spare parts available within **5-10 working days**, for **7 years** after end of sales;
  - **5 years** of operating-system updates;
  - batteries rated for 800 cycles at 80% capacity.

### F8. Large appliances: white-goods ecodesign of 2019 (EU package; UK IA as proxy)

- **Spare parts.** Parts must be available for 7-10 years and delivered within 15 working days. Repair information is reserved for professional repairers, who may have to register (Regulation 2019/2023 and others).
- **Delivery time criticised.** ANEC said the 3-week maximum delivery time "would surely be too long for a family with a broken washing machine" (WEF/BEUC 2019 coverage).
- **Resource savings were never quantified.** The UK IA (paragraph 92): "The overall savings of resource efficiency requirements however were not quantified. These savings were assessed qualitatively and predicted to be modest in comparison to the respective energy savings." The package's headline benefit was 167 TWh/yr of energy savings, not repair.
- **France reference.** Repairs rose **15-20%** after France's 2014 Consumption Law, which required sellers to disclose how long spare parts are available (UK IA, paragraph 96).
- **The UK IA's logic** follows the knowledge-monopoly framing: parts and information access "should help independent repairers to overcome barriers currently limiting their capability to compete". But it gives no number for the effect.

### F9. ESPR (2024): reparability savings could not be quantified ex ante (secondary)

The REFIT Scoreboard says it was "not possible to quantify the savings as they will depend on the number and type of products covered". https://op.europa.eu/webpub/com/refit-scoreboard/en/policy/7/7-32.html

The R2R IA nonetheless credits ESPR with 471 Mt CO2 over 15 years.

### F10. Evidence after the rules: France's repair bonus and repairability index (secondary)

- **Bonus réparation, year 1 (2023).** About 165,000 repairs and EUR 4 m paid, against about 500,000 expected.
- **Late 2023 to late 2024.** 574,163 repairs. Phone repairs grew +369% and laptop repairs +603%, after the screen-break threshold was changed.
- **Fund use.** Only **19% of the 2024 repair-fund budget** was used. Sources: CLCV observatory, Senate question 2025, Le Monde via reporting.

  https://www.senat.fr/questions/base/2025/qSEQ250303925.html
  https://www.clcv.org/storage/app/media/Bilan%20fonds%20r%C3%A9paration%202025.pdf
- **Repairability index.** Scores are self-declared by manufacturers. HOP (2024) found score inflation, and no controls or sanctions had yet been carried out. https://www.halteobsolescence.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Synthese-rapport-indice-de-reparabilite.pdf
- **Interpretation.** Even subsidised, demand for repair responds weakly. Price thresholds and convenience dominate.

### F11. Visible car spare parts: the design-rights "repair clause"

The design-law reform (Council vote October 2024) removes design protection for visible must-match spare parts, but with a long transition. ECAR, an aftermarket lobby, estimates EUR 450-720 m/yr in consumer savings; this is advocacy, not Commission data. The Commission's 2004 IA found prices of visible parts were higher in member states that kept design protection.

https://www.globalfleet.com/en/financial-models/europe/features/eu-opens-spare-car-parts-market-competition
https://ec.europa.eu/smart-regulation/impact/ia_carried_out/docs/ia_2004/sec_2004_1097_en.pdf

This is a pure parts-monopoly example: a legal monopoly over parts, distinct from knowledge.

### F12. Critique that the directive left OEM control intact (academic opinion)

A University of Santiago de Compostela paper argues that manufacturers secured a narrow right to repair that "left control of the emerging repair market" with them. It says the directive does not effectively help independent repairers overcome access barriers. EESC also urged a ban on serialisation and parts pairing.

https://investigacion.usc.gal/documentos/66ec64adda80f2183ed74e5c?lang=en

The final Directive 2024/1799 does prohibit contractual, hardware or software techniques that impede repair, and requires spare parts at a reasonable price. Enforcement is untested; transposition is due by 31 July 2026.

## Reasoning and stance notes

- **C1 (universal repair chain).** The IAs implicitly use a chain: defect, decide repair or replace, find a repairer, diagnosis/quote, parts, repair, return. But the dominant step is the **repair-or-replace decision**, which the chain omits. Most "inefficiency" is that the chain is never entered: 39.5% are enthusiastic replacers, and about 10% of broken phones are repaired. Verdict: mixed.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly).** Supported in part:
  - The smartphone IA names restriction of repair information, diagnostics and calibration software to authorised networks, plus parts pairing.
  - Of Austrians with broken phones, 26% "did not know how to repair".
  - Ecodesign rules exist precisely to open up repair info and parts.

  But the R2R IA ranks price relative to a new product, hassle and time, trust, irreparable design and taste as equal or bigger drivers. And the monopoly is as much about **parts and software locks** (legal and technical) as about knowledge. Verdict: mixed.
- **C3 (AI removes the knowledge gap).** The IAs say nothing about AI. Indirectly, AI cannot unlock parts pairing, OEM-only calibration software, IP restrictions or missing parts, so the gap is not "gone". Verdict: contradicts, weakly and indirectly.
- **C4 (an order of magnitude cheaper).** The IA's own interventions move repair take-up by only 2-21%. Repair cost is set by labour, parts and design. A display repair at 42% of the device price is mostly the part plus labour, not diagnosis or knowledge. Diagnosis is 7-22% of the cost of common repairs, so making knowledge free cannot by itself bring repair under the roughly 20% price threshold. Verdict: contradicts, partially.

  Even a 10x reduction in diagnosis and sourcing cost would not cross most consumers' thresholds for cheap goods. It could matter much more where labour and downtime dominate, such as industrial assets.
- **C5 (an AI parts factory).** The IAs do not cover bespoke fabrication. Parts availability is handled through mandatory stocking for 7-10 years. "No spare parts available" is a top Repair Café barrier, which signals demand, but there is no evidence that on-demand fabrication solves it. Verdict: silent, with a weak signal of demand.
- **C6 (inspection and certification).** Mostly silent. Trust in repair quality (8.2/10) and the "easy repair standard" are relevant. The French index's self-declared, unverified scores show a verification gap. Verdict: mixed to weakly supportive (verification is the weak link).
- **C7 (built to be inefficient).** Supported:
  - The IAs say the dominant business model "relies on higher sales of new goods".
  - Producers have "little incentive" to change.
  - The policy itself quantifies business losses of 24-26% of smartphone revenue, an incentive against repair.
  - The FTC finds little justification for restrictions.

  But the regulators chose incremental fixes, not a rebuild, and consumer preference (39.5% enthusiastic replacers) is a demand-side cause, not only structural. Verdict: supports, with caveats.

## Where the money and time go (per these IAs)

- **Consumer goods.** The decision is driven by the repair price relative to a new product (acceptable at about 20%). Repair cost is dominated by **parts and labour**. Displays are 42% of a new phone's price; diagnosis alone is 7-22% of the cost of common repairs.
- **Time and logistics.** Transport and drop-off, waiting for parts (up to 15 working days for white goods; 5-10 days for phones) and loss of the product during repair each dissuade 9-14% of non-repairers.
- **Information search.** This is a measurable but smaller share, about 19% for smartphones (finding a repairer).
- **Upfront uncertainty.** Diagnosis must be paid before the price is known.
