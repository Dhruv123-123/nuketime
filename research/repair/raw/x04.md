## Analysis: why marketplaces earn thin margins (evidence-based reasoning)
1. **They resell commodity machine time.** Xometry's spread of about 35% marketplace GM must pay for about 18% of revenue in S&M, plus ops/support, quality escapes, and repricing risk. The 88% GM ad/SaaS line is shrinking. Adj. EBITDA is about 3-6%.
2. **The knowledge/quoting layer is replicable.** Instant quoting was Protolabs' moat in 2016 (56% GM, 20% operating margin). By 2025, after rivals and networks copied it, Protolabs had 44.5% GM and a 4.7% operating margin. Xometry's own 10-K warns AI could make its marketplace "uneconomical or obsolete". When knowledge gets cheap, its margin goes to buyers, not to the platform.
3. **Supply side fragmentation is a feature for buyers, not leverage for the broker.** There are over 500K North American suppliers on Thomasnet, and Xometry uses about 5K of them. Suppliers multi-home, and the platform carries the price risk ("quote buyers prices lower than the cost we agree with a supplier").
4. **Value accrues where accountability sits.** Repeat enterprise accounts make up 98% of revenue, and Protolabs keeps moving upmarket on revenue per contact. Buyers pay for liability, quality certification (AS9100, ITAR/EAR) and dependable delivery more than for discovery.
5. **The capital is going into owned automated capacity (Hadrian, Divergent), not brokerage.** Investors are betting that the scarce layer is qualified, certified, automated production capacity in the US/defense, not information.

## Spares / repair share of demand
- **No marketplace discloses a spares/MRO share.** Xometry's 10-K does not mention MRO, aftermarket or spares. Protolabs mentions "end-of-life replacement" and "end-of-life production support" as use cases, without numbers. Demand on these platforms is dominated by prototyping, bridge and low-volume production for product development (medical, electronics, industrial machinery, aerospace, auto).
- Inference: the "AI parts factory for obsolete parts" demand is real but anecdotal and unmeasured on the largest platforms. For regulated assets (aerospace, defense, rail, nuclear), the binding constraint on a replacement part is qualification and certification, not making it. Examples are FAA PMA/DER approval and the OEM-controlled technical data package. Hadrian's emphasis on automating inspection, and Fictiv's on EAR compliance, point the same way.

## Stances
- C1: silent.
- C2: mixed. Fragmentation and opaque quoting were a real inefficiency, and Protolabs/Xometry monetised fixing them. But the evidence shows that once fixed, the knowledge layer stops earning rents.
- C3: mixed, leaning contradicts on the economics. AI quoting exists and scales (Xometry +41% growth). But "the gap is gone" means the margin is gone too. It does not remove the remaining bottlenecks: certification, capacity, quality liability.
- C4: mixed. Sourcing friction did drop a lot (instant quotes versus multi-week RFQs). Xometry is about $0.9B run-rate against a $275B TAM, under 0.5% penetration after 13 years. The physical make-and-ship cost is not dropping by an order of magnitude.
- C5: contradicts. Making a bespoke part is commoditised capacity, so it is solved-ish for non-critical parts. For critical parts the unsolved problem is drawings/IP plus qualification. No platform reports a meaningful obsolete-spares business.
- C6: supports (indirect). Hadrian automates inspection. Buyers concentrate spend on vendors that carry certs and compliance (AS9100, EAR/ITAR), and Fictiv sells export-control compliance as a service.
- C7: mixed. Fragmented regional job shops are inefficient, but a decade of digital rebuild yielded thin-margin brokers. The money now goes to owned automated factories, which suggests rebuilding capacity rather than brokering information.

## Surprises
- Protolabs' operating margin fell from 20% (2018) to 4.7% (2025) while digital quoting went mainstream. That is the clearest data point that cheap manufacturing knowledge destroys incumbents' rents rather than creating new ones.
- Xometry's high-margin ads/SaaS segment is shrinking while low-margin brokerage grows.
- Fictiv, the best-funded independent broker, sold to a components distributor (MISUMI) for an undisclosed price.
- None of the leading on-demand platforms report spares/repair as a segment.
