## Findings (verified from fetched sources)

### F1. WWII Red Ball Express: throughput was limited by truck attrition, tires and parts, not by diagnosis
Source: Ruppenthal, *Logistical Support of the Armies, Vol. II*, ch. V "Transportation Developments" (US Army CMH official history), https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-E-Logistics2/USA-E-Logistics2-5.html
- 25 Aug to 16 Nov 1944 (81 days): 412,193 tons carried, 5,088 tons/day on average. In late September about 5,400 trucks were assigned, and 1,542 were dispatched daily carrying an average of 8,209 tons. The average round trip was 714 miles and took 71.2 hours.
- Repairs rose to **1,500 per day** by the end of September 1944.
- The root problem identified was **poor first- and second-echelon (operator and unit) preventive maintenance**, tied to driver fatigue and weak discipline, together with speeding, overloading and reckless driving.
- **Accidents caused about one third of all vehicle replacement issues, more than mechanical failures did.**
- Maintenance equipment could not keep up with the repair workload. A general lack of spare parts kept vehicles deadlined, and tire reserves ran out.
- White Ball route: only 32 of 48 vehicles per company were fit for use, against 40 expected. At the end of 1944, only about 75% of authorized vehicles were available.
- Replacement factor for 2.5-ton trucks: the recommendation was to raise it from 2% to 8%.
Reading: diagnosis was cheap, since a broken truck is obvious. The binding constraints were (a) operator behaviour and preventive maintenance, (b) parts and tires, and (c) repair capacity.

### F2. ETO late 1944: about 15,000 deadlined vehicles, blamed on spare parts and repair facilities; tires as a theatre-level bottleneck
Source: Ruppenthal, Vol. II, ch. VIII "Supplying the Armies: Equipment", https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-E-Logistics2/USA-E-Logistics2-8.html
- About 100 2.5-ton trucks were pulled off Red Ball every day for repair. About **15,000 vehicles were deadlined in November 1944**, "attributed to lack of spare parts and inadequate maintenance and repair facilities."
- Tires: the War Department replacement factor of 7.5% was far too low. By early September, 70% of Advance Section trucks had run about 10,000 miles, against an expected tire life of about 12,000. COMZ projected a **deficit of at least 250,000 tires** by the end of January 1945. About 1,000 vehicles were deadlined for lack of tires by early December. **About 40% of tire replacement need came from underinflation, overloading or other abuse.** Retreading was limited by a shortage of camelback (retread rubber).
- Local "bespoke" production was started under pressure: Goodrich in Paris made about 4,000 tires a month and Michelin about 2,000 a month from January 1945, but the effort was threatened by a carbon black shortage. Local manufacturing ran into upstream raw-material limits.
- Upstream production limits included foundry capacity for axles, transmissions and engines. Shipping limits: 75 loaded ships for 14 berths (end of September) and 80 for 18 (end of October).
- Tanks: the M4 replacement factor was raised step by step from 7% to 20%. December 1944 First Army medium tank losses were nearly 400. By the end of November there was no true reserve (3,344 tanks on hand against a T/O&E of 3,409 plus a 937 reserve requirement). Emergency transfers covered the gap: 75 tanks from the Mediterranean, 351 from 21 Army Group, and 1,010 from the US.
Reading: in mass mechanised war, the expensive stages were parts supply, raw materials, port and transport capacity, and preventive discipline. "Understanding" was not scarce. Local manufacture helped only at the margin, and only when the materials were available.

### F3. Cannibalization is routine and costly: 850,000 cannibalizations and 5.3 million extra maintenance hours (FY1996-2000)
Source: GAO-02-86, *Military Aircraft: Services Need Strategies to Reduce Cannibalizations* (Nov 2001), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-02-86 (PDF: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-02-86.pdf)
- Navy and Air Force reported about **850,000 cannibalizations requiring about 5.3 million additional maintenance hours** in FY1996-2000. Navy data were reportedly understated by up to 50%, and the Army did not collect servicewide figures.
- Navy policy notes that a cannibalization **doubles labour and documentation**: two removals and two installs instead of one.
- Over half of maintainers reported working more than 50 hours a week. Cannibalization hurt morale and reenlistment.
- Main causes: spare parts shortages, high operating tempo, aging fleets. Another cause was speed: it is sometimes faster to cannibalize than to wait for a part from across the base or town.
- **"As many as half of all Navy cannibalizations are not caused by supply system problems"** (Worra, NPS thesis, 2000).
- **Key diagnostic point:** "A Navy study also found that cannibalizations are sometimes done because mechanics are not trained well enough to diagnose problems or because testing equipment is either not available or not working. In these cases, parts are swapped from one aircraft to another until the problem is solved."
Reading: this is the cleanest military evidence that **poor diagnosis multiplies parts demand and labour**. Swap-to-diagnose turns a knowledge gap into a parts and labour cost, which partly supports C2 and the value of cheaper diagnosis in C4. It also shows the military accepts doubled labour to avoid parts waits, so the parts lead time sets the price of the workaround.

### F4. No Fault Found (NFF) costs DoD more than $2B a year
Source: Wikipedia "No fault found", citing Werner, *Aerospace America* (AIAA, Feb 2015), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_fault_found
- NFF (a part is removed as faulty, then tests good at the shop) "is thought to cost the US DoD in excess of US$2 billion per year." This is a secondary citation.
- **[unverified, from memory]** Published aerospace NFF rates for removed avionics LRUs are often 30-60%.
Reading: misdiagnosis at the flight line sends good parts through the repair pipeline. Better diagnosis here directly cuts parts, shipping and depot cost (supports C4 in part).

### F5. F-35: a real knowledge monopoly, enforced by contract and IP, not by cognition
Source: GAO-23-105341, *F-35 Aircraft: DOD and the Military Services Need to Reassess the Future Sustainment Strategy* (Sep 2023), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- "At the outset of the program, DOD decided not to procure technical data from the prime contractor." As a result, the prime and subcontractors "were left with significant control over and involvement in the program's sustainment."
- Maintainers at all three sites visited said missing technical data stops them making repairs. "The contractor and the original equipment manufacturer of specific components control the technical data necessary to understand, troubleshoot and repair many of the F-35's components."
- The main reason an aircraft is non-mission-capable for maintenance is that complex troubleshooting is "not covered in technical data provided to maintainers." Units must call contractor field service representatives for what maintainers called "routine troubleshooting."
- Example: on a deployment, an ejection seat issue required **helicoptering an OEM contractor representative to the ship**. This is close to the founder's airliner AOG example.
- Answers to technical data requests take up to 60 days. Complex issues need multiple requests and take 60+ days. All installations surveyed relied to some extent on contractors just to get **part numbers**, and 7 of 14 had to ask the local contractor rep.
- Depot Component Maintenance Manuals "are ambiguous and rarely are detailed enough for depot personnel to make the repair... depot personnel not only cannot fix the part, but they cannot learn and understand how to fix the part."
- Subcontractors refuse to pass proprietary data through the prime for competitive reasons. These disputes pushed some depot workload activations out to 2028.
- Numbers: mission capable rate about 55% (March 2023). Non-mission-capable supply rose from about 17% (FY2020) to about 27% (March 2023), and the program office had funded only enough spares for about 15% because lower was "not affordable." Non-mission-capable maintenance exceeded its 10% goal by about 5 points.
- Depot repair cycle time: 172 days (2017), 131 days (August 2020), 141 days (February 2023), against a 60-90 day goal. The **primary impediment was lack of repair material** (parts and piece-parts), not knowledge alone.
- **Organic military depots repaired components in about 72 days, more than twice as fast as OEMs**, and nearly half met a 30-day target. Even so, **73% of components still went back to the OEM**, because organic capability had not been stood up, mostly due to data rights and activation delays.
- More than 10,000 components were awaiting repair (March 2023), up from about 4,300 (April 2019). The program office bought new parts instead of repairing ones it already had.
- Lifecycle cost is more than $1.7 trillion, of which about $1.3 trillion is operations and sustainment.
Reading: **strong support for C2** on a flagship modern system. But the monopoly is **legal and contractual**: drawings, tech data, part numbers, software. AI cannot infer data it cannot see, and the gap only closes if data rights are bought or forced. This **contradicts C3** ("essentially gone"). Even with knowledge in hand, material shortage and depot capacity still bind.

### F6. F-35 sustainment costs are rising, not falling; recommendations go unimplemented
Source: GAO-24-106703 (April 2024), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106703
- Sustainment estimate rose 44%, from $1.1T (2018) to $1.58T (2023). Air Force cost per tail-year is $6.6M, against an original target of $4.1M. Availability has trended down for 5 years, and no variant meets its goals.
- GAO has made 43 recommendations since 2014, and 30 (about 70%) are unimplemented.
Reading: supports C7, structural inertia. Institutions, contracts and incentives change slowly even when the problem is well diagnosed.

### F7. F-35 ALIS: digital records and traceability as a bottleneck (inspection and certification layer)
Source: GAO-20-316, *F-35 Sustainment: DOD Needs to Cut Billions in Estimated Costs to Achieve Affordability* / ALIS findings (2020), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-316
- DoD IG (June 2019): of 263 spare parts delivered to one site in June 2018, **213 (81%) arrived without the required electronic records**, so they could not be installed with confidence.
- One location had aircraft **grounded 9,262 hours (9% of possible flight hours) in FY2019 due to unresolved ALIS action requests**, mainly from missing or inaccurate electronic parts records. Another site lost 2,200 grounded hours in 6 months waiting for contractors to resolve parts-related action requests.
- Users spent 5,000-10,000 hours a year at one location manually tracking data that ALIS should have captured.
- Fleet-wide, 16,221 non-mission-capable hours (about 2%) were directly attributable to ALIS issues.
Reading: **supports C6**. In safety-critical repair, the part plus its pedigree (records, airworthiness traceability) is the deliverable. Paperwork failures ground aircraft even when the physical part is on the shelf. This is a software and data problem that AI could plausibly help with.

### F8. Fleet-wide: 4 of 49 aircraft types met mission-capable goals in most years; parts, maintenance and aging are the causes
Source: GAO-23-106217, *Weapon System Sustainment: Aircraft Mission Capable Goals Were Generally Not Met...* (Nov 2022), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106217
- Only **4 of 49** aircraft types met their annual mission-capable goal in a majority of FY2011-2021, and 26 never met it. In FY2021 only 2 of 49 did.
- Causes cited: parts and supply problems, maintenance challenges, aging aircraft. Maintenance costs as a share of operating and support costs rose by $1.2B since FY2011. Operating and support costs were about $54B in FY2020.
Reading: the underperformance is chronic and systemic (supports C7). The causes named are parts and supply and the maintenance workforce, not diagnosis as such.

### F9. Navy submarines: depot capacity and workforce, not knowledge, cost 10,363 idle days and $1.5B
Source: GAO-19-229, *Military Readiness: Analysis of Maintenance Delays Needed to Improve Availability of Navy Attack Submarines* (Nov 2018), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-229
- FY2008-2018: **10,363 days of idle time and maintenance delays**, and **more than $1.5B** spent supporting submarines with no operational capability while waiting.
- Causes: public shipyards running above capacity, workforce shortages, facility needs, and no business case for public versus private workload split. USS *Boise* sat pierside for over two years.
Reading: **contradicts C4's implicit model** that diagnosis and understanding dominate. In heavy industrial repair (ships, subs, airframes), the scarce layer is **physical repair capacity**: dry docks, skilled trades, facilities and scheduling. The Navy knows exactly what work is needed and lacks the capacity to do it.

### F10. DoD institutional IP and technical data gap is three decades old
Source: GAO-22-104752, *Defense Acquisitions: DOD Should Take Additional Actions to Improve How It Approaches Intellectual Property* (2021-22), https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-104752
- DoD "often does not acquire the IP it needs to operate and maintain" systems. GAO has reported for about 30 years that insufficient IP reduces readiness and raises costs.
- DoD funded only 5 IP Cadre positions in OSD through FY2023 and published an IP guide in May 2025. Training guidance was due by 30 October 2026.
Reading: supports C2, since the knowledge monopoly is structural and long-lived. The fix is legal and acquisition policy (data rights at contract award), not AI inference. That partly contradicts C3.

### F11. Cannibalization as a parts strategy for obsolete platforms (diminishing manufacturing sources)
Source: Wikipedia "Cannibalization (parts)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannibalization_(parts)
- HMS *Eagle* was kept after decommissioning (1972) as a spares source for *Ark Royal*. USS *Kitty Hawk* was kept running with parts from the decommissioned USS *Independence*. Lifetime buys are the standard hedge against diminishing manufacturing sources.
Reading: when the supplier is gone, the military default is cannibalization or a lifetime buy, not bespoke manufacture. That shows the gap C5 targets is real. Militaries have not considered it solved.

## Background evidence [unverified, from memory; could not fetch in this session]
- **Soviet WWII repair:** Soviet tank repair services carried out on the order of 400,000+ tank repairs in 1941-45. Most "lost" tanks were damaged rather than destroyed and were returned to service, so forward recovery and repair supplied a large share of tank strength. Lesson: field repair with simple, standardised designs (T-34) and interchangeable parts beat elaborate diagnostics. Design for repairability sat upstream of everything.
- **US WWII ordnance:** US doctrine was echelon-based (1st-5th echelon maintenance). Ordnance medium and heavy maintenance companies and evacuation units recovered and rebuilt tanks behind the lines. A large share of knocked-out Shermans were repaired and returned. Later analyses credit the size and mobility of the recovery and repair train over "knowledge."
- **Israel 1973:** IDF field repair teams returned a large fraction of knocked-out tanks to battle within about 24 hours, which is widely cited as a decisive factor on the Golan. This is the origin of modern BDAR thinking. Of roughly 1,000 Israeli tanks put out of action, most were repaired. Wikipedia gives 400-800 destroyed and about 600 damaged or captured (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War).
- **Modern BDAR doctrine (US ATP 4-31):** in combat, assessment uses simplified decision trees and checklists so crews and mechanics can decide in minutes whether to fix forward, recover or cannibalize. Expedient repairs are temporary, flagged, and must be inspected and replaced with standard repairs later. Doctrine treats safety re-inspection as a separate, mandatory step (relevant to C1 and C6). Speed comes from **lowering the standard temporarily** and **delegating authority**, not only from better diagnosis.
- **Ukraine 2022-25:** the US and allies set up remote maintenance support (a Remote Maintenance and Distribution Cell in Poland). US maintainers coached Ukrainian soldiers through repairs over video calls and chat, using manuals and photos. Repair hubs in Poland, Slovakia, Romania and Germany handled heavy repair. This is the closest real analog to AI-assisted remote diagnosis: remote expertise worked well for field-level fixes. Heavy damage (barrels, engines, transmissions) still needed hubs, spare parts and long transport legs, and parts commonality across dozens of donated platforms was a major problem.
- **Additive manufacturing in the US military:** the Air Force Rapid Sustainment Office and the Navy and Marines use 3D printing and reverse engineering for obsolete parts. Widely reported examples include a C-5 lavatory cover and B-52 and KC-135 parts. Non-structural parts come fast, but flight-critical parts require **qualification and certification that takes months to years** and is often the binding cost (partly contradicts C5, supports C6).

## Synthesis: diagnosis vs parts vs logistics
1. **Mechanically simple, high-volume systems (WWII trucks and tanks):** diagnosis is nearly free. Cost and time go to **parts and consumables (tires), transport and port capacity, repair labour and facilities, and operator abuse and preventive maintenance**. About a third of truck losses were accidents, and about 40% of tire demand was abuse. The biggest lever was not repair at all: it was **prevention and design**.
2. **Complex electronic and software systems (modern aircraft):** diagnosis becomes costly. Swap-to-diagnose cannibalization and NFF (more than $2B a year to DoD) show misdiagnosis multiplying parts and labour. Here the knowledge gap is real, but it is largely **an IP and data-rights gap** (F-35 tech data, part numbers, manuals). AI cannot fully close it without the data.
3. **Heavy industrial assets (ships, submarines, depot-level airframes):** the work is known. The bottleneck is **depot capacity, skilled workforce, facilities and scheduling** (10,363 idle days, $1.5B).
4. **Even with knowledge, parts bind:** F-35 depot cycle time is driven primarily by "lack of repair material." Organic depots were already 2x faster than OEMs once they had the data and material.
5. **Records and certification are a hidden bottleneck:** 81% of F-35 spares arrived without electronic records, and 9% of flight hours were lost at one site to records issues. The pedigree of a part is part of the part.
6. **Military workarounds for parts scarcity:** cannibalization (doubles labour), lifetime buys, local manufacture (WWII Paris tires, modern additive manufacturing), and temporary BDAR standards. Each trades one cost for another. None is free.

## Stance notes
- C1 (universal chain): mostly supported. Doctrine has distinct assess, recover/evacuate, diagnose, parts, repair, inspect and return steps. But the military adds **recovery and evacuation** as a step and **prevention/operator care** before "notice." BDAR also shows the chain is often deliberately **short-circuited** (temporary fix now, full repair and inspection later), so it is not a fixed linear sequence.
- C2 (knowledge monopoly): supported for modern complex systems (F-35, IP gap for 30 years, part numbers held by contractors). Contradicted for simple and heavy systems, where the binding constraints are parts, labour and capacity.
- C3 (AI removes the gap): contradicted or mixed. The F-35 monopoly is contractual and IP-based. AI cannot read drawings it does not have, and subcontractors deliberately withhold data. Remote expert coaching in Ukraine shows knowledge transfer can be cheap at field level, a plausible AI analog.
- C4 (10x on four stages): mixed. Better diagnosis would cut swap-to-diagnose and NFF waste, but parts and material shortages, depot and shipyard capacity, and transport dominate time in most of the evidence. Physical delivery and capacity do not drop 10x because of AI.
- C5 (bespoke part factory solved): contradicted. WWII local tire production hit raw-material limits. Modern additive manufacturing hits qualification and certification limits. Militaries still rely on cannibalization and lifetime buys.
- C6 (inspection essential and must evolve): supported. BDAR requires re-inspection of expedient repairs, and F-35 records and traceability failures ground aircraft.
- C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed, leaning supports. Chronic missed mission-capable goals, about 70% of GAO F-35 recommendations unimplemented, and the DoD decision not to buy tech data all point to structural incentive failures. But part of the "inefficiency" is a rational trade-off: buffer stocks are expensive (F-35 funded spares only to 15% non-mission-capable supply because lower was "not affordable"), and WWII combat logistics was optimised for speed of advance, not repair efficiency.

## Surprises
- Accidents, not mechanical failure, drove about a third of Red Ball vehicle replacements, and abuse drove about 40% of tire demand. Operator behaviour mattered more than diagnosis.
- GAO cites a Navy study suggesting up to half of Navy cannibalizations are not caused by supply problems; some are swap-to-diagnose because of poor training or broken test equipment.
- F-35 organic military depots, once enabled, repaired 2x faster than the OEMs, yet 73% of components still went to the OEM because of data rights and activation delays. This is the knowledge monopoly made visible in numbers.
- Missing electronic paperwork, not missing parts, grounded F-35s for 9% of possible flight hours at one site.
- The F-35 office deliberately funded spares only to about 15% non-mission-capable supply because full buffering was unaffordable, so part of the inefficiency is chosen.
