## Findings

### F1. History and mechanism of power-by-the-hour (PBH): risk and knowledge move to the OEM [verified]
- Bristol Siddeley coined "power by the hour" in 1962 for Viper engines on HS/BAe 125 business jets. Operators paid a fixed sum per flying hour for a complete engine and accessory replacement service, so they no longer needed to stock engines. Rolls-Royce revived the scheme in the 1980s and trademarked the term. GE and Pratt & Whitney offer equivalents, and Jet Support Services (JSSI) offers OEM-independent hourly programmes.
- Mechanism: the operator pays a fixed $/EFH. The provider owns repair, overhaul and replacement, and plans engine removals ahead of time.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_by_the_Hour ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_maintenance
- Reading: under PBH, the steps from noticing a fault to returning the engine to service become the OEM's internal process. The operator no longer has to diagnose, understand or source anything for the engine. The knowledge does not reach the operator. It moves to the OEM and is locked into a contract.

### F2. The razor-and-blades economics behind PBH [verified, secondary]
- Engine OEMs "deeply discount" engine sales, by up to 90% and by at least 40% on new aircraft. They recover this through long-term spares and service revenue, and life-limited part (LLP) list prices rise over time to recoup the discount. The page says LLPs can be 80% of a shop-visit cost (tagged citation needed).
- Engines were about 31% of the $70B that airlines spent on MRO in 2017. Aviation Week put commercial engine MRO at $25.9B in 2018, and CFM was projected to be 36% of MRO demand over 2018-22.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_maintenance ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_by_the_Hour
- Reading: the "inefficiency" is designed into the pricing on purpose. Losses on hardware are funded by margin on the aftermarket. This is a business-model fact, not missing knowledge.

### F3. Academic evidence: performance-based contracts improve reliability [PK]
- Guajardo, Cohen, Kim and Netessine (2012), "Impact of Performance-Based Contracting on Product Reliability: An Empirical Analysis," Management Science 58(5):961-979. They used Rolls-Royce engine data. Under PBC (PBH-style) contracts, engine reliability (time between unscheduled removals) was roughly **25-40% higher** than under time-and-materials contracts, after controlling for engine and operator. The paper explains this by incentive alignment: the OEM keeps the repair risk, so it puts its knowledge into keeping the engine on wing.
- Kim, Cohen and Netessine (2007), "Performance contracting in after-sales service supply chains," Management Science, is the theory paper behind it.
- URL (403 when fetched): https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1474
- Reading: this is the strongest hard evidence that outcome contracts change behaviour and availability. They do it by changing incentives, with no new knowledge involved.

### F4. Defence PBL: the cost case is unproven and data is poor [verified]
- GAO-09-41 (Dec 2008): seven years after DoD made performance-based logistics (PBL) its preferred support strategy, its ability to cut costs "remains unclear." For the only two systems that had both a PBL and a traditional arrangement, **the PBL version cost more**. Program offices had limited cost data, and business case analyses were often missing or not updated. Contract length, funding instability, who owns the inventory, and missing cost metrics all weakened the contractor's incentive to cut costs. UK MoD availability contracts were believed to have cut costs, but those contracts are longer and use open-book accounting.
- Source: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-09-41
- A 2003 USAF finding (reported by Aviation Week, via Wikipedia) said contractor logistics support cost more than in-house depot support. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance-based_logistics
- Reading: this contradicts the idea that outcome contracts are automatically efficient. Without visible cost data, the provider can capture the gains.

### F5. F-35: contractor-led sustainment and missing technical data [verified]
- GAO-23-105341: the F-35 mission capable rate was about **55%** (March 2023), far below goals. More than **10,000 components** were waiting for depot repair. Field maintainers lacked technical data and training. DoD relies heavily on the contractor (Lockheed Martin) for sustainment and has "not identified or obtained the technical data needed." Lifecycle cost is over $1.7T, of which about **$1.3T is operating and support**. As of March 2026, six recommendations were still open. DoD aims to transfer several sustainment elements by the end of 2028 but still has to settle intellectual property. Engineering sustainment stays with the Joint Program Office.
- Source: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105341
- Reading: this is the clearest public case of a real knowledge monopoly in repair. The owner, the US government, cannot repair its own asset because it lacks the technical data and IP rights. The bottleneck is the data rights, not data that cannot be known. AI cannot make up the missing type design or IP rights.

### F6. Pratt & Whitney GTF powder-metal crisis: availability failure under OEM-controlled repair [verified]
- In July 2023, a recall covered 1,200 of about 3,000 PW1100G engines, and in September 2023 it was widened to all of them. P&W expected an average of **about 350 A320neo-family aircraft on the ground through 2026**. It estimated the cost at **$6-7B**, about 80% of it customer compensation. Inspections or shop visits took **250-300 days**.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_%26_Whitney_PW1000G
- Reading: diagnosis was fast. The fleet-wide fault (a powder-metal contamination defect) was identified and understood. The time went into shop capacity, parts (HPT disks) and the shop queue. This contradicts C4's focus on diagnosis and understanding. In this case the bottleneck was physical MRO capacity and parts manufacturing.

### F7. Trent 1000 durability: the OEM keeps the cost risk, and the airline still loses availability [verified]
- IP compressor blade cracking (2018) and IP turbine blade corrosion-fatigue (2016) affected the fleet. Aircraft on ground peaked at **44**. Package C inspection intervals were cut from every 200 to every 80 cycles, and the FAA cut ETOPS from 330 to 140 minutes, affecting **12 named airlines**. Rolls-Royce's estimated costs for 2017-2023 were raised to **£2.4B**.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_Trent_1000
- Reading: under TotalCare-type cover, Rolls-Royce paid the repair cost. Airlines still lost aircraft availability and had to lease replacement lift. Outcome contracts move cost risk, but they cannot remove physical repair time.

### F8. Fleet-wide supply chain and engine bottleneck [verified]
- IATA (Dec 2024): about 700 aircraft (2% of the global fleet) were parked for engine inspections. The average fleet age reached a record 14.8 years. Narrowbody lease rates were 20-30% above 2019. Deliveries were 30% below forecast, with a backlog of about 17,000 aircraft.
- Source: https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2024-releases/2024-12-10-02/
- [PK] The related IATA and Oliver Wyman study (Dec 2024) estimated that supply-chain problems would cost airlines more than **$11B in 2025**, including about **$3.1B of excess engine maintenance** and about $2.6B of extra leasing. IATA DG Willie Walsh repeatedly attacked OEM aftermarket practices in 2024-25. He criticised restrictions on parts and repairs and limits on independent MROs, and asked regulators to look at competition in the aftermarket.

### F9. Rolls-Royce TotalCare: scale, and profits driven by repricing [PK]
- [PK] Most of Rolls-Royce's widebody (Trent) fleet hours are under LTSAs/TotalCare, roughly 85-90% of large-engine flying hours. LTSA revenue is booked on a percentage-of-completion basis against expected shop-visit costs. That makes profits very sensitive to time-on-wing assumptions. In 2018-2020, onerous-contract charges on Trent 1000 LTSAs created large losses.
- [PK] From 2023, CEO Tufan Erginbilgic ran a turnaround. Its main levers were **repricing and renegotiating LTSA contracts**, longer time on wing, and lower shop-visit costs. Civil Aerospace operating margin rose from low single digits to the mid-to-high teens (2024-25). Rolls-Royce announced a durability programme of roughly £1B, including Trent XWB-97 work, aiming to double time on wing for hot-and-harsh operators.
- Reading: OEM gains under outcome contracts came mostly from pricing power and engineering knowledge of their own designs, plus engine-health-monitoring data the OEM collects. The data flows to the OEM, not to the airline. The knowledge concentration that C2 describes is real, but it is a deliberate outcome of the business model.

### F10. GE TrueChoice and the size of GE's services business [PK]
- [PK] GE Aviation launched TrueChoice in about 2015 as a menu: TrueChoice Flight Hour (PBH), Overhaul, Material (parts), Transitions (end-of-life engines) and others. The menu was partly a response to customers who wanted choice over full-wrap lock-in.
- [PK] GE Aerospace gets about **70% of its commercial engines revenue from services**. Commercial services revenue grew mid-teens or more in 2024-25. Customer service agreements (CSAs) carry multibillion-dollar contract assets. CFM LEAP is mostly under OEM-linked agreements, and CFM has licensed a limited "Premier MRO" network. A limited, licensed set of independents is allowed in, with OEM parts and repair data controlled through the licence.
- [PK] In Dec 2020 the SEC charged GE, which paid $200M, over misleading disclosures. These included how GE Power booked LTSA cost-estimate changes and factored LTSA receivables. This shows how opaque LTSA accounting can be.
- URL to verify: https://www.geaerospace.com/investor-relations ; SEC press release 2020-312: https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2020-312

### F11. Pratt EngineWise [PK]
- [PK] EngineWise is P&W's brand for service and analytics: EngineWise Comprehensive (PBH), predictive health monitoring and MRO network management. The GTF crisis exposed its limits. Even with full OEM ownership of the repair chain, P&W had to send engines to MRO partners such as MTU, Lufthansa Technik and EME, and shop capacity was fixed.

### F12. Caterpillar CSAs: services as a dealer-run growth engine [PK]
- [PK] Caterpillar set a goal (2019 Investor Day) to double services revenue from $14B in 2016 to **$28B by 2026**. Services revenue reached about $24B in 2024. Cat reports over **1.5 million connected assets** (Product Link/VisionLink telematics), and CVAs/CSAs are sold and run by about 150 independent dealers. Condition monitoring centres turn telematics and fluid analysis (S·O·S) into "prioritised service recommendations." Dealers then do the repair with Cat parts, so the knowledge flows to Cat and its dealers.
- [PK] Cat's main levers for services growth are connected assets that flag repair needs, plus parts and rebuild (Cat Reman, Certified Rebuild). This is also the area where right-to-repair activity hits construction and ag equipment (Deere more so than Cat).
- URL to verify (403 for this agent): https://www.caterpillar.com/en/investors.html

### F13. Independent PMA/DER parts and repairs exist, and outcome contracts suppress them [PK]
- [PK] HEICO and other PMA makers sell FAA-approved replacement parts, typically **30-50% below OEM list**. DER repairs (FAA Designated Engineering Representatives) give non-OEM repair schemes. PMA has only a small share of engine parts, low single digits. One reason is that OEM PBH contracts, lessor return conditions and some insurers exclude or penalise non-OEM parts and repairs.
- [PK] Delta TechOps, Lufthansa Technik and AFI KLM E&M have developed reverse-engineered repairs and pushed back on OEM restrictions. AFI KLM complained publicly about OEM aftermarket "lock-in." Lufthansa Technik and others ran EU competition complaints about restricted access to engine repair data and parts, as far back as the 2000s.
- Reading: alternatives to the OEM knowledge monopoly have existed for decades without AI (reverse engineering, DER engineering). The binding limits are certification (a new PMA/DER approval takes months to years plus test evidence), contract terms and lessor acceptance. Knowing what to make is not the limit.

### F14. Product-service systems more broadly [verified]
- Xerox pay-per-copy, Michelin tyres-per-km, Philips pay-per-lux and Atlas Copco Contract Air are classic outcome contracts. The literature makes claims (for example "factor 4" eco-efficiency) without measured results, and admits that not all PSS reduce material use. Mont's paper is titled "Panacea or myth?"
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product-service_system

## Synthesis

**Who owns knowledge and repair under outcome contracts?**
- Outcome contracts take the operator out of diagnosing, understanding and sourcing. The OEM (or its dealer) does all three, using telematics and engine-health data that the operator generates but the OEM keeps.
- This does not remove the "knowledge monopoly." It makes it formal. The operator gives up in-house capability (it loses skills) and swaps it for cost certainty. The OEM gains data and repair volume, and that strengthens its position again.
- Independents survive through licences (CFM Premier MRO, Cat dealers) or in older and end-of-life fleets (PMA, DER, used serviceable material) where PBH cover lapses.

**Cost and availability evidence**
- For: Guajardo et al. found 25-40% better reliability under PBC [PK]. UK MoD availability contracts were believed to cut costs (GAO).
- Against or mixed: DoD PBL cost savings were unproven, and PBL cost more in the two direct comparisons (GAO-09-41). The F-35 has a 55% mission capable rate under contractor-led sustainment with no government data rights (GAO-23-105341). OEM-wrapped engines (GTF, Trent 1000) had large availability failures: about 350 aircraft on the ground, 250-300-day turnarounds, and 44 aircraft on ground peak. The cause was shop capacity and parts, not diagnosis. OEM margin recovery came from repricing LTSAs, so customers pay for the risk transfer [PK].

**Where time and money go**
- Engine repair cost is dominated by parts: LLPs and hot-section parts, up to about 80% of a shop visit. Shop labour and capacity come next.
- Repair time is dominated by queues for shop slots and parts lead times (castings, forgings, HPT disks). Diagnosis and "understanding" for fleet-wide issues happen quickly, are centralised at the OEM, and are already data-driven (EHM).

**Real bottleneck**
- Parts manufacturing capacity (castings and forgings), certified MRO shop capacity, and the IP and certification regime: type certificate holder data, PMA/DER approvals, lessor and insurer acceptance.
- Knowledge is held by contract and law, not because it is scarce. AI lowers the cost of the engineering analysis for a DER/PMA case. It does not make the approval, testing or qualified-capacity steps go away.

## Stances (summary)
- **C1 (one chain): mixed.** The steps exist, but outcome contracts bundle them into a single provider. The chain is driven by prediction (planned removals) rather than by noticing a fault. Real time goes into queues between steps.
- **C2 (knowledge monopoly): mixed, leaning supports.** OEMs do capture knowledge and data, as the F-35 data-rights case and PBH lock-in show. But the monopoly is contractual and legal: IP, certification, razor-and-blades pricing. It is not simply a skills gap.
- **C3 (AI closes the gap): contradicts.** The gap is IP, data access and certification. In the strongest cases (F-35, GTF) diagnosis was not the constraint.
- **C4 (10x on four steps): contradicts.** Cost is mostly parts (LLPs up to about 80% of a shop visit), and time is mostly shop and parts capacity (250-300 days for the GTF). Diagnosis and understanding are a small share.
- **C5 (AI parts factory): contradicts, weakly.** PMA/DER already exist, and their limit is certification, testing and contract or lessor acceptance. LLPs and hot-section parts need certified materials and processes.
- **C6 (inspection essential, must evolve): supports.** FAA ETOPS limits and inspection intervals drove the Trent 1000 outcomes. Inspection is the gate.
- **C7 (built to be inefficient): mixed.** The economics are deliberately designed (discount the hardware, earn on the aftermarket). That is profit-efficient for OEMs, and outcome contracts can raise reliability. But GAO and the IATA evidence show the costs are high and customers are locked in.
