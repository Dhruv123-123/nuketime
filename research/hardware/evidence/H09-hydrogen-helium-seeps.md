# H09: Sensing natural hydrogen and helium seeps to find and own the rights

Research note. The shared web search and fetch budgets ran out partway through this slice. Facts with a fetched or cited source are marked as such. Anything recalled but not checked this session is marked "(unverified)".

## Summary
- Natural (geologic) hydrogen is drawing serious money. Koloma raised a $245.7M Series B in 2024, and secondary sources put its total near $400M after Osaka Gas and MHI invested. HyTerra hit 96.1% H2 on Kansas's Nemaha Ridge. Mali's Bourakebougou field (about 98% H2) is still the only producing field.
- Surface seeps (the "fairy circle" depressions) pulse every day. In Brazil's Sao Francisco Basin, soil H2 at 1 m averages about 100 ppm and peaks above 1,000 ppm, but only for about half of each day. One-off grab samples therefore miss or misread seeps. Continuous logging is the real data gap.
- Cheap H2 sensors drift and respond to other gases (CO, humidity, temperature). A useful node needs paired sensors, self-calibration and a He channel to tell deep gas from biological H2. A node built from parts could cost about $150-400 (unverified).
- Rights are cheap, and the law is unclear. In most US states, nobody has settled whether hydrogen belongs to the mineral owner or the surface owner. Wyoming treats H2 as "gas". Minnesota and Iowa have widened their gas definitions. France and South Australia put it under state permits. Kansas fee leases appear to cost only tens of dollars per acre (unverified).
- Buyers exist but they are thin. They are Koloma, HyTerra (Fortescue-backed, unverified), Gold Hydrogen, Japanese gas utilities and, for helium, the industrial gas majors. The step-up is real only once a well flows. A seep map alone is a weak sale. The best version owns the H2 plus He right under a pulse-verified seep cluster and farms it out for a carry.

- Who owns the rights:
  - Wyoming counts hydrogen as "gas" under its oil and gas rules. Royalty on private minerals is negotiated (https://www.uwyo.edu/ser/_files/docs/research/publications/cerpa-hydrogenleasing-snapshot.pdf).
  - Minnesota has a nonpetroleum gas framework (https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/minutes/senate/2024/envlegacy/20240404/envlegacy_20240404_SF5048-DNR-Non-Petroleum-Gas-Fact-Sheet.pdf).

  - In the US, rights sit with private landowners, not the state (https://amp.insurancejournal.com/news/midwest/2024/11/19/801804.htm).
  - South Australia brought hydrogen under its Petroleum and Geothermal Energy Act regulations in about 2021 (unverified). France added native hydrogen to its Mining Code in 2022, with state exclusive exploration permits (PER) (unverified).

## Hardware design sketch: "SeepNode"
- **Sensing:**
  - Probe: a 1 m stainless soil-gas probe with a diffusion chamber.
  - H2: two different H2 sensors (one electrochemical, about $60-120; one Pd-MOS or thermal-conductivity, about $20-60). Disagreement between the two flags drift.
  - Cross-gas: a CO sensor (about $30) to correct for CO.
  - Helium: thermal-conductivity He sensing works only at percent level (about $50). A cheaper way to get He/H2 ratios is a sealed vial auto-sampler that takes 7 samples per week, read later by lab mass spectrometry at about $50-100 each (unverified).
  - Environment: a barometric, temperature and humidity chip (about $5) for the pulse-timing signature.
  - Calibration: a small solenoid pump pulls air every 6 h for a zero.
- **Power and comms:** a 2 W solar panel, a LiFePO4 battery and a LoRa radio to a cellular gateway ($250 per gateway, covering about 20 nodes).
- **Bill of materials:** about $250-400 per node for the prototype and about $120-180 at 1,000 units (unverified).
- **Deployment:** about $100 of labour per node with a hand auger. A 20-node grid over a 5 km2 circle cluster costs about $8-10k all-in.
- **Time:** about 6-8 weeks to a working prototype and 3 months of field data.
- **Optional add-on:** drone imagery plus ML to find circles (they are visible in public NAIP/Sentinel images) and rank them for node placement.

## What data it unlocks that is not public
- Public data is circle outlines from satellite images and a few one-time soil-gas surveys published by explorers.
- No one publishes multi-month, pulse-resolved H2 flux with He/H2 ratios across hundreds of circles. Brazil had a single station ring.
- The daily pulse matters. A one-time reading taken at night can read zero over a strong seep. Pulse amplitude versus barometric forcing may separate deep, fault-fed H2 from shallow biological H2. That separation is the proving step.
- Incumbents run few or no continuous sensors. They drill on gravity and magnetic data, basement depth and old well logs. Koloma is reported to use proprietary ML on subsurface data (unverified).

## How it compounds into owned assets
- **The right:** a fee mineral lease or option on H2 plus He plus "all non-hydrocarbon gases" (drafted to beat the ownership doubt by signing both the surface and the mineral owner). Target Kansas and Nebraska (Nemaha Ridge, Midcontinent Rift), Minnesota and Iowa for helium, and later South Australia and France permits.
- **Cost:** tens of dollars per acre, perhaps $5-50/ac (unverified). That is cheap before proof.
- **Step-up:**
  - A sensor-verified seep cluster can be farmed out to HyTerra, Koloma, Gold Hydrogen-type explorers or Japanese utilities for a carried interest plus an override (unverified).
  - A flowing H2 plus He well is worth far more. Helium at about $300+/mcf is the cash leg, because H2 at about $1-2/kg has no pipeline buyer yet.
- **The moat:** each season of node data trains the "seep-to-reservoir" classifier, and only wells drilled on our acreage close the label loop.

## Risks and kill conditions
- Seep strength may not predict an accumulation. Leaky seeps can mean no trap. Kill the idea if node-ranked circles show no link to well results across 5+ partner wells.
- The ownership doubt cuts both ways. A court or statute could hand H2 to the surface owner, or put it under state leasing. Signing both owners helps.
- Buyer depth: there are only about 10-20 funded H2 explorers, and they self-source acreage in Kansas. Helium majors buy gas, not acreage.
- Helium prices are volatile, and the US auction market is small.
- Sensor drift or false positives from biological H2 and CO. Pass only if the two H2 sensors agree within 20% against the lab.

## Cheapest first test
- **What:** 10 nodes at about $3k, plus 20 lab He/H2 vials at about $2k, on circle depressions near a known Kansas H2 well, for 30 days. Total about $8k.
- **Pass:**
  - Daily pulses are seen at 3+ nodes, with peaks matching barometric pressure falls.
  - Node H2 agrees within 20% with lab gas chromatography.
  - He/H2 in the vials is above the air baseline at the pulsing nodes, and not at control sites off the circles.
  - A landowner quote for H2 plus He rights comes in under $50/ac.
- **Fail:** no pulse at 30 days, or the sensors cannot be told apart from CO or biological noise.
