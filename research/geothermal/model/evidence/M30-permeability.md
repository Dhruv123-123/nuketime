## Bottom line

Heat is common in the Great Basin; open, connected, *currently stressed* fractures at 0.5-3 km are rare, and that is what decides MWe. The best evidence is Ormat's own back-solve of 40 producing fields: the recovery factor runs from about 4% to 53% (median 16%), and the top five fields all have median well productivity above 60 kg/s/bar while the bottom five are all at 1 kg/s/bar or less. Well productivity itself spans more than two orders of magnitude (0.5 to over 100 kg/s/bar). Even after calibration at a drilled field (Krafla), permeability is uncertain by more than 100x. Temperature at depth, by contrast, is usually known to within tens of degrees once a gradient hole is in. So for our "how much hidden hydrothermal" number, the permeability term carries most of the spread, and today nearly every public model (USGS, INGENIOUS, GeoCore) handles it only with proxies such as distance to a fault. The physics we can use before drilling is well established: faults that are critically stressed in today's stress field tend to be the ones that carry fluid (Barton, Zoback and Moos 1995; Dixie Valley, Hickman and Barton), and faults that have not slipped recently tend to seal themselves with silica and calcite. So a better model scores every mapped and buried fault for (1) slip tendency under a *sampled* local stress field, (2) recency of slip, (3) geometry (step-overs, terminations, intersections), and then updates that prior with cheap direct flow signals. The big new idea in this slice is that **ranch water wells are free permeability tests**. Driller logs already record pumping yield and drawdown (specific capacity, which converts to transmissivity), and a cheap pressure logger left in a well for 2-4 weeks reads the earth-tide response, which gives transmissivity and storage at that spot (Hsieh et al. 1987). The temperature curve our drop probe already measures also gives vertical flow rate (Bredehoeft and Papadopulos 1965). And our E-field MT nodes, if built DC-coupled with non-polarizing electrodes, also record self-potential, which responds to moving water. So the sensor upgrade is small: add a pressure-and-barometer logger and an SP channel to kit we were going to build anyway. Each ranch visit then returns heat, chemistry *and* a permeability measurement, and the model gets a real permeability label set that Zanskar, which says permeability is its gap, does not have.

## Findings

### 1. How much permeability drives MWe

1. **Recovery factor tracks well productivity, across 40 real fields.** Libbey and Murphy (Ormat, SGW 2026) back-solved recovery factors on 40 developed analog fields. Range about 4% (Raft River, Patua) to 53% (Salt Wells), median 16%, mean 19%. The five highest (40-53%) all have median production well productivity index (MPWPI) above 60 kg/s/bar and are "Discrete" or "Enhanced Distributed" permeability. The five lowest all have MPWPI of 1 kg/s/bar or less and are "Limited Distributed". Source (fetched): https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/db/GeoConf/papers/SGW/2026/Libbey.pdf
2. **Four permeability regimes, with productivity bands.** The same paper defines: Discrete (fracture networks in tight granite or metamorphic rock, 0.5-2.5 km2, MPWPI 5 to over 100 kg/s/bar); Limited Distributed (distributed fractures, under 1 to over 100 km2, MPWPI 0.5-5); Enhanced Distributed (background plus system-scale structures, MPWPI at least 5, up to over 100); Stratigraphic (sedimentary matrix, usually over 5 km2, Salton Sea about 37 km2). Power density: 80% of fields fall between 5.6 and 38.5 MW/km2. The four highest (43-69 MW/km2) are all 270 °C+ Enhanced Distributed. Seven fields below 150 °C range from 0.2 to 20 MW/km2. Same source. **This is a ready-made class label and prior for our model.**
3. **The spread is mostly permeability.** For a fixed area and temperature, the recovery factor varies about 13x across analogs (4% to 53%). Temperature at a confirmed field is usually known to tens of degrees. Permeability posteriors at Krafla still spanned more than two orders of magnitude after Bayesian calibration (Scott, O'Sullivan, Maclaren et al.; via M15): https://essopenarchive.org/users/549252/articles/603411-bayesian-calibration-of-a-natural-state-geothermal-reservoir-model-krafla-north-iceland
4. **Simple well arithmetic (calc).** A 150 °C binary well cooled to about 70 °C gives about 336 kJ/kg of heat. At about 10-12% conversion that is about 35-40 kJ/kg, so roughly 25 kg/s per net MWe (calc, rough). With a pump drawdown of about 20 bar: PI 1 kg/s/bar gives 20 kg/s, which is under 1 MWe per well. PI 10 gives 200 kg/s, which is pump-limited at about 100 kg/s, or about 4 MWe per well. So moving PI from 1 to 10 changes the well count for a 20 MWe plant from more than 20 wells to about 5. That is the difference between no project and a good one, with the same heat.
5. **Benchmark productivity values are contested.** NREL's EGS Shot analysis assumed 38.1 kg/s/bar for producers and 70 kg/s/bar for injectors, described as the 90th percentile of hydrothermal wells (Augustine et al. 2023, NREL/TP-5700-84822, via L17): https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy19osti/71833.pdf (cited in L17; the TP-84822 PDF itself not re-fetched). A critic (Yearsley 2026, via L07) argues 38 kg/s/bar is about 40x too high for EGS. Either way the 90th percentile for hydrothermal is tens of kg/s/bar and the low end is under 1. Two orders of magnitude.
6. **Most exploration failures are flow failures, not heat failures.** IFC (2013): 2,613 wells, 57 fields, first well in a field succeeds 50% of the time, the first five 59%. Wall and Dobson (2016): exploration drilling led to full-size development wells less than one-third of the time. Sanyal and Morrow (2012): success stabilises only after about 40 wells, and capacity jumps track revisions to the conceptual model. All via L13: https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1394062 and https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/db/IGAstandard/record_detail.php?id=26553 . The split between "too cold" and "too tight" in those failures is not published in the summaries I have (unverified), but the Great Basin field histories (Dixie Valley edge wells, Desert Peak 27-15, many Raft River wells) are mainly hot-but-tight, which is why EGS demos were sited in them (from memory, unverified).
7. **Zanskar names permeability as its gap.** Founders say temperature only "in some cases" leads permeability and there is "very few data" on deep fluid circulation (Volts 2025, via M13): https://www.volts.wtf/p/is-there-way-more-conventional-geothermal . In the PFA programme, 9 of 11 Phase 1 teams graded permeability as their weakest evidence (Garchar et al. 2016, via M03): https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2016/Garchar.pdf

### 2. Stress state and critically stressed faults: the physics we can use before drilling

8. **Hydraulically conductive fractures are the critically stressed ones.** Barton, Zoback and Moos (1995, Geology 23:683) compared televiewer fracture picks with temperature-log flow anomalies in three deep holes (Cajon Pass, Long Valley, Nevada Test Site). The fractures that carried flow were the ones whose shear-to-normal stress ratio was at or above a friction of about 0.6-1.0. Non-flowing fractures plotted below. (from memory, unverified; DOI 10.1130/0091-7613(1995)023<0683:FFAPAF>2.3.CO;2)
9. **Dixie Valley shows the same fault productive in one place and tight in another.** Hickman, Barton, Zoback and co-workers logged wells along the Stillwater fault. Wells in the productive segment cut a fault that was critically stressed for normal slip under the measured stress. Wells on a segment about 8 km away, at similar temperature, were not productive. There the fault was not critically stressed (lower differential stress, SHmax direction less favourable) and fracture zones were sealed by mineral fill. (Hickman et al. 1998/2000; Barton et al. 1998 SGW; from memory, unverified.) **This is the clearest published case where stress state, not heat, decided the well.** It is exactly the "hot but dry" failure that a heat-only model cannot see.
10. **Slip and dilation tendency formulas are public and cheap.** Ts = tau/sigma_n (Morris et al. 1996) and Td = (sigma1 - sigma_n)/(sigma1 - sigma3) (Ferrill et al. 1999), applied by Faulds et al. (2013) at Patua, Tuscarora, McGinness Hills, Salt Wells, San Emidio and Neal Hot Springs (via L22): https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1136720 (fetch blocked this session). INGENIOUS ships slip and dilation tendency on Quaternary faults as a layer (L22, M02).
11. **But the stress input is the weak part, so the layer is noisy.** SHmax can rotate up to about 90° over tens of km near the edges of extensional provinces (Lund Snee and Zoback 2020, via L22). The regional map is interpolated from sparse breakouts and focal mechanisms. A 20-30° SHmax error can flip a fault from "high" to "low" tendency (calc; follows from the formulas for steep faults near optimal orientation). A tendency layer computed from one smoothed stress field is therefore over-confident. M17 reports dilation tendency correlated -0.56 with measured shallow temperature in one PFA study, the wrong sign, which is a warning that the layer can mislead when the stress input is wrong or the target is shallow (via M17). Stress-map source: https://scits.stanford.edu/ (Lund Snee and Zoback, Stanford Center for Induced and Triggered Seismicity; URL from memory, unverified).
12. **Pore pressure and friction matter as much as orientation.** "Critically stressed" depends on Pp/sigma_v and on friction (0.6-1.0, Byerlee). In the Great Basin many reservoirs sit near hydrostatic, so the normal-faulting regime puts steep faults close to failure almost everywhere SHmax is favourable (from memory, unverified). This is why fault *orientation relative to local Shmin* plus *slip recency* together beat either one alone.

### 3. Mineralized (sealed) versus open faults

13. **Faults self-seal on geologic short times.** Silica and calcite precipitate as hot fluid cools and degasses. Modelling and lab work (e.g. Lowell, Van Cappellen and Germanovich 1993, Science) suggest an active fracture can seal in years to thousands of years unless it slips again (from memory, unverified). So permeability is not a fixed rock property. It is a balance of slip (opens) and precipitation (closes). This is the "fault-valve" picture (Sibson).
14. **What this means for features.** (a) Recency of slip and slip rate are permeability features, not just hazard features: USGS Qfaults carries 4 age classes and 4 slip-rate classes (via L22). Faulds weights of evidence gave young NE-striking faults a contrast of 2.52 for systems hotter than 160 °C (via M11). (b) Visible sinter, silicified breccia, opal and calcite veining along a fault at surface are evidence of *past* flow and possible present sealing. They raise P(system ever existed) and are ambiguous for P(open now). (c) A self-sealed cap is also why blind systems are blind. The seal is part of the play, so the model needs "open at depth, sealed at top", not just "open".
15. **Permeability falls with depth, but less in active regions.** Manning and Ingebritsen (1999, Rev. Geophys.) fit log k ≈ -14 - 3.2 log z (k in m2, z in km) for continental crust. Ingebritsen and Manning (2010) show that geologically active crust (tectonic or hydrothermal activity) sits orders of magnitude above this curve (from memory, unverified). This is a good prior for k(z) in the model, with a regional "activity" offset that the stress and slip features shift.
16. **Earthquakes open permeability, and water wells can see it.** Elkhoury, Brodsky and Agnew (2006, Nature 441:1135) used the phase of the earth-tide response in Piñon Flat water wells to show permeability jumped (up to about 3x) after distant earthquakes, then healed over months (from memory, unverified). So the tidal method works in ordinary wells, and it detects the very "recently opened" state we care about.

### 4. Observations that constrain permeability before (or instead of) a deep well

17. **Specific capacity from driller logs (free, illegible, huge).** State water-well logs in NV, UT, ID, OR and NM usually record pumping rate and drawdown at completion. Specific capacity (Q/s) converts to transmissivity with standard Theis/Cooper-Jacob relations (T ≈ 1.2-1.5 x Q/s for unconfined/confined approximations; textbook, unverified for exact constant). Tens of thousands of such logs exist (D03, M06 cover the temperature side of the same logs). They are shallow (mostly under 300 m) but they sample the fault zones that feed outflow plumes. **Nobody in public geothermal ML uses them as a permeability layer** (based on L03, L22, M14 review).
18. **Earth-tide and barometric response in a well.** Hsieh, Bredehoeft and Farr (1987, Water Resources Research 23:1824) showed that the phase lag and amplitude of the water level response to M2 and O1 tides give aquifer transmissivity and storativity (from memory, unverified). The signal is millimetres to a few centimetres, so it needs about 0.1 mm resolution and barometric correction, plus 2-4 weeks of record to separate tidal lines. Many USGS NWIS continuous-level wells already have this data at 15-60 min intervals.
19. **Temperature curvature gives vertical flow.** Bredehoeft and Papadopulos (1965, WRR) showed that the curvature of a temperature-depth profile in a steady vertical flow gives the Peclet number and so the vertical Darcy velocity (from memory, unverified). Our drop probe logs exactly this profile. Upward-curved profiles mean upflow (permeable path to heat), downward-curved mean recharge (masking, M23). This turns every ranch-well log into a flow estimate, not just a gradient.
20. **Seismicity and focal mechanisms give local stress.** Focal mechanism inversions (e.g. from the Nevada Seismological Lab and ANSS catalogs) give local SHmax and regime where there is enough small seismicity. M12 found seismology is good at stress and structure. Hawaii PFA gave seismicity and ground deformation the largest permeability weights (w = 2.1 each, via M03). A short node deployment that records 20-50 small events can fix the local stress orientation to perhaps ±10-15° (calc, rough, unverified), which is what the slip-tendency layer needs.
21. **Helium isotopes see deep open paths.** High R/Ra in ordinary well water marks faults open to the deep crust (Banerjee et al. 2011; Kennedy and van Soest 2005; via M22): https://www.usgs.gov/publications/deep-permeable-fault-controlled-helium-transport-and-limited-mantle-flux-two . It is a permeability label, not a heat label.
22. **Self-potential (SP).** Moving water generates streaming potentials, typically tens to hundreds of mV over upflow zones (Corwin and Hoover 1979, Geophysics; from memory, unverified). SP needs non-polarizing electrodes and DC-coupled voltmeters, which is the same hardware as E-field MT, minus the high-frequency band.
23. **Passive seismic and joint inversion.** The San Emidio and Crescent Valley work (Akerley, Warren, Gasperikova, Pullammanappallil 2023) used passive seismic emission tomography and joint MT/gravity/seismic inversion to map 3D permeability (via L22, M12): https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1986084 (fetch blocked). M12 notes the results are retrospective at known fields.
24. **InSAR and GPS.** Geodetic dilatation ranks near the bottom in USGS ML feature importance (via M11), so regional strain is a weak permeability term. Local InSAR subsidence or uplift around a hot-spring area or well field can show pressure-connected volume (from memory). NASA OPERA DISP-S1 now gives North America Sentinel-1 displacement as a free product (from memory, unverified).
25. **Injection well records.** Every operating Nevada field reinjects. NDEP UIC permits and NDOM geothermal well files list injection rates and wellhead pressures, which convert to injectivity (kg/s/bar) at real wells (D03 notes 938 NDOM wells; UIC detail from memory, unverified). Together with Libbey's 40 analogs, this is the training set for "what permeability class does a field of this structural type have".

## What dominates uncertainty about blind resources (this slice)

1. **Permeability class at 0.5-3 km.** Two orders of magnitude in well productivity and 13x in recovery factor at fixed heat. This is the single largest term in MWe per discovered system.
2. **Local stress orientation.** Interpolated regional SHmax can be off by tens of degrees, enough to flip slip tendency. Most model layers treat it as known.
3. **Open now versus sealed.** Mineral sealing is time-dependent and invisible to maps. Slip recency is the best proxy, and Qfaults ages are coarse.
4. **Connectivity and volume.** A permeable fault segment may be a few hundred metres long or several km. Area (0.5-2.5 km2 in Discrete regimes versus tens of km2) drives MWe linearly.
5. **Buried faults.** Faults under valley fill have no trace, so no tendency score at all (M11).
6. **Label bias.** Productivity data exist only where fields were developed. There are almost no published "hot but tight" well records with numbers, so the model under-learns the failure class.

## What a sensor could fill

- **Pressure-and-barometer logger left in the ranch well (add-on to the drop probe).** 2-4 weeks of 1-5 min level data with about 0.1 mm resolution gives tidal phase and amplitude, hence transmissivity and storage, plus barometric efficiency. Commercial loggers cost several hundred to about $1,000 each (Solinst, In-Situ; price from memory, unverified). A DIY version around a sealed pressure sensor and a logger board may cost under $100 in parts (unverified). Left behind on the same visit that signs the heat option, it is picked up at the next visit. This is the cheapest direct permeability measurement there is.
- **Temperature-curvature analysis from the drop probe log.** No new hardware. Fit Bredehoeft-Papadopulos to each profile and report vertical Darcy flux with error bars. Needs about 0.01 °C resolution and slow logging, which the probe spec should state.
- **DC-coupled SP channel on the E-field MT nodes.** Use non-polarizing electrodes (Pb-PbCl2 or Ag-AgCl) and keep the DC band. The same node then records streaming potentials over upflow. Near-zero extra cost per node.
- **Three-component geophone on each node.** Records small earthquakes during the MT deployment, giving local focal mechanisms and stress orientation over weeks. M12 and H03 already proposed this; this slice gives it a concrete job: fix SHmax for the tendency layer at each candidate.
- **Optional: soil-gas CO2 flux and helium along the scored fault.** Covered in H04 and M22. It tells you which fault segment leaks today.
- **Optional, later: slug or short pump test kit for ranch wells.** A pressure logger plus a slug of known volume gives local hydraulic conductivity in an hour (standard Hvorslev / Bouwer-Rice method; from memory). Needs owner consent, which we have on the same visit.

## Implications for a better model

1. **Split the latent field into heat and permeability, and make permeability first-class.** P(resource) = P(hot) x P(open | hot) x P(fluid), as Hawaii PFA did (M03), but with k(x, z) as a continuous log-permeability field with a Manning-Ingebritsen depth prior and a regional "activity" offset, not a 0/1 proxy.
2. **Compute slip and dilation tendency stochastically.** Sample SHmax azimuth, stress ratio, regime, friction (0.6-1.0) and Pp from their uncertainty (Lund Snee and Zoback plus local focal mechanisms), for every mapped and inferred fault, and carry P(critically stressed) per fault segment rather than one deterministic value. Update the stress field where our geophones record events.
3. **Add a recency term.** Combine Qfaults age and slip rate, scarp morphology from lidar, and seismicity within a few km into "slipped recently" probability. Interact it with tendency: critically stressed AND recently slipped is the Dixie Valley productive-segment signature.
4. **Ingest the free direct-flow data nobody uses.** Specific capacity from driller logs, NWIS tidal analysis, injection permit injectivity, lost-circulation depths from scanned logs. These become sparse but direct permeability observations that condition the k field via kriging or a Gaussian process with fault-aligned anisotropy.
5. **Use Libbey's regimes as an output class.** Predict, for each candidate, a probability over {Discrete, Limited Distributed, Enhanced Distributed, Stratigraphic} and draw recovery factor and MW/km2 from the class-conditional analog distributions. This replaces Zanskar's flat 30 MWe per discovery (M13) with a size distribution that is honest about permeability.
6. **Report MWe as P90/P50/P10 driven by the permeability class.** Expect the spread at a pre-drill target to be about 10x, mostly from the permeability class. Show buyers which observation would narrow it most (value of information, M17): usually a tidal/specific-capacity test and a stress fix are cheaper than a gradient hole and cut the permeability spread more.
7. **Label "hot but tight" explicitly.** Mine EGS demo sites, edge wells and abandoned geothermal wells (NDOM, GTC holes in L16) for low-productivity labels, so the classifier learns the failure class it currently never sees.
8. **Commercial angle.** A heat-rights bank that can say "this parcel is hot AND its fault is critically stressed AND the ranch well shows high transmissivity and upward flow" is selling a de-risked permeability story, which is the thing every buyer (and Zanskar by its own account) is missing. Permeability evidence raises the option value of a $1-2/acre private heat right more than another temperature point does.
