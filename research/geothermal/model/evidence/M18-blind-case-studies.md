# M18: Blind system case studies. What signal came first, how deep the heat was, and how big the field became

Date: 23 Oct 2026. This file builds on `../../evidence/L02-blind-systems.md` (McGinness, Blue Mountain, Big Blind, Gabbs, Granite Springs summaries and the blind-share numbers), `../../evidence/A05-hidden-heat-in-mines.md` (mining-hole temperatures at McGinness and Don A. Campbell), `../../evidence/Z02-drilled.md` (Zanskar wells) and `M03`, `M11`, `M14`. It does not repeat their market arguments. New here: one table across 13 systems with the *first* signal, the time it sat unused, the depth to 100 °C and the final size, plus Desert Peak, Salt Wells, Lightning Dock, Tungsten Mountain, Don A. Campbell, San Emidio SW and Patua details from primary sources. Web budget: 9 searches, 10 fetches. Some numbers come from OpenEI wiki summaries of primary reports; they are marked.

## Bottom line

Across 12 named systems plus one control, the first sign of a blind system was almost never a geothermal survey. It was a **hole drilled for something else**: gold or mineral holes (McGinness Hills, Blue Mountain, Don A. Campbell, Tungsten Mountain), a farm well (Lightning Dock, and Stillwater per Faulds), or an oil company's regional gradient program (Desert Peak, Salt Wells). Only the newest finds (Gabbs Valley, Granite Springs, Big Blind) started from a map or model. Then **2 m probes and 100-300 m gradient holes confirmed every one of them cheaply**. The most useful pattern for the business is the **lag**: the first hot hole sat unused for **10 to 65 years** before a plant (median about 25 years, calc). At McGinness Hills, mining holes hit hot water in 1982-84 and 88 °C at about 300-365 m in 2004; Ormat took the first-ever lease in 2007, and the field is now about 160 MW. At Don A. Campbell, early-1990s mining data showed hot water and a magnetic low; Ormat nominated the land in 2001, won it in 2007, and runs about 30 MW. So the signal was public or semi-public long before anyone locked the land. A second pattern is **bias in the training set**. In nearly every historic case, 100 °C was reached **shallower than about 300 m**, because shallow outflow plumes are what accidental holes find. Big Blind (121 °C at about 823 m, no shallow wells) is the first public case of a system with no shallow hit, and it is the kind most of the remaining stock probably looks like (inference). A better model must (1) treat "a hole for something else found warm water" as the top feature and mine those archives, (2) label each system by depth to 100 °C and by first-signal type, not just "known or not", and (3) correct for the fact that today's labels over-sample shallow outflow. The sensors that fill the gap are those that see heat in the 100-800 m range without a drill rig: re-logging existing water and mining holes (our drop probe), dense 2 m arrays, and MT/E-field nodes for clay caps and deep conductors.

## Findings with sources

### F1. Accidental holes were the first signal in 7 of 11 blind systems with a known first signal
- Mineral holes: McGinness Hills, Blue Mountain, Don A. Campbell, Tungsten Mountain (4).
- Farm or water wells: Lightning Dock (1). Faulds also lists Stillwater.
- Oil-company regional gradient programs: Desert Peak, Salt Wells (2). Phillips's 53 holes were "the only exploration tool that located the thermal anomaly" at Desert Peak, which Benoit calls "the first blind geothermal discovery in the Basin and Range."
- Old O&G holes that were *not* hot enough but still constrained a model: Pumpernickel.
- Map or model first: Gabbs Valley, Granite Springs, Big Blind (3).
- Faulds et al. (2019) say the same in one line: most known blind systems were found by "regional gradient drilling programs (e.g., Desert Peak) or by accident during the drilling of agricultural wells (e.g., Stillwater) or mineral exploration holes (e.g., McGinness Hills, Tungsten Mountain, Blue Mountain)."
- Sources: Benoit 1982, https://data.nbmg.unr.edu/public/Geothermal/GreyLiterature/Benoit_DsrtPkGeothermField_Geol_1982.pdf ; NBMG exploration notes, https://nbmg.unr.edu/Geothermal/Exploration.html ; Faulds DOE final report, https://gdr.openei.org/files/756/Faulds-DE-EE0006731-Report-v2.pdf ; OpenEI Lightning Dock, https://en.openei.org/wiki/Lightning_Dock_Area ; OpenEI Salt Wells, https://openei.org/wiki/Salt_Wells_Area

### F2. The signal sat idle for decades, and the land was then won cheaply
- Lightning Dock: boiling water in a 1948 farm well; first commercial plant 2013 (65 years).
- McGinness Hills: hot mineral holes 1982-84; lease 2007; power 2012. Ormat's 10-K says no geothermal leasing had ever happened there before 2007.
- Don A. Campbell: early-1990s mining data; Ormat nominated the parcel in 2001 and secured it only in 2007 (Kraal et al. 2025). A05 records that Ormat reviewed the mining data "at no cost."
- Blue Mountain: hot gold holes in the early 1990s; the miner took non-competitive leases and sold them with the data; power 2009.
- Salt Wells: anomaly defined by 1980; plant 2009.
- Plain reading: the scarce input was never the data. It was someone reading it and locking the land. That is the exact Terranox wedge.
- Sources: Kraal et al. 2025 SGW, https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/db/GeoConf/papers/SGW/2025/Kraal.pdf ; Ormat 10-K, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1296445/000143774916026139/ora20151231_10k.htm ; NREL Mining G.O.L.D. (in A05), https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/81946.pdf

### F3. Cheap shallow tools confirmed every case
- 2 m probes: Tungsten Mountain (2007, ≥6 km anomaly, matched the hot mineral holes), Don A. Campbell (2010, 6 km², 19.3 °C above background), Gabbs Valley (124 stations), Lightning Dock (a 1 m probe in 1956 found a 12 °C anomaly).
- Gradient holes of 90-250 m: Desert Peak (53 holes), Lightning Dock (53 AMAX holes), Gabbs (124 °C at 152 m), Granite Springs (96 °C at 250 m).
- Geophysics that co-located: magnetic lows (Don A. Campbell, Gabbs), shallow gravity highs from silica (Don A. Campbell), HTEM conductors under 100 m from smectite clay (Don A. Campbell), MT under Gabbs.
- Sources: OpenEI Tungsten Mountain 2 m, https://openei.org/wiki/2-M_Probe_At_Tungsten_Mountain_Area_(Shevenell,_Et_Al.,_2008) ; Kraal 2025 (above); Faulds 2018 Gabbs, https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70201803

### F4. Depth to 100 °C was shallow in almost every historic find: a sampling bias
- Under 100 m: Desert Peak, Lightning Dock, Don A. Campbell.
- 100-300 m: Salt Wells, Gabbs, Tungsten Mountain (inferred), Patua.
- 300-700 m: McGinness Hills, Blue Mountain.
- Deeper: Big Blind (121 °C at ~823 m), Pumpernickel (~138 °C at ~760 m).
- Why it matters: accidental holes are mostly under 300 m. So the historic label set over-represents systems with a shallow outflow plume. Systems whose plume does not reach 300 m are under-counted, and a model trained on known sites will under-score them. Big Blind and Pumpernickel are the first public, deliberate finds of the deeper kind (inference).
- The shallow plume is often outflow, not upflow. At Don A. Campbell, the 120-130 °C basin-fill aquifer is outflow, and the deep volcanics show only a conductive gradient (Kraal 2025). At Granite Springs, a hot gradient did not locate the upflow (M03).

### F5. Sizes span 4 to 160 MW, and size is not predicted by the strength of the first signal
- The first signals were similar, 88 °C water in mining holes, at both McGinness (now ~160 MW) and Don A. Campbell (~30 MW).
- Lightning Dock had boiling water at 26 m, yet produced only 4-15 MW.
- So first-signal temperature predicts *existence*, not *size*. Size tracked reservoir temperature (168 °C at McGinness against 128 °C at Don A. Campbell), structural setting, and deep permeability.
- Ormat expanded McGinness over 3 phases (72 to 138 to ~160 MW), so first estimates were low.
- San Emidio SW zone: P50 47 MW net from one new structural trend next to an old 1987 plant. Blind extensions of known fields are a real class.
- Sources: ThinkGeoEnergy on San Emidio 2017, https://www.thinkgeoenergy.com/with-larger-resources-at-san-emidio-u-s-geothermal-seeks-different-interconnection/ ; Ormat McGinness release, https://investor.ormat.com/news-events/news/news-details/2021/Ormat-Completes-McGinness-Hills-Complex-Expansion/default.aspx

### F6. Patua is a control, not a blind case
- Patua has 13 hot springs (28-96 °C), and Magma Power drilled to 132 °C in 1962. It should be labeled "surface-expressed." L02 already flagged this.
- It is still useful. Even with obvious springs, it waited about 50 years for a plant. That supports F2: lag comes from land and capital, not from a lack of signal.
- Source: NBMG exploration notes (above); OpenEI Patua, https://en.openei.org/wiki/Patua_Geothermal_Area

### F7. New labels are arriving in 2026
- Granite Springs: an INGENIOUS project-area permit (operator RESPEC, contractor Welsco) for TG wells to about 1,500 ft in Pershing County, with drilling from May 2026. The result is not yet public.
- These holes will give the first deliberate 450 m label at a play-fairway-only site. Watch for its release.
- Source: NV Division of Minerals permit, https://prod.minerals.nv.gov/siteassets/content/programs/geo/geopermits/geo-1739-5-21-2026-nbmg-granite-springs-project-area-pershing.pdf

## What dominates uncertainty

1. **How many blind systems have no shallow plume.** All historic accidental finds had 100 °C within ~600 m, mostly within 300 m. The deep-only class (Big Blind type) has one or two public examples, so its size is a guess.
2. **Size given discovery.** First-signal strength does not predict MW (F5). Permeability and reservoir temperature at 1-2 km set size, and neither is visible from the surface.
3. **Survivorship in case histories.** Hot mining holes that led to dry or tiny prospects are rarely written up, so the hit rate of "hot mining hole leads to a plant" is unknown.
4. **Label noise.** Sources disagree (Salt Wells 140 °C against 220 °C in the same wiki; Don A. Campbell 30 MW in Kraal against 42 MW in A05). Several depths here are inferred.

## What a sensor could fill

- **Re-log existing holes** (water wells, open mining holes, old TG holes) with the ranch drop probe. That is the first-signal channel in F1, and the drop probe can measure gradient and conductivity at 50-300 m for a few hundred dollars per hole. It extends the "accidental hole" signal to holes that were never logged.
- **Dense 2 m arrays** remain the cheapest confirmation (F3). Every case where they were run lit up the anomaly. Logging probes left in place for a season would also remove the seasonal noise.
- **E-field-only MT nodes** for the 100-800 m clay cap and for deep conductors. That is the depth band where Big Blind-type systems hide and where accidental holes do not reach (F4).
- **Ground magnetics from a drone.** Magnetic lows from altered rock marked Don A. Campbell and Gabbs at the prospect scale.

## Implications for a better model

1. **Make "hole drilled for another reason found warm or hot water" a first-class feature and a label source.** Build it by OCR plus LLM extraction from NBMG and BLM mining files, water-well logs and O&G records. It is the historic #1 first signal.
2. **Use typed labels, not binary ones.** Record first-signal type, depth to 100 °C, reservoir temperature, MW and blind/semi-blind/expressed (Patua = expressed, Salt Wells = semi-blind with relict sinter). Train separate heads for *existence* and *size*.
3. **Correct for depth bias.** Weight or stratify training so that a site with no shallow wells is not read as cold. Treat "no data" as unknown, using the degree-of-exploration covariate from Coolbaugh 2006 (L02).
4. **Run a time-split back-test using this table.** Hide each system at the year before its first signal and ask whether the model ranks it high. Desert Peak (1973), McGinness (1982), Don A. Campbell (1990), Gabbs (2015) and Big Blind (2023) are a ready test set.
5. **Track the lag as a land signal.** Any cell with a public hot-hole record and no geothermal lease is a McGinness-style opportunity. Rank those first for land locking.
6. **Score blind extensions of known fields** (San Emidio SW) as a separate, lower-risk class.
