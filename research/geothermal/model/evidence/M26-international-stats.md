## Bottom line

The best international numbers say the same thing three ways. **(1) Once a field is known, wells mostly work.** IFC: 78% of 2,613 wells succeed, with mean 7.3 MWe per well. Iceland: 74% of 213 wells succeed, with mean 4.9 MWe. **(2) The first wells in a new field are close to a coin flip** (IFC 50%, Iceland 43%), and a whole new *prospect* turns into a developed field less than one time in three (Wall and Dobson). **(3) What decides the outcome is the conceptual model: temperature, pressure and permeability at the target, not geology class, depth or drilling skill.** IFC found *no clear effect* of geology or resource type on success rate, and no link between depth and success. Iceland's failures were mostly low static pressure (10% of wells), with temperature (4%), permeability (3%) and "too shallow" (3%) behind it. Around the world, the conceptual model is built from three things: an MT/TEM clay-cap image, geothermometers on fluids, and a temperature tie. The clay cap is the most-used and most-trusted pre-drill layer abroad, but it can fail as a false positive. At Tutuila (American Samoa) a strong ≤10 Ω·m cap sat over 37 °C rock. For our model this means: **(a)** use a hierarchical prior where P(success | field exists) ≈ 0.5 rising on a log curve with well count, and P(prospect becomes a field) ≈ 0.2 to 0.33. **(b)** Treat a clay cap as a strong but not decisive likelihood ratio, and make it decisive only with a temperature tie. **(c)** Use a right-skewed (lognormal-like) per-well MW prior, set lower for the Great Basin. The sensor gaps abroad that would also lift our US program are cheap: a **TEM loop to fix MT static shift** (Iceland's standard), a **methylene-blue / SWIR clay kit for water-well and mining cuttings** (the Philippine and NZ standard for finding the cap base in holes), and a **static water level and pressure reading** on every drop-probe visit, because low pressure was Iceland's single biggest failure cause.

## Findings

### F1. IFC 2013: capacity is skewed, and geology class does not predict success
Source: Avato (IFC) ESMAP presentation of the IFC study, https://www.esmap.org/sites/esmap.org/files/1-Patrick%20Avato.pdf ; summary at https://www.thinkgeoenergy.com/global-study-on-success-of-drilling-geothermal-wells-by-ifc/
- Sample: 2,613 wells in 57 fields in 14 countries, about 70% of the world's commercial geothermal wells. The fields hold 7,700 MW of the 10,700 MW installed then. That is about 46 wells per field (my division), so the sample is heavily mature fields. Status is unknown for 12% of wells.
- **Learning curve:** cumulative success follows **y = 0.07 ln(x) + 0.48** (R² = 0.99), where x is the well's number in the field sequence. Over 50 years, exploration-phase success has *risen*, development success is flat, and operation-phase success has *fallen* (fields get drilled out at the edges).
- **Capacity per well:** mean **7.3 MWe**. The phase means are 6.0 MWe (exploration), 7.3 (development) and 7.5 (operation). The **mode is 3 MWe** (4 MWe in exploration), the skew is 1.64, and the maximum is 54 MWe. Capacity barely improves as drilling goes on.
- **Geology:** "no clear effect" of geology or resource code on *success rate*. Higher enthalpy raises *capacity*, but not strictly. (Note: L13, citing Lowry 2017's summary, says sedimentary-basin fields had higher success. The IFC slides I read do not show that split. Treat the setting effect on success as weak or unproven.)
- **Depth:** most wells are about 2.2 km deep, and depth shows no link to success or capacity.
- **Re-drills:** 16% of wells were re-drilled. Re-drills succeed 87% of the time against 77% for originals, and re-drills tend to be all-or-nothing by field.
- The authors' own conclusion: exploration-phase success "is hard to predict", and "estimating resource temperature during exploration is key to forecasting capacity". This points at a temperature-at-depth model, which is our M16 sub-model.

### F2. Iceland (ÍSOR-2014-053): 213 wells, with failure causes
Source: Sveinbjörnsson and Thórhallsson, ÍSOR-2014-053 for Orkustofnun, http://os.is/gogn/Skyrslur/ISOR-2014/ISOR-2014-053.pdf ; summary at https://www.thinkgeoenergy.com/report-success-of-high-temperature-geothermal-wells-in-iceland/
- 213 production wells and 21 injection wells in 7 high-temperature fields. 158 of the 213 (74%) were successful, meaning good enough to connect to a plant. No field was below 50%.
- **Learning curve:** 43% for the first well, 60% for the first five, and a 74% plateau after about 15 wells. This is almost the same shape as IFC, in a very different country, which gives two independent datasets for the prior.
- **MW per well:** 4.9 MWe mean across all wells, 6.7 MWe for productive wells, and a most-likely value of 4.8 MWe (SD 2.3). The five main plants run 1.3 to 5.3 MWe per well (weighted 3.5).
- **Failure causes (share of all wells):** low static pressure **10%**, drilling problems 6%, low temperature 4%, low permeability 3%, too shallow 3%. So in a well-imaged volcanic country, temperature was rarely the problem. Hydraulics (pressure and permeability) was the problem.
- **Design effects:** directional wells average 6.1 MWe against 4.0 for vertical wells. Large casing (300 to 350 mm) averages 8.9 MWe against 5.5 MWe. Steam caps over 230 to 240 °C two-phase zones gave 11.0 MWe with 100% success. In other words, "how you drill" changes MW by up to 1.5 to 2 times, while "where you drill" decides whether you get anything.

### F3. Project-level risk is much worse than well-level risk
- Wall and Dobson (2016): exploration drilling led on to full-size development wells **less than one third of the time** (quoted in DOE GeoVision 2019, p. 37, https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2019/06/f63/GeoVision-full-report-opt.pdf). Already in L13. The new point is how to use it: the IFC and Iceland curves are conditional on a field that was later developed, so they are P(well works | field real). The prospect-level prior must stack a separate P(field real), around 0.2 to 0.33, on top.
- Sanyal and Morrow (2012), via L13: success "varies wildly" over the first 5 wells and settles only after about 40 wells. MW per well moves in *steps* that line up with revisions of the conceptual model. This is the cleanest evidence that knowledge (the model) drives outcomes, which is the premise of our company.

### F4. The clay cap is the world's main pre-drill layer, and here is how it is used
- **Physics (standard model).** Smectite and zeolite alteration at about 70 to 220 °C makes a 1 to 10 Ω·m cap. Chlorite and epidote above about 240 °C make a more resistive core. Iceland's Árnason et al. (WGC 2000) and Ussher et al. (WGC 2000) set this out (unverified details; not fetched this run). The *base* of the conductor tracks roughly 200 to 240 °C, which is why the cap's shape is used to aim wells at the upflow.
- **Resistivity measures alteration, not today's temperature.** If a system has cooled, the cap stays (a "fossil" cap). If it heated recently, the rock may be hotter than the clays show. Árnason and ÍSOR state this hysteresis as the main caveat (unverified, from memory). M08 already lists the fossil cap as a false-positive class. The new point is that Iceland and NZ fix it with a temperature tie in a well, not with better MT.
- **A documented false positive: Tutuila, American Samoa (SGW 2017).** MT found a ≤10 Ω·m layer from sea level to about −500 m under most of the survey area. The two gradient holes (about 640 to 670 m deep) reached only about 37 °C, with 20 to 30 °C/km gradients and no hydrothermal alteration. The revised model blamed cold meteoric water and non-hydrothermal clay. https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2017/Wilmarth.pdf . This is a clean case of a high cap likelihood with zero heat, and it should be a labelled negative in the cap likelihood model.
- **Cumming's warning (via M15):** low-resistivity outlines are often several times larger than the real reservoir, so cap *area* must not be used as reservoir area.
- **New Zealand (unverified, from memory).** DSIR-era Schlumberger resistivity outlined the ~23 TVZ fields by a 5 to 10 Ω·m boundary zone (Bibby et al. 1995, JVGR). Mokai was largely concealed at the surface and was delineated by resistivity before drilling. This is the best large-N natural experiment: in a whole province, every field was a resistivity anomaly, and the resistivity area roughly scaled with natural heat output. The catch is that the TVZ is hotter and more clay-rich than the Great Basin.
- **Kenya (unverified, from memory).** KenGen at Olkaria and GDC at Menengai used MT+TEM, gravity, gas geochemistry (fumaroles) and soil CO2 for siting. Olkaria has 300+ wells averaging about 5 to 6 MWe. Menengai had a strong caldera conductor and high-output wells, but some wells were too hot and corrosive. Paka, Korosi and Silali were drilled later with mixed early results. The Kenyan lesson: the best rift prospects all have fumaroles. Blind rift systems are not what Kenya drills.
- **Indonesia and the Philippines (unverified, from memory).** Pertamina (Indonesia) and PNOC-EDC (Philippines) standardised MT+TEM, gas geothermometry and methylene-blue (MeB) clay testing on cuttings. MeB measures smectite content while drilling, so the crew knows when the bit has left the cap and entered the reservoir (Gunderson, Cumming, Astra and Harvey, WGC 2000; unverified). Failures that are often cited are about overestimated size or permeability, not missed heat. The Philippines' Northern Negros 49 MW plant ran far below rating and was retired (unverified). Indonesian greenfield exploration drilling run by government programs (GREM / PT SMI) began in the 2020s because private first-well risk was too high (unverified).
- **Türkiye (unverified, from memory).** Most of the 1.7 GW is in the Büyük Menderes and Gediz grabens: 150 to 240 °C, in fractured marble, quartzite and schist of the Menderes Massif under graben fill, with high CO2. Many newer fields (for example around Aydın and Manisa) had weak surface signs and were found by MT plus gravity plus drilling along graben-bounding faults. This is the closest international analogue to Great Basin blind, fault-hosted and sediment-covered systems. Success depended on hitting the fractured carbonate or marble in the basement, which is a permeability target. A clay cap is thin or absent there, as in the Great Basin.

### F5. What pre-drill data lines up with success, ranked across the cases
1. **A temperature measured near the target** (gradient hole, geothermometer, or a nearby deep well). IFC calls temperature "key to forecasting capacity". Tutuila shows that without it, a cap can mislead.
2. **The shape of the clay cap** (a dome, its base, or a gap over a fault) in volcanic settings. Every big volcanic field abroad was imaged this way. It is weaker in Türkiye and Great Basin-style fault systems.
3. **Hydraulics.** Static pressure (water-level depth) and permeability are the main failure causes in Iceland. No surface method measures them well, which explains why "drilling problems" and "low pressure" stay high even in mature countries.
4. **Not predictive:** broad geology class (IFC), depth (IFC, Iceland) and drilling-practice learning (Sanyal and Morrow).

## What dominates uncertainty (this slice)
1. **P(field real) at prospect level.** Well-level stats are conditional on success. The project-level number (<1/3) has a small, biased sample, and blind systems have almost no international analogue, because most fields abroad have surface manifestations.
2. **Transfer from volcanic to fault-hosted settings.** The clay-cap method was tuned in hot volcanic arcs (Iceland, NZ, Indonesia, Philippines). In Great Basin and Turkish graben settings the cap is thin, patchy or masked by basin brine and lacustrine clay.
3. **Hydraulics.** Pressure and permeability caused most failures in Iceland and cannot be seen well from the surface.
4. **Per-well MW for blind, lower-temperature systems.** The IFC mean of 7.3 MWe comes from high-enthalpy fields. Great Basin binary wells are often lower (a few MWe; unverified). Using the global mean would overstate blind-system MW.
5. **Survivorship bias.** Fields that failed early are under-represented in IFC (mean of 46 wells per field).

## What a sensor could fill
- **TEM loop for MT static shift.** ÍSOR's standard is to run a central-loop TEM sounding at each MT site to correct static shift (unverified detail). A small, cheap TEM transmitter (one coil and battery, plus our E-node as receiver) at a subset of carpet sites fixes the near-surface distortion that H03 named as the E-node's main risk. It also gives the top 100 to 300 m resistivity on its own.
- **Methylene-blue and SWIR clay kit for cuttings.** Ranch water wells, mining holes (M20) and our own gradient holes produce cuttings. A field MeB titration kit (cheap reagents) plus a handheld SWIR/NIR spectrometer measures smectite against illite/chlorite. That turns a cold, shallow hole into evidence of *how far it is to the cap base*, which is where the 200 to 240 °C isotherm sits. It is the cheapest way to tell an active cap from a fossil cap or a lacustrine clay.
- **Static water level and pressure on every drop-probe visit.** Iceland's top failure cause was low pressure. The ranch drop probe already measures pressure. Log static water level and drawdown and recovery over time (a slug or pumping test while the pump is off) as a crude permeability and head layer, and feed it to the hydraulic sub-model.
- **Temperature tie on every conductor** (existing pick). Tutuila shows that the cap needs a temperature tie to count.

## Implications for a better model
1. **Two-level success prior.** P(prospect becomes a field) is about 0.2 to 0.33 (Wall and Dobson), and it is what our existence model (M15 occupancy) has to estimate. P(well n works | field) = 0.07 ln(n) + 0.48 (IFC), with Iceland as a second fit (43% → 74%). Never mix these two. Buyers think in the second number, while we take risk on the first.
2. **Use international fields as a physics-likelihood training set, not as location labels.** IFC, Iceland, NZ and the Philippines hold thousands of wells with resistivity, clay and temperature together. Use them to fit P(T at 1 km > 150 °C | cap shape, cap base depth, conductance, setting). Then down-weight it for fault-hosted settings with a setting-specific term (Türkiye is the bridge analogue).
3. **Explicit false-positive classes for conductors.** Add "cap without heat" (Tutuila: meteoric water, non-hydrothermal clay), "fossil cap" and "basin brine / lacustrine clay" as mixture components. A cap with no temperature tie gets a likelihood ratio of perhaps 2 to 3 (M08). A cap with a tie and a smectite-to-illite transition in cuttings gets far more (my estimate, unverified).
4. **Per-well MW prior: lognormal, setting-specific.** Global mean 7.3, mode 3, skew 1.64 (IFC). Iceland 4.8 ± 2.3. For blind Great Basin binary systems, shift lower and fit to NV well data (D03). The whole-field size still comes from area × power density (M15).
5. **Add a hydraulics term.** Low pressure and low permeability caused more failures than low temperature in Iceland. Add a static-head and permeability sub-model, fed by water levels from the drop probe and by fault slip and dilation tendency (M11), and let it veto hot-but-tight cells.
6. **Do not use setting class as a strong success feature.** IFC found no clear effect. Setting matters for *size* and for which evidence counts, not for the well success rate itself.
7. **Value of the learning curve to our business.** Because the first five wells succeed at about 59%, a buyer pays a large step-up once 1 to 2 wells hit. Our job is to move prospects from the 0.2 to 0.33 "field real" prior to above 0.5 before anyone drills, using cheap sensors. Each 10-point gain at that stage is worth more than the same gain later.
