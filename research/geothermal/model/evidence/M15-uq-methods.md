# M15: Which uncertainty method gives a calibrated chance that a blind system exists, and how big it is

Slice M15, model track. Date: 23 Oct 2026. This builds on `M01-usgs-assessment-methods.md` (count-times-size frame, the shared 11.6x band, F1 < 0.10, Rg ranges), `M13-zanskar-methods.md` (1 blind system per ~550 holes, Bayesian evidential learning, FNO surrogates), `M03-play-fairway-results.md` (Hawaii heat x permeability x fluid "veto", Bayesian neural nets on 83/62 Nevada sites) and `../../evidence/L03-ml-geothermal.md`, `L05`, `Z03`. It does not repeat them. What is new here: a side-by-side of seven uncertainty methods, what each one can and cannot calibrate, the published evidence that today's geothermal capacity estimates are **not** calibrated, and a concrete model design.

Search notes: 10 web searches and fetches worked. Several method papers from outside geothermal (deep ensembles, conformal prediction, occupancy models, simulation-based inference) are cited from my own knowledge, not fetched this session. They are marked (from memory). Anything I inferred is marked (unverified) or "my analysis".

## Bottom line

No single method gives a calibrated answer to both questions. They need two different tools, joined in one Bayesian model. **Existence** ("is there a blind system in this cell?") is a rare-event classification problem with positive-unlabelled labels and a strong exploration bias. The method that can be calibrated is a **hierarchical Bayesian occupancy-detection model** (from ecology). It splits "not found" into "absent" and "present but not yet detected, given how much drilling was done here". Its base rate is pinned by discovery-process statistics (Zanskar's ~1 per 550 holes, USGS 205-system inventory). It is then recalibrated on spatially blocked hold-outs and checked with a reliability diagram. **Size** ("how many MWe?") is best done as **area x power density** (Cumming/Wilmarth lognormal), not heat-in-place. The size prior should be **corrected for size-biased discovery** (big, leaky systems get found first; Kaufman), so blind systems are not given the size of famous ones. Heat-in-place Monte Carlo is not calibrated. Its authors and users say so: Garg and Combs show the P90 swings 3x on the minimum recovery factor alone, Ormat's 2026 analog set has recovery factors from 4% to 53%, and none of the three main papers (Wilmarth 2014, Cumming 2016, Libbey 2026) publishes a predicted-versus-built hindcast. Gaussian processes and the new LANL "In-Context Earth" transformer give **well calibrated temperature-at-depth** (PIT within 2.5% KS), and that is the best sub-model for the heat term. Ensemble disagreement and conformal prediction are useful as **guards** (where the model extrapolates, where to send a probe). They are not a probability of discovery. Simulation-based inference (neural posterior estimation over a fault-and-flow simulator) is the right tool **at the prospect stage**, once a probe or MT line gives local data. The binding limit on calibration is not the method. It is the count of honest labels: with about 10-20 blind discoveries since 2008, nobody can show a reliability curve for existence. **Our probe program changes that.** Every drop-probe gradient in a scored cell is a pre-registered, prospective test. A few hundred of them give the first published calibration curve in geothermal exploration. That is a moat, and a data-room asset buyers will pay for.

## Findings

### F1. Heat-in-place Monte Carlo (USGS, industry): good for structure, not calibrated
- Garg and Combs (SGW 2010) on Silver Peak, Nevada: with the minimum recovery factor at 0.05, the P90-P10 band is 41-146 MWe (3.6x). Setting the minimum to 0 (which they argue is correct before drilling) gives 13-140 MWe (10.8x). Changing thickness and area gives 8-137 MWe (17.1x). **The P10 hardly moves. The P90 moves 5x.** They say exploration-stage P10s are "almost certainly" higher than what will be achieved, and that distribution shape (uniform, triangular, lognormal) changes results in a "poorly studied" way. They recommend using the Monte Carlo only as a go/no-go tool. https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2010/garg.pdf
- Libbey and Murphy (Ormat, SGW 2026) back-solved recovery factors on **40 developed "calibration analogs"** by fixing each field's 30-year average power as the mean. Recovery factors range **about 4% to 53% (median 16%, mean 19%)**. They tie the P90-P10 range of Rg to permeability regime (MPWPI). There is no hit-rate, bias or reliability statistic. https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/db/GeoConf/papers/SGW/2026/Libbey.pdf
- Meaning (my analysis): Ormat's own analog set says the USGS fixed Rg range (0.08-0.25, M01) is too narrow by about 2x on each side. A 4-53% spread is a 13x factor on MWe by itself. Heat-in-place is a fine **physics skeleton** for a Bayesian model. As a free-standing Monte Carlo with expert triangles it has no published calibration.

### F2. Probabilistic power density: simpler, better anchored, still not hindcast
- Wilmarth and Stimac (SGW 2014): **53 fields** (>200 °C, >15 MW, >10 years history). Power density rises about 2 MW/km² per 10 °C. It is **lognormal, 4.1-48.3 MW/km², median 12.0, mean 16.2, fitted P90/P50/P10 = 3.9/13.0/27.8 MW/km²**. Rifts and extensional settings above 270 °C sit above 20 MW/km². Arcs sit below 15. Area is a 500 m buffer around producing wells. No predicted-versus-actual test. https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2014/Wilmarth.pdf
- Cumming (SGW 2016): MWe = area (lognormal, from a range of conceptual models) x power density (lognormal). The product is lognormal, so P10/P50/P90 are closed form. A separate probability-of-success term (POS) for a 1-4 well program multiplies it. He warns that low-resistivity MT contours are often **several times larger than the real reservoir**. He also says about 100 developed fields are far too few for frequentist calibration (he wants 1,000-10,000). https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2016/Cumming.pdf
- Gap for us: the Wilmarth set is >200 °C. Blind Great Basin systems are mostly 120-180 °C binary plays. A **low-temperature power-density curve** (binary plants, 100-200 °C) is not public. We can build it from EIA-923 generation plus BLM unit and well maps (unverified as a done thing; nobody has published it that I found).

### F3. Size-biased discovery: the correction nobody in geothermal applies
- In petroleum, the USGS and MMS model a play as a finite number N of pools with lognormal sizes. Pools are found **in proportion to size, without replacement**. Bayesian predictive distributions then give the number and sizes of the undiscovered pools, with uncertainty in N and in the lognormal parameters (Importance sampling, MCMC). A peer review found the older system "under-represents the degree of uncertainty". Kaufman and Schuenemeyer, AAPG 2007, https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2007/annual/abstracts/lbKaufman.htm ; background on PETRIMES/GRASP, https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/106325
- For geothermal the "size" that drives discovery is surface leakage (hot springs, sinter), not MWe. So the bias is in **leakiness**, which is only partly tied to MWe. A blind system can be big (my analysis). The model should learn two linked variables, size and leakiness, and the discovery chance as a function of both plus exploration effort. USGS 2008 borrowed the identified size distribution unchanged (M01 F1). That is the second-largest driver in M01 F6.

### F4. Existence needs an occupancy-detection model, not a plain classifier
- The core label problem is known: every unexplored cell is labelled "negative" (M01 F1). Caraccioli, Mordensky, Lindsey, DeAngelo, Burns and Lipor (GRC Trans. 2023) show that **expert-picked negatives (62 in the Nevada ML project) "likely imparted modeling bias"**, that XGBoost wins when training reflects the **natural rarity** of systems, and that F1 is unreliable under this class imbalance, so probabilities at known positives are a better check. https://www.usgs.gov/index.php/publications/dont-let-negatives-hold-you-back-accounting-underlying-physics-and-natural
- Positive-unlabelled (PU) learning theory (Elkan and Noto 2008, KDD, from memory): if you know the labelling rate c = P(labelled | positive), you can turn a "labelled vs not" classifier into a true probability by dividing by c. Here c is **not constant**. It depends on local exploration effort.
- Ecology solved this exact problem: **occupancy models** (MacKenzie et al. 2002, Ecology 83:2248, from memory) estimate psi = P(species present) and p = P(detected | present, survey effort) jointly from repeat surveys. Mapping: psi(x) = P(blind system in cell x) from geology. p(x) = 1 - exp(-lambda x effort(x)), where effort = gradient holes, oil and gas wells, water wells logged, MT km, within the cell. Zanskar's "1 blind system per ~550 holes" (M13) is the effort-to-discovery rate that pins lambda.
- Spatial calibration is the trap. A Kazakh bauxite study (Research Square preprint 2025, not peer reviewed) shows ROC-AUC falls from **0.984 (random CV) to ~0.84 (spatial blocks) to 0.77-0.78 (leave-one-deposit-cluster-out)**, with a cluster-bootstrap 95% CI of 0.64-0.80. Conformal sets rejected background terrain well but stayed broad for ore-like terrain. An area-of-applicability mask flagged **20 of 69 top targets as extrapolative**. The authors conclude the map is triage, "not a probability-of-discovery surface". https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10387437/v1 . The same will hold for geothermal maps scored on random CV.

### F5. Temperature at depth: GP/kriging and in-context transformers are already calibrated
- LANL "In-Context Earth" (O'Malley, Johnson, Santos and 10 others, arXiv 2605.16665, 2026): an 830k-parameter transformer that reads the 20 nearest bottom-hole temperatures and outputs a 128-bin temperature distribution. US MAE **4.7 °C** versus 5.1 °C for universal kriging. Error grows with depth: 3.1 °C (0-2 km), 7.9 °C (2-4 km), 11.2 °C (4-6 km). **Calibration: PIT Q-Q tracks uniform, KS 2.5% (vs 5.9% for a Gaussian baseline)**, weaker at depth. Out of distribution (Alberta, Australia, UK) it cuts kriging error 39-78%. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16665
- Meaning: the heat term can be calibrated today with public BHT data. The hard part is not temperature in conductive ground. It is the **convective anomaly**, which is rare and spatially sharp. Kriging smooths it away. In-Context Earth is trained mostly on conductive wells, so it will also under-predict blind upflows (unverified). This is exactly where a probe reading inside the cell beats any interpolator.

### F6. Prospect-scale: Bayesian calibration and simulation-based inference work, and are cheap now
- Auckland group: Bayesian calibration of the Krafla natural-state model (Scott, O'Sullivan, Maclaren, Nicholson et al.) found permeability posteriors **spanning more than two orders of magnitude**, even at a drilled field. https://essopenarchive.org/users/549252/articles/603411-bayesian-calibration-of-a-natural-state-geothermal-reservoir-model-krafla-north-iceland
- Data Space Inversion (de Beer et al., arXiv 2407.15401, 2025) samples the **posterior predictive** (temperature, MWe) directly from an ensemble of prior simulations, without inverting for parameters, and they call it robust and efficient for geothermal models. https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15401v3
- Zanskar's surrogates (Liang et al. SGW 2026, M13 F3): FNO family trained on 1,200 stochastic simulations, UQ not yet run.
- Simulation-based inference (Cranmer, Brehmer and Louppe, PNAS 2020, arXiv 1911.01429, from memory): train a neural posterior estimator on (simulated parameters, simulated observations) pairs. It is **amortized**: once trained on a family of Basin-and-Range fault-upflow models, the posterior for a new site comes back in milliseconds from its probe gradients, spring chemistry and MT. Its calibration can be checked by simulation-based calibration (rank histograms), which is a proper test. My search found no published geothermal-exploration SBI paper. This is open ground.

### F7. Ensembles and conformal prediction: guards, not answers
- Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017, arXiv 1612.01474, from memory) give the best calibration among common neural methods, but **all methods lose calibration under dataset shift** (Ovadia et al., NeurIPS 2019, arXiv 1906.02530, from memory). Blind-system search is shift by design: we score ground with few wells using a model trained where wells are dense.
- The USGS 2025 favorability map's P05-P95 is a 1,200-split ensemble (M01 F3). It measures model and split variance only, not label noise or drilling bias.
- Conformal prediction (Angelopoulos and Bates, arXiv 2107.07511, from memory) gives guaranteed coverage only if test points are exchangeable with calibration points. Weighted conformal (Tibshirani et al. 2019, arXiv 1904.06019, from memory) can fix covariate shift if the shift weights are known. In our case the weights are the exploration-effort model from F4, so the two fit together.
- Use: ensemble spread and an area-of-applicability distance (Meyer and Pebesma 2021, Methods Ecol. Evol., from memory) decide **where to send probes**. They do not price land.

## What dominates uncertainty (per this slice)
1. **Base rate and detection effort.** How many blind systems exist per unit of favorable ground, and how likely each would already have been found given the drilling in that cell. This sets existence probabilities. Today it is one national multiplier (M01).
2. **Recovery factor or power density for blind, low-temperature systems.** Ormat's analogs span 4-53% Rg (13x). Power density spans 3.9-27.8 MW/km² P90-P10 (7x) and is only measured above 200 °C.
3. **Reservoir area.** Cumming: MT low-resistivity outlines are often several times too large. Area enters MWe linearly.
4. **Size bias between found and blind systems.** Unknown sign in practice, and never modelled in geothermal.
5. **Too few honest outcomes to test calibration.** About 100 developed fields (Cumming) and perhaps 10-20 post-2008 blind discoveries (unverified count). A reliability bin with 30 outcomes at p = 0.2 has a standard error of about ±0.07, so a usable curve needs several hundred labelled cells (my arithmetic).
6. **Permeability.** Two or more orders of magnitude even at Krafla after calibration. It drives whether heat becomes MWe.

## What a sensor could fill
- **Prospective calibration labels at scale.** The ranch water-well drop probe and 2 m probe lines give a binary or ordinal outcome (convective gradient or not) in a cell we scored before the visit. A few hundred of them give the first real reliability diagram for geothermal existence probabilities. Cost per label is a site visit, versus about $1-2M per gradient hole.
- **Effort data.** Each probe visit adds known "survey effort" to its cell, so the occupancy model can say "searched and absent" instead of "unlabelled". This is what turns PU learning into proper inference.
- **Local temperature to replace the interpolator inside anomalies.** In-Context Earth and kriging are calibrated in conductive ground but smooth out upflows. A measured gradient in the cell removes the largest error term exactly where it matters.
- **Area constraints for size.** E-field MT nodes give clay-cap outline and conductance. Used inside SBI (not as a raw contour, per Cumming's warning), they narrow the area term.
- **Chemistry for temperature.** A conductivity reading plus grab sample for geothermometers narrows reservoir temperature, which sets the power-density curve.
- What a sensor cannot fill cheaply: recovery factor and permeability. Those stay as priced priors until a flow test, and we hand that risk to the buyer.

## Implications for a better model
1. **Build a two-part hierarchical Bayesian model.** Part A, existence: psi(x) = logistic(features, gradient-boosting score, hierarchical region effects). Detection p(x) = 1 - exp(-lambda x effort(x)). Pin lambda with discovery history (Zanskar's ~1/550 holes, USGS discovery dates). Part B, size: MWe = area x power density, both lognormal, with a size-and-leakiness discovery bias term (Kaufman style). Fit in Stan or NumPyro. The output is a full posterior for each cell: P(exists) and the MWe distribution conditional on existence, and so expected MWe and P(MWe > 20).
2. **Use the In-Context Earth or GP temperature model as a calibrated sub-model** for the heat term, and pass its predictive distribution into Part B.
3. **Score calibration the honest way.** Spatial-block and leave-one-cluster-out hold-outs, reliability diagram, Brier and log score. Never quote random-CV AUC. Use conformal and an area-of-applicability mask to flag cells where the model extrapolates.
4. **Pre-register every probe.** Before each field visit, freeze the cell's predicted P(convective gradient) and log it. After the visit, record the outcome. Publish the reliability curve once it has a few hundred points. No competitor has one. Zanskar's own calibration is self-reported ("3 of 3", M13).
5. **Route probes by value of information, not by top score.** Visit cells where (a) the posterior is uncertain and (b) the land is still cheap. Ensemble spread plus AOA distance is the targeting signal. This maximises both calibration learning and land alpha.
6. **At the prospect stage, switch to amortized SBI.** Train a neural posterior estimator on thousands of fault-upflow simulations (cheap compute). Feed it probe gradients, chemistry and MT. Its MWe posterior, checked by simulation-based calibration, is what goes in the buyer data room in place of a heat-in-place triangle.
7. **Build the missing low-temperature power-density curve** from EIA-923 generation and BLM well maps for US binary plants. It is public data that nobody has assembled, and it fixes the weakest size input for blind Great Basin plays.
8. **Replace one national band with local bands.** Because the occupancy model uses local effort, cells with heavy drilling and no find get tight, low posteriors. Cells with high favorability and no effort keep wide, high-upside posteriors. That is exactly the list of land to lock first.
