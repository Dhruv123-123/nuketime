# L18: Point processes, occupancy-detection and search-effort correction

Date: 24 Oct 2026. Scope: what ecology and petroleum statistics know about counting things you cannot fully see, and how it maps onto hidden geothermal systems. SENTINEL-COX (`../../model/MODEL.md`) already uses a thinned log-Gaussian Cox process (LGCP) and a shared mask latent. This file checks that choice against the literature and lists what to add. Nine web searches were run. The web-fetch budget ran out after one fetch, so most details come from abstracts and my own reading. Unchecked numbers are marked (unverified).

## What the field knows

**1. Presence-only data are a thinned point process.** Warton and Shepherd (2010, Annals of Applied Statistics) showed that "presence plus background" modelling is a Poisson point process. Renner and Warton (2013, Biometrics) showed MaxEnt is the same model. So a geothermal favorability map trained on known systems vs. random background cells is an inhomogeneous Poisson intensity. It is not a probability of occurrence. Its scale is set by how hard people looked.

**2. Observed intensity = true intensity x detection x effort.** Fithian, Elith, Hastie and Keith (2015, Methods in Ecology and Evolution) pooled survey data and museum records for many species. All species share one bias field. The shared field is what makes the bias identifiable. Dorazio (2014, Global Ecology and Biogeography, https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70147923) wrote the joint model: a point process for occurrence, thinned by survey bias and by imperfect detection. He showed bias and detection cannot be separated from presence-only data alone. You need some planned surveys with repeat visits. Koshkina et al. (2017, MEE, https://www.usgs.gov/publications/integrated-species-distribution-models-combining-presence-background-data-and-site) fit both data types in one likelihood. The planned surveys pin detection. The presence-only records add spatial coverage.

**3. Occupancy models separate "absent" from "missed".** MacKenzie et al. (2002, Ecology 83:2248) use repeat visits to a site. The detection history (for example 0-1-0) identifies the detection rate p and the occupancy psi. If p = 0.3 and you visit three times, you miss 1 - 0.7^3 = 34% of occupied sites (my arithmetic). Royle and Nichols (2003, Ecology) tie p to abundance, so big populations are easier to see. Royle and Link (2006, Ecology, https://www.uvm.edu/~tdonovan/Occupancy%20Exercises/Exercise12/Royle_Link_2006.pdf) add false positives. Miller et al. (2011, Ecology) mix "certain" and "uncertain" detections. Nichols et al. (2008, Journal of Applied Ecology) use several detection methods per site. That is the ecological version of our "two detectors per cell" design. Guillera-Arroita (2017, Ecography) reviews the whole field.

**4. Spatial occupancy now scales.** Doser et al. (2022, MEE) released spOccupancy. It uses nearest-neighbour Gaussian processes (NNGP) and fits hundreds of thousands of sites. Integrated models now have R tooling: inlabru and PointedSDMs (Mostert and O'Hara 2023, MEE, https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.06.511075.full.pdf).

**5. Integration helps only if bias is modelled.** Isaac et al. (2020, Trends in Ecology and Evolution) set out the integrated distribution model agenda. Fletcher et al. (2019, Ecology, https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10110684) gave a practical guide. Simmonds et al. (2020, Ecography) ran simulations. Adding biased presence-only data helped little, and could hurt, when the bias was not modelled. It helped when the bias was modelled with a covariate (unverified, from memory of the abstract).

**6. Preferential sampling biases maps.** Diggle, Menezes and Su (2010, JRSS-C, https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/51919/) showed that when sample sites are chosen because the value is high, kriging is biased. The fix is a joint model of the sample locations and the values, sharing one latent field. Temperature wells are drilled where people expect water or heat. So our temperature-at-depth field is preferentially sampled.

**7. Petroleum discovery-process models count what is left.** Kaufman, Balcer and Kruyt (1975) and Barouch and Kaufman (1977) model discovery as sampling without replacement, proportional to size (https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/48701). Big fields are found first. The dated discovery sequence then tells you how many small and mid fields remain. Lee and Wang built PETRIMES on this (https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/106325). This is the time-axis twin of spatial thinning.

**8. Minerals and geothermal already saw the bias.** Coolbaugh, Raines and Zehner (2007, Natural Resources Research, https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70029904) studied Great Basin geothermal systems. They found that data-driven favorability maps are distorted by exploration bias (known systems lie near hot springs, roads and old wells). They used that bias to estimate undiscovered resources. Carranza (https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/27348/) went from prospectivity maps to counts of undiscovered prospects. Bilal et al. (2026, Remote Sensing, doi 10.3390/rs18132091) split geology from effort proxies (night lights, population) in a positive-unlabeled porphyry copper model.

Main lesson: the gains are modest in ranking AUC. They are large in **absolute counts**. Ignoring detection biases N low by the full missed fraction. That number drives our bank's value.

## Datasets used

- Ecology: eBird checklists (complete lists with effort fields: duration, distance, observers), GBIF presence-only records, North American Breeding Bird Survey (planned repeat routes), UK BTO atlases. "Complete checklist" is the key idea. A list that says "everything I saw" turns non-reports into non-detections.
- Petroleum: Alberta and North Sea dated field discoveries with sizes (PETRIMES); USGS play assessments.
- Minerals and geothermal: Coolbaugh's Great Basin geothermal GIS (https://data.nbmg.unr.edu/public/Geothermal/GreyLiterature/Coolbaugh_GeothermalGIS_NV_2002.pdf); ASTER, Landsat, SRTM, VIIRS night lights, GHSL population (Bilal 2026); USGS MRDS.
- Our effort analogues: NDWR 146,235 driller reports; CA DWR OSWCR about 1.1M; TG holes in OFR 99-425; INGENIOUS 2 m stations (GDR 1391); NDOM and UT DOGM wells.

## What this means for our model design

- **Keep the thinned LGCP. It is the right model.** The Warton-Renner-Fithian line proves favorability-vs-background is a Poisson intensity. SENTINEL-COX just makes the thinning explicit.
- **Treat driller reports as "complete checklists".** A Nevada driller log that records water temperature, or says "cold", is a non-detection with known effort and depth. Logs that skip temperature are presence-only effort. AI extraction should tag each log as one of: warm detection, explicit cold, or silent. That turns millions of silent wells into graded effort.
- **Share one bias field across "species".** Fithian's trick maps to us directly. Hot springs, mines, oil wells, water wells and mineral occurrences are all found by the same access-driven effort. Fit them jointly with a shared effort field. Then effort is identifiable from the other "species", not only from geothermal finds.
- **Add a Diggle-style preferential-sampling term to the temperature field.** Let well locations and the thermal latent share the Gaussian field. Otherwise warm valleys look warmer than they are, and empty ranges look colder.
- **Add a size-biased time axis.** Use the dated discovery sequence (Steamboat, Dixie Valley, then blind finds like Blue Mountain, Patua, McGinness, Zanskar's Big Blind) as successive sampling proportional to size and visibility. This gives a second, independent estimate of N_hidden to check the spatial one.
- **Design the field survey as a multi-method occupancy study.** Nichols 2008 says use two or more methods per site, with K >= 2 visits or methods, picked by stratified-random sampling. Our Sentinel well visit plus a 2 m probe or E-MT line is exactly that. Pre-register it.
- **Use time-to-detection to save visits.** In ecology, time to first detection in one visit replaces repeat visits. For us, depth to first anomalous gradient in one hole plays that role. It identifies p from a single hole.
- **Model false positives too.** Royle-Link and Miller 2011 matter. Warm water from deep circulation in normal-gradient basins, or a mis-extracted "hot" word in an OCR'd log, is a false positive. Give AI-extracted readings a "certain/uncertain" tag and a confusion rate.

## Gaps nobody has filled that we could exploit with AI

1. **No one has fit an integrated occupancy point process to resource discovery.** Ecology does it routinely. Minerals stop at PU learning with effort proxies. Geothermal stops at Coolbaugh 2007. We would be first. Spend: weeks, not years.
2. **Effort is always a proxy (roads, night lights).** No paper builds effort from the actual dated record of who looked, where, at what depth. LLM extraction of driller logs, BLM lease files, USGS open-file reports and old theses can build a true effort cube E(x, depth, year, method).
3. **No checklist-completeness inference for wells.** An AI reader can classify each log as "would have recorded heat if present" or not. That is the eBird "complete list" flag, recovered from scans.
4. **No size-biased discovery model for geothermal.** The data exist (dated discoveries, MWe). An agent can assemble the sequence for the US and for analog basins (Taupo, Kenya, Turkey) for a hierarchical prior.
5. **Active learning on psi x (1 - p).** Ecology picks survey sites with expected-information designs, but rarely with money attached. Our VOI-Bank can pick sites by dollars of land value per unit of detection uncertainty, and update weekly.
6. **Simulation-based checks with real effort geometry.** Synthetic Nevadas with planted systems, thinned by the real extracted effort, can show where N is identifiable before we buy anything.
