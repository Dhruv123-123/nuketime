# L03: USGS Great Basin favorability and temperature-at-depth ML (2024-2026)

Date: 24 Oct 2026. Builds on `../../evidence/L03-ml-geothermal.md` and `../../model/evidence/M01`, `M14`, `M16`, `M31`. New here: a leakage audit of the 2025 USGS map from its full text, the 2024 elevation result, and the 2026 releases.

## What the field knows

The USGS team (Stanley Mordensky, Jacob DeAngelo, Erick Burns, with John Lipor and Pascal Caraccioli at Portland State) is rebuilding the 2008 Great Basin hydrothermal assessment. The work has four parts.

1. **Favorability map.** Mordensky, Burns, Lipor and DeAngelo (2025), *Geothermics* 133, 103450, CC BY. Full text: https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1873&context=ece_fac . This is the map the new assessment will be conditioned on.
2. **Temperature at depth.** Burns, DeAngelo and Williams (2024), a 3D conductive model on a 4 km grid from 1 to 6 km (doi:10.5066/P149FR54; SGW 2024 paper https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/db/GeoConf/papers/SGW/2024/Burns.pdf). It is physics, not ML. It fits only 379 of 4,672 wells deeper than 50 m. The other 4,293 are "non-conductive", meaning water flow disturbs them. DeAngelo, Burns and Mordensky (2025, doi:10.5066/P14B8XOH) push spring geothermometer temperatures into this model to get a circulation depth per spring. Some come out deeper than 6 km.
3. **System inventory.** DeAngelo, Burns and Mordensky (SGW 2026; https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70273870 ; data doi:10.5066/P13AXSYL). It lists 205 systems: 28 with plants, 78 known electric-grade, and 99 new "probable". Electric-grade means a well above 85 °C with evidence of convection. Points within 10 km are merged into one system. Each system gets one "throat of upflow" point. Undiscovered resource will be estimated by conditioning system counts on favorability level. That number has not been published yet.
4. **Method papers.** "When less is more" (2023), "Cursed?" (GRC 2023), "Don't let negatives hold you back" (Caraccioli, GRC 2023), and the elevation paper (Caraccioli et al., GRC Transactions 48, 2217-2236, 2024; https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70264692). The elevation paper splits elevation into a regional trend and a valley-scale residual. The old models scored western Nevada higher simply because elevation rises to the east. Splitting the two signals reduced this bias but did not remove it. Models with the split signals "consistently" did better (no numbers in the abstract). It also confirms that random negatives beat expert-chosen ones.
5. **2026 zone model.** Mordensky and DeAngelo (2026, doi:10.5066/P14QM39R). It uses 53 public features over the INGENIOUS region. Unsupervised clustering splits the region into "zones", or regimes, and a supervised model runs per zone. The release gives a P50 of 120 realisations. The paper's skill numbers were not found (unverified).

## Best methods and their reported skill

**Mordensky et al. 2025, from the full text:**
- **Labels.** They start from 3,869 well "convective signals": measured well heat flow minus the modelled conductive heat flow (DeAngelo 2022, 2023). Low signals within 4 km of a higher signal are dropped, leaving 3,275. These are binned into ordinal classes: low (-25 to 25 mW/m²), high (50 to 325) and very high (>325). The 25 to 50 band is left out. Counts: 709 low, 1,272 high, 522 very high. Those sum to 3,225, not 3,275, and the paper does not explain the gap.
- **Pseudo-negatives.** Each split adds 2,170 random cells labelled "low". Each must be more than 4 km from a high or very high well.
- **Features (16).** Conductive heat flow; distance to Quaternary faults; distance to Quaternary magmatism; magnetics; isostatic gravity; depth to basement; shear, dilation and second-invariant strain rate; two seismic densities; and five MT conductance layers from 2 to 200 km. No spring or manifestation layer is used, which is good.
- **Model.** XGBoost as ordinal regression, with cut points at 0.5 and 1.5. Hyperparameters come from 5-fold CV. They run 1,200 Monte Carlo realisations. **Each one is a random 80/20 split, not a spatial one.**
- **Results.** 15 of 28 power-producing systems fall in the top 1% of the map. The abstract says 26 of 28 sit above the 80th percentile; the Discussion says 24 of 28 are high or very high. The two do not match. High plus very high covers about 9% of the map. AUC on power plants is 95.3, against 75.2 for Coolbaugh 2005 and 72.2 ± 2.2 for the 56 models of 2008. AUC on the 2008 training sites is 84.7, against 68.8. Hidden and exposed systems score about the same: 5 of 9 hidden and 10 of 19 exposed are very high. Poorly predicted systems: McGinness Hills, Don Campbell, Elko and Wabuska.
- **Leakage audit (my reading).**
  1. *Neighbour leakage.* The splits are random and wells cluster in fields. Near-copies of the same field land in both train and test. The paper does not discuss spatial autocorrelation.
  2. *The plant test is not independent.* The paper itself notes that training wells sit near many power-producing systems. The 28 plants are exactly where wells are densest. So "15 of 28 in the top 1%" and "AUC 95" mostly measure recall of known fields, not discovery.
  3. *"Hidden" does not mean undiscovered.* The 9 hidden systems are producing fields (for example Don A. Campbell and McGinness Hills) with many wells. They test whether the map can score a system with no surface expression. They do not test whether it can find one before anyone drills.
  4. *Feature and label share a parent.* The label is measured heat flow minus a conductive model. The conductive heat-flow feature comes from the same well heat-flow compilation (DeAngelo 2023). How strongly they are coupled is not quantified (unverified).
  5. *Sampling bias, which the paper admits.* Low-signal wells sit in areas of high conductive heat flow, and the CDFs are biased for 10 of the 16 layers. Wells go where people already expected heat.
  6. *Collinearity.* Some layers correlate up to |0.94|.

  None of this makes the map wrong. It means the true out-of-area skill for a new, blind upflow is unknown and probably lower.

**Other skill numbers (from prior files, for context):** F1 below 0.10 for every model on the 2008 problem (2023). Half of the high-signal wells captured in the top 3% of area by truncated XGBoost (GRC 2023). Stanford InterPIGNN temperature MAE 4.8 °C under a 10 km spatial block hold-out (Aljubran and Horne 2024). It is the only spatially blocked number here.

## Datasets used

- Mordensky 2025 features and predictions, ScienceBase 66e88690d34e0606a9db9b43, doi:10.5066/P14EET2C (P05, P25, P50, P75, P95 rasters).
- Well heat-flow and convective-signal compilation, DeAngelo et al. 2023, doi:10.5066/P9BZPVUC (3,869 signals).
- 3D temperature model v1.1, doi:10.5066/P149FR54. Geothermometer depths, doi:10.5066/P14B8XOH.
- 205-system inventory, doi:10.5066/P13AXSYL. Zone data with 53 features, doi:10.5066/P14QM39R.
- NVML grid: 1,695,126 cells, 83 positives and 62 negatives (Caraccioli 2026, *Geothermics* 103833).
- Inputs: USGS Quaternary fault database; Zeng 2022 strain rates; EarthScope/USArray MT conductance; Nevada Bureau of Mines gravity and magnetics; ANSS seismicity; INGENIOUS (GDR 1391).
- EGS assessment data (135 GWe), doi:10.5066/P14LU7WD.

## What this means for our model design

- **Re-score the USGS map honestly before beating it.** Rerun their exact XGBoost on doi:10.5066/P14EET2C with spatial blocks of 20 to 50 km and a leave-one-system-out test on the 28 plants. That gives a true baseline gap, likely much lower than AUC 95. It is also a cheap, publishable credibility asset (~1 GPU-day).
- **Treat the 4,293 "non-conductive" wells as our richest label pool.** USGS drops them from the temperature model. We should fit them with a censored column likelihood: upflow, downflow or lateral outflow. This is how SENTINEL-COX's 1D column model gets thousands of extra labels.
- **Keep the ordinal or continuous convective-signal target, but model it as censored.** Drop the arbitrary 25 to 50 mW/m² gap. Treat each signal as a noisy measurement with depth and quality metadata, not as a class.
- **Replace random pseudo-negatives with search-effort-thinned negatives.** The 2,170 random "low" cells are exactly the selection bias SENTINEL-COX's dated search-effort layer is meant to fix. Use well density, road distance and lease history as the propensity.
- **Use the USGS P95 minus P05 spread as a free prior on where to buy information.** But widen it, because it holds only split variance. It contains no label noise, no spatial leakage and no sampling bias.
- **Remove regional trends from every feature, not only elevation.** The 2024 result shows XGBoost exploits west-east gradients. Detrend heat flow, strain and MT, then run adversarial checks: can latitude and longitude alone predict the label?
- **Race the 99 "probable" systems and the coming undiscovered-resource release.** When USGS publishes counts conditioned on favorability, the top-percentile cells become common knowledge. Lease-status checks and nominations on these cells come first.
- **Model per regime.** The zone approach (53 features, per-zone models) and the badly predicted systems (McGinness Hills, Elko, Wabuska) both say that one global model mixes system types. A shared latent mask or system-type class handles this better than hard zones.

## Gaps nobody has filled that we could exploit with AI

1. **No spatially blocked or time-split skill number exists for any USGS favorability map.** An AI agent can run the full CV grid (block sizes, leave-one-system-out, pre-2000 train and post-2000 test) over every public release in days.
2. **No model uses the shape of the temperature log.** Labels are a single heat-flow value per well. AI digitisation of scanned logs (GDR, NBMG, BLM well files) would give curvature: outflow reversals and isothermal sections. That separates "masked" from "cold".
3. **No ML temperature-at-depth model for the Great Basin includes convection.** Burns 2024 is conductive by design. A physics-informed model trained on the residuals, using geothermometer depths as soft constraints, would map where the conductive model is wrong. Those places are the targets.
4. **No one models the labelling propensity.** No one asks why a well exists where it does. Language-model reading of BLM case files, water-rights logs and mining claims can build a dated search-effort layer.
5. **No one has validated against negative outcomes.** Dry and cold holes from operator reports and DOE-funded drilling are not used as test cases. Agents can mine GRC papers, BLM sundry notices and state well files to build that set.
6. **There is no feature-level uncertainty.** Heat flow is interpolated, but its kriging variance is not passed to the model. Propagating it is cheap and changes where we sample.
