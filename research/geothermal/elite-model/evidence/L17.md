## What the field knows

Oil and gas spent 60 years learning to price a hole before drilling it. Four tools matter for us.

1. **The petroleum system and play.** Magoon and Dow (1994, AAPG Memoir 60) define a petroleum system as source, reservoir, seal, trap, plus timing of generation and migration. A **play** is a family of prospects that share the same system and risks. Geothermal already has the same frame: Moeck (2014, Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews 37:867-882) catalogued geothermal play types; Play Fairway Analysis (Shervais et al., Snake River Plain, https://gdr.openei.org/files/1231/Shervais-SRP%20PFA-EE0006733-Phase1.pdf; Nielson 2015, https://pangea.stanford.edu/ERE/pdf/IGAstandard/SGW/2015/Nielson.pdf) borrowed the oil word "fairway" directly.
2. **Chance-of-success (CoS) matrices.** White (1993, AAPG Bull. 77:2048, "Geologic risking guide for prospects and plays") and Otis and Schneidermann (1997, AAPG Bull. 81(7):1087-1109, https://archives.datapages.com/data/bulletns/1997/07jul/1087/1087.htm) set the standard. Geologic chance Pg = P(source) x P(reservoir) x P(trap/seal) x P(timing/migration). Each factor is read off a table that maps **evidence quality** (none, analog, direct data) and **favourability** to a number. Milkov (2015, Earth-Science Reviews 150:453-476, "Risk tables for less biased and more consistent estimation of probability of geological success") made these tables explicit so two geologists give the same number (unverified page range). The key split is **shared (play) risk vs local (prospect) risk**. Pg(prospect) = P(play works) x P(this prospect works | play works). Common Risk Segment (CRS) maps paint the shared factors as traffic-light polygons, then multiply them (https://sherwoodgeo.com/play-fairway-analysis-and-common-risk-segment-mapping.html). Shell and others patented automated play chance mapping (US 8,749,549, https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8749549).
3. **Dependency between prospects.** Because prospects in one play share risk, a dry hole lowers the chance of its neighbours and a discovery raises it. Delfiner (2003, SPE 84439, "Modeling dependencies between geologic risks") and Van Wees et al. (2008, TNO, Bayesian belief network for prospect interdependency in the Netherlands) formalise this (unverified details). Portfolio value is not the sum of stand-alone values. Drilling the "information well" first can be worth more than drilling the best well first.
4. **Discovery process models and creaming curves.** Arps and Roberts (1958, AAPG Bull. 42) modelled discoveries in the Denver-Julesburg basin as dF = F(1 - exp(-c A w / B)): fields of area A are found at a rate set by wildcat count w, basin area B and a "discovery efficiency" c. Kaufman, Balcer and Kruyt (1975) recast this as **successive sampling without replacement, probability proportional to size^beta** from a finite lognormal population. Barouch and Kaufman (1977) gave estimators. Meisner and Demirmen (1981, J. Royal Stat. Soc. A 144:1-31, "The creaming method") fit Shell's creaming curves with a Bayesian version and forecast remaining discoveries. Lee and Wang at the Geological Survey of Canada built PETRIMES on the same idea (Lee 2008, "Statistical Methods for Estimating Petroleum Resources", Oxford UP). Drew, Schuenemeyer and Bawiec (1982, USGS PP 1252, https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1252) added economic truncation: small fields go unreported, which bends the observed size curve. Lillestøl and Sinding-Larsen (NHH 2016, "Log-normal creaming and the likelihood of discovering additional giant petroleum fields", https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/2374986) is a modern lognormal-creaming take. Kaufman and Schuenemeyer (2007) reviewed the old USGS/MMS systems and found they **understate uncertainty** (cited in M15).

## Best methods and their reported skill (numbers)

The honest skill test in oil is the **lookback**: archived pre-drill estimates vs results.

| Study | Result |
|---|---|
| Norway regulator (Sodir/NPD) lookback, 195 wildcats 1990-97 | Volumes over-estimated by a factor of **2.5** on average |
| Sodir, wildcats 1998-2007 | Over-estimate factor **2.5** |
| Sodir, targets 2007-16 | Oil: **58%** inside predicted range, 36% below, mean over-estimate **1.4x**. Gas: 47% inside, mean **2.1x** |
| Sodir, 8th-14th rounds (1997 report) | Volumes overstated **2.2x**; outcome inside range in only **2 of 10** cases. Success chance was **understated** |
| Sodir, dry-target cause 2007-16 | North Sea: source/migration **46%**, trap **28%**, reservoir absent 16%, poor reservoir 10% |
| Bond and Bagley (2018), large recent discoveries | Many fell below the pre-drill **P80**, i.e. in the bottom fifth of the forecast (via GeoConvention 2020) |
| Citron et al. (2017) assurance survey | **37 of 48** teams (77%) do post-well reviews; only 40% share results with explorers |
| Otis and Schneidermann (1997), Chevron | After adopting the process, portfolio predicted and actual success counts roughly matched over about five years (unverified numbers) |
| Milkov (2020, AAPG Bull., BLTN18128) | Explorers' aggregate CoS forecasts were roughly calibrated, but individual prospects were poorly discriminated; luck dominates small portfolios (unverified detail; https://archives.datapages.com/data/bulletns/2020/01jan/BLTN18128/bltn18128.html) |
| Meisner and Demirmen (1981) | Creaming fits with beta near 0.5-1 for mature Shell provinces (unverified) |

Three lessons repeat across all of them. First, **chance is roughly calibrated once firms track it; volume is not**. Bias is 1.4-2.5x high on size. Second, **calibration came from feedback, not from better geology**: Citron (2000, https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2000/annual/abstracts/0120.htm) says bias is "endemic" but falls with lookbacks. Third, **failure cause matters**. Classifying why each dry hole failed and comparing with the pre-drill weakest factor is how risk tables get recalibrated.

**How majors rank prospects.** The standard is expected monetary value: EMV = Pc x NPV(success) - (1 - Pc) x dry-hole cost, where commercial chance Pc = Pg x P(volume > minimum economic field size). Ranking uses EMV per dollar of exposure, then a portfolio step: Markowitz-style efficient frontier of EMV vs variance (Hightower and David 1991; Ball and Savage 1999), plus utility for risk aversion (Cozzolino 1977) (all unverified details). Value of information for seismic or an appraisal well follows Newendorp (1975) and Bratvold and Begg (2010, "Making Good Decisions", SPE). Benchmarking firms sell the outcome data: Westwood's Wildcat and prospect-risk reports (https://www.westwoodenergy.com/reports/custom-reports/prospect-risk-and-benchmarking-report).

**Petroleum systems (basin) modelling** is the physics layer. PetroMod and Temis compute burial and thermal history, calibrated to bottom-hole temperatures and vitrinite reflectance (Hantschel and Kauerauf 2009). This is the same thermal calibration problem as our 1D column likelihoods (M04, M16). Probabilistic basin models already treat heat flow as an uncertain input and sample it.

## Datasets used

- **Sodir (NPD) FactPages and resource reports**: every Norwegian wellbore, discovery, and pre-drill vs post-drill volume (https://www.sodir.no/en/whats-new/publications/reports/resource-report/resource-report-2018/chapter-7). The best public lookback set in the world.
- **UK NSTA well and field data**, **BOEM Gulf of Mexico field reserves with discovery year** (classic creaming-curve inputs).
- **USGS National Oil and Gas Assessment** play files and field size data; USGS PP 1252.
- **Commercial**: S&P Global (IHS) EDIN, Wood Mackenzie, Westwood Wildcat. Paid, but the outcome tables are what majors calibrate on.
- **Geothermal twins we already hold or know**: IFC 2013 well success set (2,613 wells), BLM lease sale history, GDR Play Fairway layers, INGENIOUS, TNO ThermoGIS (Dutch geothermal assessed with oil-style play risk).

## What this means for our model design

- **Split risk into shared play risk and local prospect risk.** Our shared latent mask class (MODEL.md) is the right place. Add a play-level term per structural setting and basin (heat source, permeability, cap/mask) so a dry TG hole lowers the chance of every cell that shares that play, as in Delfiner and Van Wees.
- **Publish a geothermal risk table.** Build a Milkov-style table: for each element (heat, permeability, fluid, cap, mask), map evidence quality (none, analog, regional layer, direct gradient, slimhole) to a probability band. Let the ML fill it, but force every cell's number to trace back to the table. Buyers and BLM bidders already think in this language.
- **Rank by EMV per dollar of exposure, then by portfolio.** RNPV/acre is our EMV. Add Pc = P(exists) x P(MW > minimum economic size at our capture stage) explicitly, then choose leases on an efficient frontier that penalises shared-play concentration. Correlated leases in one play are one bet, not ten.
- **Use the Arps-Roberts discovery efficiency as an effort-thinning prior.** Our Cox count already thins by search effort. Fit c per province and per search channel (springs, oil wells, mining holes, TG holes). M33 found beta near zero for Nevada size bias; keep beta as a fitted parameter, not a fixed 1.
- **Shrink size forecasts by the oil lookback bias until our own lookback exists.** Oil explorers overstate volume 1.4-2.5x. Apply a default 0.5-0.7x haircut to MW-if-exists in the economics layer (my judgment), and report it.
- **Make lookback a first-class output.** Archive every pre-drill P(exists), T(z) and MW forecast with a hash and date. Log the failure cause for every cool hole by element. This is the cheapest calibration engine in the industry, and Norway shows it works.
- **Plan drilling order for information, not only for value.** In dependent portfolios, test the cell whose result moves the most other cells first. That is EVSI over the shared play term, which the VOI-Bank spine can compute.

## Gaps nobody has filled that we could exploit with AI

1. **No geothermal lookback database exists.** Oil has Sodir and Westwood; geothermal has nothing public. An AI pipeline over GRC papers, BLM sundry notices, state well files and OSTI reports can rebuild pre-drill expectations vs outcomes for hundreds of US geothermal wells. That becomes our calibration set and a sellable data product.
2. **Risk tables are hand-written.** An LLM panel can draft element-by-element risk for every cell from the evidence stack, citing sources, then be calibrated against outcomes. That gives consistent CoS at a scale no team of geologists can match.
3. **Creaming curves by search channel.** Oil plots one curve per basin. We can plot one per data type (springs, oil wells, mining holes, our sensors). A step up after a new data type is direct proof of edge.
4. **Automated dry-hole cause classification.** Extract the failure reason (too cool, no permeability, cold-water mask) from reports with AI, then update each element's risk table.
5. **Dependency learned, not assumed.** Fit the play correlation from the spatial pattern of hits and misses in the historical record, rather than from expert-set dependency factors.
