# Lane B: the ceiling. Exploration metres, spend, and the drilling-contractor industry

Written 19 Oct 2026. Tags: (memory) = from prior knowledge, not re-verified this session; (estimate) = my arithmetic or judgement.
Note: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through this lane. Gaps that need a follow-up search are listed in section 9.

## 10-line summary
1. Global nonferrous exploration budgets were USD 12.40B in 2025 (S&P), the third flat or falling year in a row. Minesite work hit a record 45% of that and grassroots a record low 21%. All-metals spend including iron ore and coal is about USD 15-17B (estimate). MinEx: USD 16.1B in 2022, against a USD 42.1B peak in 2012.
2. Australia (ABS) drilled 11.8 million metres in the four quarters to June 2026 on A$4.27B of spend, about A$360 of total spend per metre. Only 22% of those metres were drilled for *new* deposits; 78% were around existing deposits.
3. Global exploration drilling (estimate): about 50-80 million metres a year and USD 6-9B a year paid to drilling contractors (central about USD 7.5B). The true greenfield "discovery hole" slice is only about USD 1.5-2B a year.
4. Uranium is small. Canada's uranium exploration was CAD 458M in 2025, with CAD 737M intended for 2026 (NRCan). Global uranium drilling is about USD 0.35-0.6B a year, and Athabasca drilling about CAD 300-450M a year (estimate).
5. Contractors are a low-margin, fragmented, cyclical business. Major Drilling, the biggest, did CAD 889M revenue in FY26 at an 11.6% EBITDA margin with a 688-rig fleet. Foraco did USD 258M at 18%. Boart Longyear, about USD 2B in revenue at its 2012 peak (memory), sold to AIP in 2024 for about USD 371M.
6. Fleet utilisation runs at 40-75%: Boart 47% (2023), Foraco 40%, Major about 50%, Capital 74%. The binding constraint right now is labour, not rigs; Major says "the pool of available experienced drillers [is] drying up". A working rig earns about USD 1.7-2.3M a year (Capital's revenue per operating rig is USD 191k a month).
7. The technology and tooling layer earns better margins but is small. IMDEX captured USD 2.40 per USD 100 of global exploration spend: A$520M revenue at a 31% EBITDA margin. That is exactly the "$100-400M/yr tool market" the rubric rejects.
8. MinEx: the average discovery cost tripled to USD 218M, and globally, exploration from 2012 to 2021 returned 0.69 of what it cost (USD 196B spent, USD 135B of discovery value). The value pool is the discoveries, about USD 13B a year, not the drilling.
9. Verdict: a company selling "cheaper holes" as a service or tool tops out at roughly the size of IMDEX or Major Drilling (USD 0.5-1B revenue). The very large outcome comes from owning the rights that cheaper or fewer holes de-risk (a prospect generator or explorer), or possibly from an autonomous-rig contractor that breaks the labour cap.
10. Against the rubric: a pure service fails test 6 (ceiling) and test 1 (the physical step is the whole product). A cheap-drilling capability used inside an asset-owning explorer passes, but it then looks like Terranox's own ladder.

## 2. Global metres and the drilling-contract market: derivation (estimate)

**Metres.** Australia's 11.8 Mm/yr comes from roughly 17-18% of world exploration spend: MinEx gives Australia 12% of world spend for 2012-21 and the S&P share has been ~16-17% recently (memory). Scaling up gives 65-100 Mm a year. Australia is unusually metre-intensive (shallow RC/aircore), so I discount that to **about 50-80 Mm/yr worldwide (estimate)**. Canada cross-check: CAD 4.4B of spend, about 50% on drilling, at an all-in contract rate of CAD 200-350/m, gives about 6-11 Mm (estimate).

**Dollars to contractors.** Two methods:
- *Top-down:* USD 15-17B of all-metals exploration times a drilling share of about 45-55% (memory: the common rule of thumb is that drilling is about half an exploration budget; I could not find a cited source) gives **USD 7-9B**.
- *Bottom-up:* known exploration-weighted contractor revenue (table below) is about USD 3.0-3.5B. The listed and PE-owned leaders hold perhaps 40-50% of the non-China market, which implies USD 6-8B before China's domestic state geological teams.
- **Central estimate: about USD 7.5B a year for contracted exploration and resource-definition drilling worldwide (range 6-9B).**
- Of that, the true greenfield discovery-hole slice tracks the grassroots share of budgets (21%), so it is only **about USD 1.5-2B a year (estimate)**. Late stage takes about USD 2.5B and minesite about USD 3.4B.
- Market-research reports put "mining drilling services" at USD 2.4-3.7B (IMARC: https://www.imarcgroup.com/global-mining-drilling-services-market). That understates the market: it is about the sum of the top five contractors.

**Uranium subset (estimate).**
- Canada: CAD 458M → 737M of uranium exploration. If 55-65% goes to drilling (deep diamond holes, often helicopter-supported), Canadian uranium drilling is **CAD 250-480M/yr**, almost all of it in the Athabasca.
- Globally: the OECD-NEA/IAEA Red Book reports uranium exploration *plus development* of more than USD 1.78B over 2023-24, about USD 0.9B a year including development (https://www.ans.org/news/2026-09-15/article-8391/newest-red-book-notes-the-need-for-more-u-investment/). I put global uranium exploration drilling at **USD 0.35-0.6B/yr**.
- Even a 30% cost cut across all uranium drilling saves about USD 100-180M a year industry-wide. That is a feature, not a company.

**Unit economics (estimate).**
- Capital: USD 191k a month per operating rig is about USD 2.3M a year per working rig.
- Major Drilling: CAD 889M divided by about 350-400 working rigs is about CAD 2.2-2.5M a year per working rig.
- So each additional working rig, plus its crews, is a USD 1.7-2.3M a year revenue unit. Running 24/7 needs about 2-3 crews per rig, each a driller plus 1-2 helpers.

**Margins.** Contract drilling earns 10-19% EBITDA and about 10% EBIT through the cycle. That is the profile of a commodity service with pricing power only at cycle peaks. Major Drilling's adjusted gross margin fell from 25.6% to 22.3% even as revenue grew 22%: labour and ramp-up costs move first, and price catches up later.

**Rate cycle.** MDI expects rigs "to be gradually deployed ... at incrementally higher prices" and says "the industry is nearing capacity in terms of labour". Capital had placed all of its 2026 rig orders by October 2025. Foraco's backlog rose 83% y/y. This is the upswing; 2013-16 and 2023-24 were the troughs, with utilisation below 50%.

## 4. Labour, the real constraint
- Major Drilling FY26: "labour is expected to remain our most significant challenge ... With the pool of available experienced drillers drying up, we have increased the number of trainee drillers, which has and will continue to temporarily affect productivity." The worst regions are Canada, the US, Australia and Chile, and MDI runs training centres there. (https://www.majordrilling.com/major-drilling-announces-fourth-quarter-results-and-record-annual-revenue/)
- Northern Miner: juniors are trimming programs because of driller and geologist shortages. One explorer works through "a list of 17 different contractors ... just to find one drill"; majors lock up rigs on long-term contracts. (https://www.northernminer.com/news/skills-shortage-tightens-grip-on-junior-explorers/1003888558/)
- MiHR: Canadian mining needs to fill 190,000-255,000 positions in the next decade (via the same Northern Miner coverage).
- IMDEX put Australian rig utilisation at 79% ("nearing capacity") against 72% in North America (date of that figure uncertain).
- Takeaway: fleet utilisation of 40-75% alongside a labour shortage means rigs are cheap and plentiful while skilled crews are scarce. A technology that removes crew-hours per metre (automation, rod handling, remote operation) attacks the real bottleneck. Master Drilling (Desert Elephant), Boart Longyear and Epiroc are all working on automated or remote rigs (memory for Boart and Epiroc).

Interpretation:
- Discovery value created is about USD 13.5B a year (2012-21 average). That is the same order of magnitude as total exploration spend and about twice total drilling spend.
- Exploration as a whole destroys value (V/C 0.69). Halving drilling cost alone would lift V/C to roughly 0.9-1.0 (estimate, if drilling is about 50% of spend), which is still only break-even. Better *targeting* (fewer holes per discovery) moves the ratio more than cheaper metres do.

## 8. Conclusion: is "cheaper exploration holes" a big enough pool?

**As a service or tool market, no.** It fails rubric test 6.
- The whole contract pool is about USD 7.5B a year. The genuinely "exploratory" (grassroots) slice is about USD 1.5-2B, and uranium about USD 0.4-0.6B.
- The incumbents show where value settles. Major Drilling, the clear leader, does CAD 0.9B of revenue at an 11.6% EBITDA margin. Boart Longyear, once a USD 2B business, sold for USD 371M. Energold went into CCAA. Margins compress whenever the cycle turns, because buyers (juniors) cut drilling first.
- The tooling and data layer earns better margins, but IMDEX shows its share: about 2.4% of exploration spend, or A$0.5B a year after decades. That is the "$100-400M/yr tool" outcome.
- Rubric test 1 also fails: in a drilling-service company, the physical step *is* the product, not something trivial or abstracted.

**Who could still build something large, and how:**
1. **An automated, crew-light drilling contractor (the "labour arbitrage" play).**
   - If one or two people can run a rig that now needs two to three crews, the company can take share in an upcycle when everyone else is labour-capped, and keep 25-30% EBITDA margins instead of 12%.
   - Ceiling: a contractor with USD 1-2B of revenue across exploration, minesite and geotechnical work, worth perhaps USD 2-5B (estimate). That is good but not "very large", and it is hardware-plus-operations heavy.
   - Incumbents are already moving: Master Drilling's robotic rig, and Epiroc and Boart automation (memory).
   - It sits squarely inside Dhruv's 2026-10-03 drilling ban.
2. **An asset owner that uses cheaper or fewer holes as an internal edge (the "capture the step-up" play).**
   - The value pool is the discoveries (about USD 13.5B a year, MinEx) and the land step-up on a hit (from about $1 to about $10,000/ha in the brief). It is not the USD 7.5B paid for metres.
   - A company that drills 2-3x more targets per dollar, through low-cost scout holes, slim-hole or sonic/RC pre-collars, or drilling only where the downhole signal justifies it, and *owns the ground*, captures that step-up (rubric tests 3 and 4).
   - This is a prospect generator or explorer, the same ladder Terranox is on. Selling the capability to others would hand the step-up to the client.
3. **A data company that makes each hole more informative.** Examples: AI core and chip logging, downhole spectral and geophysical sensing, and real-time "stop or deepen" decisions. It fails as a standalone business because IMDEX/Datarock, Boart/Veracio, Corescan/Minalyze/Orexplore already own it and it is capped at a few percent of spend. It only escapes the cap if, like option 2, it is used to pick and own ground.

**Bottom line:** cheaper holes are a cost lever, not a market. The prize is very large only if the company owns what the hole proves. A pure contractor or tool vendor tops out at roughly IMDEX or Major Drilling scale (USD 0.5-1B revenue, sub-USD 5B value). The kind of company that captures the value is a vertically integrated explorer or prospect generator with its own low-cost drilling arm. For uranium specifically, the total pool (about USD 0.5B a year of drilling worldwide) is too small to be the thesis by itself.
